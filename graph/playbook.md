## adversity-and-mortality

### [Adversity](../articles/adversity-and-mortality/adversity.md) — Context

*A team lead learns that the product she spent two years building is being cancelled and her role will end in three months.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This is a disaster and it's unfair. I'll replay every meeting tonight to find who's to blame, and tell everyone I'm totally fine. | This really hurts, and I don't have to pretend otherwise. I can't change the decision, but I can update my CV, talk to two mentors this week and tell my partner how I'm feeling. In a few months I'll look back at what I'd do differently, not just at how to feel better about it. |

**Why:** Separating the loss itself from the stories piled on top of it, acting on what is still controllable and leaning on others protects judgment, and open, exploratory reflection afterwards is the kind linked to wisdom.

Ask yourself:
- Am I adding a "second arrow" of blame, catastrophe or self-pity to the pain this situation actually brings?
- Which parts of this are up to me right now, and which are not?
- When I look back on this, am I trying to understand what happened and what it taught me, or only to tie it up neatly?
- Is the load more than I can carry alone, so that it is time to lean on others or talk to a professional?

### [Ageing](../articles/adversity-and-mortality/ageing.md) — Context

*At 66, Ruth is invited to join a volunteer board that is adopting new budgeting software. Her first thought is that she is "too old to learn this stuff" and should leave it to younger members.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| My memory isn't what it was and computers were never my thing. I'll decline before I embarrass myself. | I'm slower at new software, but I know budgets and this community. I'll say yes, ask for a training session, and pair up with someone who knows the tool. |

**Why:** Treating a stereotype as a fact about yourself narrows your life, and beliefs about ageing are linked, modestly, to later health; choosing where to invest and getting help for the rest keeps both the contribution and the learning.

Ask yourself:
- Am I declining this because of what I can actually do, or because of what I assume people my age can't do?
- With the time and energy I have, which few things deserve most of it, and what can I let go?
- Is there something I still want to learn, or someone younger I could help, this year?
- Am I treating a real health change as "just age", when it might deserve a doctor's attention?

### [Amor fati](../articles/adversity-and-mortality/amor-fati.md) — Principle

*A project manager's carefully planned product launch is cancelled after a reorganisation, and months of her team's work will never ship.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This should never have happened. I keep replaying the meeting where it was killed and resenting everyone in it, and I've stopped caring about the next project. | It happened, and it is now part of my story and the team's. What did these months teach us, and what can we carry into the next thing? I'll grieve it honestly, then decide what to build from it. |

**Why:** The wiser response neither pretends the loss was good nor fights the fact that it occurred; it takes the event as given and asks what a life that includes it can become.

Ask yourself:
- Am I spending my energy wishing this had not happened, or deciding what to make of the fact that it did?
- Am I calling something "fate" to avoid changing what I could and should still change?
- Could I tell the story of this setback as part of a life I would still choose to live again?

### [Failure](../articles/adversity-and-mortality/failure.md) — Context

*A proposal you spent two months on is turned down by the leadership team, and the written feedback lists several weaknesses.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You skim the feedback, decide the committee never understood the idea, tell yourself you are just not good at this kind of work, and avoid opening the document again. | You wait a day, then read the feedback as if it were about a colleague's proposal. You sort the points into what you could have done differently (the cost estimate was thin) and what was never yours to decide (this year's budget priorities). You ask one reviewer what would have changed their mind, fix the estimate and decide whether the idea is worth bringing back next cycle. |

**Why:** The first response protects your self-image by tuning out the information and turning one setback into a verdict on yourself. The second takes the sting out of the feedback so you can actually use it, and keeps what you control apart from what you don't.

Ask yourself:
- Have I actually read and understood the feedback, or have I looked away from it?
- Which parts of this outcome were in my control, and which were not?
- Am I treating myself as I would treat a friend who failed in the same way?
- Is this a mistake to fix, a risk that didn't pay off, or a sign to change course?
- What would I learn if this had happened to someone else?

### [Grief and bereavement](../articles/adversity-and-mortality/grief-and-loss.md) — Context

*Two months after her father's death, Dana enjoys a weekend away with friends, then feels guilty and wonders whether she is grieving "wrong" because she has not hit a depression stage.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'm supposed to be in the sad stage by now. If I can laugh, maybe I didn't love him enough, so I'll cancel the next plan. | Grief doesn't run on a timetable. Missing him and getting on with life can both be part of it, so I'll keep the plan and also set aside Sunday evening to look through his letters. |

**Why:** Research finds no fixed sequence of stages, and moving back and forth between facing the loss and rebuilding daily life is a normal, healthy pattern rather than a sign of loving less.

Ask yourself:
- Am I judging my grief, or someone else's, against a schedule of stages that the evidence does not support?
- Have I made room both for remembering the person and for the practical and social life I still have to live?
- Has intense yearning or numbness kept me from functioning most days for many months, so that it is time to talk to a professional?

### [Memento mori](../articles/adversity-and-mortality/memento-mori.md) — Practice

1. Pick a fixed cue for the reflection, such as waking up, a commute, or the last few minutes before sleep.
2. Bring to mind, concretely and in your own case, that your life will end and that you do not know when.
3. Look back over the last day or week and ask how you actually spent your time and attention.
4. Ask what you would regret leaving undone or unsaid, and whom you would think of first.
5. Choose one concrete action for today that reflects what matters, and let go of one petty grievance or craving.
6. Return your attention to the task in front of you; if the reflection leaves you more anxious than clear, shorten it or stop.

*You have spent a week stewing over a curt email from a colleague and are drafting a sharp reply late in the evening while your family is in the next room.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll send a reply that puts them in their place, then keep going through my inbox until midnight. | If my time were shorter than I assume, this exchange would not be worth another evening. I'll send a short, civil reply tomorrow and spend tonight with the people in the next room. |

**Why:** Remembering that time is limited and uncertain shifts attention from winning a minor status contest to the relationships and values you would actually want to have served.

Ask yourself:
- If my time were shorter than I assume, would this still be worth my anger or effort?
- Am I using the thought of death to clarify what matters, or to feed dread or reckless indulgence?
- What would I want to have said or done for the people involved here?

## compassion-and-relationships

### [Compassion](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/compassion.md) — Virtue

*A reliable team member misses a client deadline and, when you ask about it, mentions that a parent was taken into hospital last week.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I either brush past it ('sorry to hear that, but the client is furious, I need it by tomorrow') or I am so upset for them that I quietly redo the work myself at midnight and never mention the deadline again. | I stop and take it in: 'That's a hard week. What would help most right now?' I move the deadline with the client myself, ask whether they want work taken off them or kept as a distraction, and agree a check-in for next week so the rest of the team isn't left guessing. |

**Why:** Compassion notices the suffering and is moved by it, but it also stays steady enough to choose help that fits this person and stays fair to everyone else involved.

Ask yourself:
- Have I actually noticed what this person is going through, or only what their trouble costs me?
- Am I acting to ease their suffering, or to escape my own discomfort at seeing it?
- Is my concern shaded by a quiet judgement that they brought this on themselves, or that I am above them?
- Would I feel and act the same way if the people suffering were many, distant or unlike me?

### [Compassionate reappraisal](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/compassionate-reappraisal.md) — Practice

1. Sit somewhere quiet and settle your breathing for a minute or two before you start.
2. Recall one specific time a particular person hurt or wronged you, and name plainly what they did and what it cost you.
3. Picture the offender as a whole person with a history, fears and other sides to them, not only as the author of this act.
4. Treat the wrong as a sign that this person needs to grow or change, without telling yourself it was acceptable.
5. Try, for about two minutes, to sincerely wish them something good, such as healing or becoming someone who would not do this again.
6. Notice your thoughts, feelings and body as you do this, and repeat the exercise when you catch yourself replaying the hurt.
7. Decide separately, on the facts, what boundary, conversation or accountability the situation still needs.

*A teammate missed a handover deadline and then told your manager the delay was your fault, and you lie awake replaying it.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I keep going over every word they said to the manager. They're two-faced, that's all there is to it, and I'll be cold to them from now on. | They were wrong to blame me, and I'll correct the record with the manager tomorrow. They were scared of being blamed, which doesn't excuse it, and I hope they learn to own their mistakes. I can wish them that and still watch my back. |

**Why:** Replaying the offence keeps anger and arousal high, while seeing the person as flawed and in need of change lets the anger ease without denying the wrong or giving up the practical response.

Ask yourself:
- Am I wishing this person well, or am I telling myself that what they did was fine?
- Is this wrong still going on and within my power to change, so that action rather than reappraisal is the priority?
- After the exercise, what concrete step, if any, does fairness to me or others still require?
- Am I trying this because I'm ready, or because I feel I ought to have moved on by now?

### [Empathy](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/empathy.md) — Skill

*A colleague tells you, close to tears, that their project has been cancelled after months of work, and you have your own deadline in an hour.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I absorb their upset so fully that I feel sick about it too, then I either pile on about how unfair management is or quietly avoid them for the rest of the week because it is too much. | I take a minute to really listen and say it back: 'Months of work, and it's gone. That's a real loss.' I notice the heaviness in me, remind myself it is their loss, not mine, and ask what would help. Later I check in and offer a concrete hand with the next project. |

**Why:** Feeling with someone opens the door, but staying clear about whose feeling it is and turning it into warm concern and practical help keeps you from either drowning in their distress or backing away from it.

Ask yourself:
- Have I actually tried to understand how this looks from their side, or am I assuming I already know?
- Am I feeling with them, or am I now caught up in my own distress about their distress?
- Is my empathy here going only to the person who is closest, most like me or most vivid, while others affected stay invisible?
- After understanding what they feel, what would actually help them, as opposed to what would relieve my discomfort?

### [Forgiveness](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/forgiveness.md) — Virtue

*A colleague presented your analysis as their own to a client, later admitted it and apologised, and you still have to work together on the account.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll be polite, but I'm keeping score, and I'll make sure the client finds out what they're really like. | I'll tell them plainly that it was wrong and that I'll put my name on my own work from now on. Then I'll stop rehearsing it and treat them fairly on this project. |

**Why:** Forgiving here means dropping the campaign of payback while keeping the judgement that it was wrong and the safeguards that protect you; it is neither pretending nothing happened nor trusting blindly.

Ask yourself:
- Am I holding on to this to protect myself or someone else, or mainly to make them pay?
- Have I separated forgiving from excusing, forgetting and reconciling, so I know which of these I'm actually being asked to do?
- If they keep doing this, what boundary would self-respect require alongside any forgiveness?
- Have I given myself time to name the hurt before deciding whether, and how, to forgive?

### [Generosity](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/generosity.md) — Virtue

*A project lead is two days from her own deadline when a newer colleague asks, for the third time this week, for "a quick hand" with a client report he is struggling to structure.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Either she drops her own work again and rewrites his report herself late into the night, missing her deadline and resenting him, or she snaps that she has no time and leaves him stuck. | She blocks thirty minutes that afternoon, walks him through how she would structure the report, shares a template, and agrees a longer session once her deadline has passed. She keeps her commitment and he leaves able to do the next one himself. |

**Why:** Generosity is giving the right thing, in the right amount, at the right time, without harming yourself or others; unlimited giving that sinks your own commitments and refusing all help are the two failures on either side, and teaching someone often gives more than doing the work for them.

Ask yourself:
- Am I giving this freely, or to be seen as generous, to be owed something or to avoid saying no?
- Is this the right amount and the right moment, given what I can afford and what I have already promised others?
- Does this gift actually help the person, or only make me feel helpful?
- When did I last give my time or attention to someone who could not repay me?

### [Giving advice](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/giving-advice.md) — Context

*A colleague stops by your desk and says she has been offered a promotion to manage her current team, but isn't sure she wants it. You once took a similar step and loved it.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Take it. I did exactly the same thing and it was the best move I ever made. You'll regret it forever if you say no. | Congratulations. Before I say what I think, what's making you hesitate? … It sounds as if the worry is managing two close friends. I'm not you, so take this as one view: when I faced that, what helped was talking to them early. Who else knows this team well that you could ask? |

**Why:** The wiser response listens first, finds out what the real question is, offers a view as one perspective with its reasoning shown, and leaves the decision with the person who has to live with it.

Ask yourself:
- Did the person actually ask for my advice, or am I volunteering it?
- Do I understand their situation and constraints, or am I answering the problem I once had?
- Have I explained my reasoning, so they can judge it rather than just obey it?
- Am I giving this advice for their sake, or because being the one who knows feels good?
- If they decide differently, can I let it go?

### [Golden Rule](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/golden-rule.md) — Principle

*A manager wants to thank a quiet, highly reliable analyst for rescuing a delayed project.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'd love to be praised in front of the whole department, so I'll call her up at the all-hands and give a big speech. | What I'd want is to have my effort noticed and valued. I'll ask her how she likes to be recognised; she prefers a note to her director and first pick of the next project, so that is what she gets. |

**Why:** The manager keeps the rule's core (give the respect and appreciation you'd want) but stops projecting a personal taste onto someone who doesn't share it, which is the most common way the rule misfires.

Ask yourself:
- If our positions were reversed, would I accept being treated the way I'm about to treat this person?
- Am I applying my own tastes, or the underlying respect I'd want, and have I asked what they actually want?
- Would I be comfortable if everyone in my position acted on the reason I'm acting on now?
- Does this relationship's difference in power change what treating them as an equal would require?

### [Interpersonal conflict](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/interpersonal-conflict.md) — Context

*Two siblings are arguing about who should take on more of the care for an ageing parent. One lives nearby; the other earns more and lives two hours away. The latest phone call ended with both hanging up angry.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'm the one doing everything while you just send money. You clearly don't care, and I'm done asking. | I'm exhausted and I said it badly. I don't actually know what this has been like for you from a distance. Can we each list what we can realistically give over the next six months and see what a fair split looks like? This arrangement doesn't have to be permanent. |

**Why:** The wiser response owns its part, admits what it doesn't know about the other person's position, allows that things can change, and looks for an arrangement both can live with instead of a verdict on who is the better child.

Ask yourself:
- Am I sure I know why the other person acted as they did, or am I filling the gap with the least charitable story?
- How would a neutral friend who wanted the best for both of us describe this argument?
- How am I likely to see this disagreement a year from now?
- Is there an outcome that meets the main concern on each side, or am I only trying to win?

### [Mentorship](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/mentorship.md) — Context

*A senior engineer is asked to mentor a new hire who is talented but keeps missing deadlines because she quietly says yes to every request from other teams.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| We'll grab coffee whenever I have a free slot. My advice is simple: do what I did — work late for a couple of years and say yes to everything. That's how you get noticed. | Let's meet every two weeks for the first few months and agree now on what you want from this. Before I tell you what worked for me, walk me through last week's requests — which ones did you feel you couldn't refuse, and why? I'll also introduce you to two peers who handled the same squeeze differently. |

**Why:** The wiser mentor commits to regular contact, sets shared expectations, diagnoses her situation instead of prescribing his own history, and widens her circle of support rather than making himself the only source of guidance.

Ask yourself:
- Have we actually agreed what this mentoring relationship is for and how often we will meet?
- Am I helping this person think through their situation, or retelling my own career as the answer?
- Is this relationship still serving their growth, or mainly my sense of being needed?
- Have I encouraged them to build other sources of support besides me?
- As the mentee, am I asking for honest challenge, or only for reassurance?

### [Perspective-taking](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/perspective-taking.md) — Practice

1. Before you respond, name your own first reading of the situation so you can set it aside for a moment.
2. Imagine the other person's day, constraints and goals, and ask what they know that you don't and what they want from this.
3. Write down two or three guesses about how they see it, and mark each one as a guess.
4. Check the guesses by asking open questions ("How does this look from where you sit?") and listen without rebutting.
5. Revise your reading with what you heard, then decide what to do, looking for an option that serves both of you.

*A team lead notices that a usually reliable colleague has missed two deadlines in a row and seems curt in meetings.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I decide they've stopped caring, picture how I'd feel if I were slacking, and send a sharp message about accountability. | I sketch what their fortnight probably looked like, note that I'm guessing, then ask privately how the project is going from their side. It turns out a second project landed on them without my knowing; we reprioritise together. |

**Why:** Imagining their side softened my reaction, but only asking told me what was actually going on; taking and getting perspective together led to a fair and workable fix.

Ask yourself:
- Am I imagining how they feel, or how I would feel in their place?
- Which of my beliefs about their view have I actually checked with them?
- Have I asked a question I don't already know the answer to?
- Is there an option that meets what they want as well as what I want?

### [Right speech](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/right-speech.md) — Practice

1. Before saying anything that matters, ask first whether it is true as far as you actually know, and drop it if it is not, however pleasing it would be.
2. Ask next whether it is beneficial, meaning it will help the listener or the situation rather than only relieve your feelings or win the point.
3. If it is true and beneficial but unwelcome, do not swallow it; choose a time when the person can hear it, and plan to say it plainly and gently.
4. Check your motive and tone, and speak from goodwill rather than irritation, contempt or a wish to look good.
5. While you are speaking, notice whether the words are causing needless hurt, and stop, slow down or rephrase if they are.
6. Afterwards, review the conversation; if your words did harm, admit it to the person or a trusted friend and resolve to do better, and if they went well, note what helped.
7. Watch for the four habits the tradition names, lying, divisive talebearing, harsh words and idle chatter, especially in casual talk where they are easiest to miss.

*The evening before a colleague presents to senior leadership, they send you their slides and ask, "Is this ready?" The analysis is solid, but the cost figure on slide 6 rests on an assumption you know finance has already rejected.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "Looks great, you'll be fine!" Or, at 11 pm, a long message listing every weakness you can find, ending with a suggestion to rethink the whole thing. | The analysis is strong. One thing to fix before tomorrow: slide 6 uses last quarter's cost assumption, which finance dropped. Want ten minutes in the morning to swap in the new number? Everything else I'd leave as it is. |

**Why:** The first reply is pleasing but untrue and leaves them exposed; the second is true but harsh, badly timed and more about your critique than their success. The wiser reply is true, useful and timed so it can be acted on, and its goodwill is plain.

Ask yourself:
- Is what I am about to say true, as far as I actually know, or is it a guess or a convenient story?
- Will saying it help them or the situation, or does it mainly serve my comfort, my image or my wish to be right?
- Is this the right moment and the right tone for them to be able to hear it?
- Am I holding back something true and useful only because saying it would be awkward for me?
- Did anything I said today divide people, wound someone needlessly or pass on gossip, and is there something I should put right?

### [Tzedakah](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/tzedakah.md) — Practice

1. Decide in advance how much you will give over a month or year, in proportion to your means, and treat it as a commitment rather than a mood.
2. When you see a need, offer help before the person has to ask.
3. Give warmly and say a kind word; if you cannot give, still answer with kindness and never with a rebuke.
4. Protect the recipient's dignity by giving quietly, through a channel that spares them from knowing or thanking you where you can, and don't advertise the gift.
5. Where it fits, give in a form that helps the person stand on their own, such as a loan, work, a partnership or a skill, and ask what they actually need.
6. Stay within your means so that your giving does not make you dependent on others in turn.

*The owner of a small business learns that a long-serving employee has fallen behind on rent after a family emergency and is too embarrassed to ask for help.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| She hands him an envelope of cash in front of the team and later mentions it in a staff meeting as proof that the company looks after its own. He thanks her, avoids eye contact for weeks and starts looking for another job. | She speaks to him privately, offers an interest-free pay advance with a repayment schedule he sets, and asks whether extra paid hours would help, which he takes up. Nobody else is told. |

**Why:** Maimonides places help that makes someone self-supporting at the top of the ladder of giving and treats giving that shames the recipient as a failure; the private advance meets the need, protects his standing at work and leaves him in charge of his own recovery.

Ask yourself:
- Am I giving in proportion to what I have, or only when it is convenient or visible?
- Would the person I am helping feel diminished by how I am giving, and could I give more quietly?
- Is there a way to help that leaves them more able to stand on their own, without deciding for them what they need?
- If I can't give anything right now, did I at least answer kindly?

## emotions-and-equanimity

### [Acceptance](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/acceptance.md) — Skill

*A week before a product launch, a team lead learns that a key engineer is leaving and the launch date cannot move.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I shouldn't be this rattled; I'm the lead. I'll push the worry down, tell everyone it's fine, and keep reopening the decision to let her go in my head all weekend. | I'm anxious and a bit hurt, and that makes sense the week before a launch. The departure is settled. What's still open is the handover and which features we cut, so that's where this afternoon goes. |

**Why:** The wiser response stops spending effort on fighting the feeling and the fact, which leaves attention free for the parts of the situation that can still be shaped; it accepts the departure without giving up on the launch.

Ask yourself:
- Am I fighting a feeling I'm entitled to have, or criticising myself for having it?
- Is this situation truly fixed, or am I calling it unchangeable so I don't have to act?
- Am I accepting what happened, or am I approving of it or giving up?
- Once I've let this be, what is the one step that still matters most?

### [Anger](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/anger.md) — Obstacle

*A colleague forwards your draft proposal to senior management with a note pointing out its weaknesses, without talking to you first. You find out an hour before a deadline.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You fire off a reply-all calling the move underhanded, copy your manager, and spend the afternoon retelling the story to anyone who will listen, sure you know exactly why they did it. | You notice your jaw is tight and you are drafting accusations in your head. You close the email, take a few slow breaths and finish the deadline work. Next morning you ask the colleague privately why they went straight to management, say plainly that you would rather hear concerns first, and fix the two criticisms that were fair. |

**Why:** Anger made you certain about motive and eager to punish; waiting until your body calmed down, and asking before judging, let you defend a fair working norm without escalating, and still learn from the parts of the criticism that were right.

Ask yourself:
- Am I sure what they intended, or is my anger supplying the motive?
- Is this the right person, the right issue, the right moment and the right amount of anger?
- Would I make this decision, send this message or set this penalty if I were calm?
- Am I replaying the offence to understand it, or only to stay angry?
- What do I actually want to change here, and does striking back get me any closer?

### [Cognitive reappraisal](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/cognitive-reappraisal.md) — Practice

1. Name the feeling and the specific thought driving it, for example "I'm anxious because I think this mistake will get me fired."
2. Ask whether you can still change the situation itself; if you can, plan that action first and use reappraisal only to steady yourself enough to act.
3. Test the interpretation against the facts, as you would a colleague's claim, and look for what you actually know versus what you are assuming.
4. Try one or two alternative readings that are also true, such as what the event will matter in a year, what you can still influence, or how a neutral observer would describe it.
5. Pick the reading you can honestly believe, not the most comforting one, and let it guide your next step.
6. After a while, check whether the feeling has shifted; if the reframe isn't landing, switch to another strategy such as acceptance, talking it through or taking a break.

*A project lead learns in a meeting that the client has rejected the proposal their team spent three weeks on.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This is a disaster and it proves I'm not good at this. I'll just smile, say it's fine, and stew about it tonight. | This stings, and it's a real setback. What do I actually know? They rejected this version, not the team. Some of it I can still change: I'll ask for their reasons tomorrow. The part I can't change I'll treat as one lost bid in a long year, not a verdict on us. |

**Why:** The wiser response keeps the feeling honest, uses reappraisal on the part that can't be changed, and keeps the frustration that should drive action on the part that can, instead of masking everything and ruminating later.

Ask yourself:
- Is this something I can still change, so that I should act rather than reinterpret?
- Is my new reading of the situation true, or just comfortable?
- Am I using a reframe to talk myself out of a feeling that is telling me something important, such as that I'm being treated unfairly?
- Did the reframe actually change how I feel, or do I need a different approach?

### [Dichotomy of control](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/dichotomy-of-control.md) — Principle

*A team lead has submitted a strong proposal for next year's budget and now has to wait three weeks for a committee to decide, while rumours suggest cuts.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I keep refreshing my inbox and replaying what the committee might think. If they say no, the whole year was wasted. | The decision isn't mine. What is mine: answering their questions well, briefing my team honestly, and drafting a smaller fallback plan. I'll do those this week and stop checking for news. |

**Why:** Effort moves to the parts of the situation that respond to it, the outcome is still wanted and planned for, and the waiting no longer decides how the week goes.

Ask yourself:
- Which part of this situation actually responds to what I decide or do next?
- Am I treating something I could change as if it were fixed, just to avoid acting?
- Am I staking my peace of mind on an outcome that other people or chance will settle?

### [Emotion regulatory flexibility](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/regulatory-flexibility.md) — Skill

*A manager learns in a one-to-one that her team's project is being cancelled, and has to tell the team in an hour.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Whatever I feel, I keep it off my face. That's how I always handle bad news, at work and at home. | Stay composed while announcing it, since the team needs steadiness; then say plainly that she is disappointed too, ask how they're taking it, and later talk it through with a peer. If the composure starts to feel brittle, switch to rethinking what the cancellation frees the team to do. |

**Why:** Holding back expression was right for the announcement and wrong as a blanket rule; reading the moment, having more than one response, and checking whether it works is what the research links to better adjustment.

Ask yourself:
- Does this moment call for showing what I feel, holding it back, or rethinking the situation?
- Am I reaching for the strategy I always use, or the one that fits here?
- Is the way I'm handling this feeling actually working, and if not, what else could I try?
- Do I have at least two different ways to respond when something like this happens?

### [Emotional suppression](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/emotional-suppression.md) — Obstacle

*In a project review, a senior colleague dismisses your team's proposal in front of everyone. You feel a hot rush of anger and embarrassment.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Keep a blank face, say 'fine', move on, and never mention it to anyone. Feelings have no place at work. | Stay composed in the room, since this isn't the moment, but name it briefly: 'I see it differently; can we go through the data after?' Later, ask what the criticism might be getting right, and talk it over with a trusted peer. |

**Why:** Holding back a display for a few minutes can fit the setting; making a rule of never showing or sharing the feeling leaves it running underneath, blurs what you take in, and keeps others from knowing where you stand.

Ask yourself:
- Am I hiding this feeling because the moment calls for it, or because I always hide feelings like this?
- Has the feeling actually changed, or only my face?
- Is there someone I could tell how this really landed, and have I told them?
- Could I look at the situation differently, rather than just clamping down on my reaction to it?

### [Equanimity](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/equanimity.md) — Virtue

*An hour before you present to leadership, you learn that the client your team spent three months courting has chosen a competitor.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This is a disaster. I'll open with who dropped the ball, and frankly I'm not sure this team can win anything. | That hurts, and I'll let myself feel it. What's in my hands now is an honest account of what we learned and what we'll try differently, so that's what I'll present. |

**Why:** The loss is still felt, but it no longer chooses your words; attention moves from blame and panic to the part of the situation you can actually shape.

Ask yourself:
- Am I reacting to what actually happened, or to the story I am telling about it?
- Is my calm here steadiness, or am I simply refusing to feel or to care?
- If this turned out well, or badly, would I still be able to act on my values?
- What part of this is up to me, and am I putting my energy there?

### [Hedonic adaptation](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/hedonic-adaptation.md) — Obstacle

*Six months after a long-awaited move into a bigger flat with a garden, it no longer feels special, and you find yourself browsing listings for something larger still.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The thrill's gone, so this place obviously wasn't enough. The next one will fix it. | This is what getting used to things feels like, not proof the flat was a mistake. I'll use the garden in new ways, have friends over, and notice what I actually like here before I decide whether I truly need more. |

**Why:** When joy fades, expectations tend to rise; deliberate variety and appreciation slow that drift, and naming it stops a normal fading from being misread as a reason to chase the next upgrade.

Ask yourself:
- Is this dissatisfaction about something actually wrong, or have I simply got used to something good?
- When I last got what I wanted, how long did the lift last, and what did I want next?
- Am I still noticing and varying how I enjoy this, or just owning it?
- Am I assuming I will bounce back from this setback on my own, when it may need real action or support?

### [Negative visualization](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/negative-visualization.md) — Practice

1. Choose one thing you value and tend to take for granted, such as a relationship, your health, your job or an ordinary comfort.
2. Remind yourself plainly of its nature, starting small, for example that this mug can break and that the people you love are mortal.
3. For a minute or two, imagine concretely what your life would be like without it, or how it might never have come about.
4. Return to the present and notice that you do still have it; name one way you will use or appreciate it today.
5. Before a risky plan, list what could go wrong, separate what is up to you from what is not, and prepare for the parts you can influence.
6. Stop if the exercise turns into dread or repetitive worry, and shorten it or set it aside.

*Your manager asks you to present to senior leadership next week, and you also have a job you mostly enjoy but have started to complain about.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll keep replaying how badly the presentation could go every night until then, and keep grumbling about this job. | Once, I'll list what could go wrong in the presentation, prepare for the questions I can foresee, and accept that the reaction isn't fully up to me. And I'll take a minute to picture not having this job at all, then notice what I'd miss. |

**Why:** A brief, bounded rehearsal turns vague fear into preparation and restores appreciation, whereas dwelling on the worst only adds suffering before anything has happened.

Ask yourself:
- Am I rehearsing a possible loss in order to prepare and appreciate, or am I just worrying?
- After imagining this loss, did I come back to what I still have and what I can do now?
- Which parts of the outcome I fear are actually up to me?

### [Process model of emotion regulation](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/process-model-of-emotion-regulation.md) — Framework

1. When a strong feeling starts, name it and ask whether you actually want to change it, and in which direction.
2. Ask whether the situation itself can be avoided, chosen differently or changed; if it can, start there.
3. If the situation can't change, decide where to put your attention, and avoid dwelling on what upsets you.
4. Rethink what the situation means, for example by looking at it as a detached observer would.
5. Treat holding back your expression as a short-term tool for moments that need it, not a default.
6. After a few minutes, check whether the strategy is helping; keep it, switch, or stop.

*An employee opens an email, shortly before a client call, saying that a report they wrote has to be redone over the weekend.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Keep a straight face on the call, then spend the evening replaying the email and resenting it. | On the call, hold back the frustration since it's not the moment. Afterwards, ask the manager whether part of the work can wait until Monday. For the part that can't, look at it as an outsider would (a one-off, not a verdict on my work), then check an hour later whether that's actually settled the feeling or whether I need to talk it through. |

**Why:** The wiser response uses more than one point in the process (changing the situation where possible, reappraising what can't change, holding back only briefly) and checks whether the strategy is working instead of letting suppression slide into rumination.

Ask yourself:
- Can I change this situation, or only how I see it and respond to it?
- Am I reaching for my usual strategy, or the one that fits this moment?
- Am I replaying this or concentrating on it, rather than rethinking it or looking elsewhere?
- Is what I'm doing with this feeling working, or is it time to switch?

### [Rumination](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/rumination.md) — Vice

*In your annual review, your manager says your project updates are often late. That night you cannot stop thinking about it.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "Why do I always do this? What does she really think of me? I'm probably the weakest person on the team." Two hours later you are still replaying the meeting and have decided nothing. | "If a colleague told me this, I'd ask what exactly went wrong and what they'd do next. The last three updates slipped because I waited for complete data. I'll propose a short interim update every Friday and check that works for her." Then you put it down for the night. |

**Why:** Asking what happened and how to act, from a step back, turns the same worry into a plan, whereas asking why in a self-critical loop keeps the hurt fresh and leaves the problem untouched.

Ask yourself:
- Has this round of thinking produced a new insight or a next step, or am I replaying the same scene?
- Am I asking how to deal with this, or only why it happened and what it says about me?
- What would I advise a friend in exactly this position, and can I take that advice tonight?
- Have I tested the fear I keep circling, or only rehearsed it?

### [Stoic reserve clause](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/stoic-reserve-clause.md) — Practice

1. Before you start something that matters, name the outcome you want and commit to doing your part fully.
2. Say or write the proviso out loud, for example "I will launch on Friday, if nothing prevents it", and list two or three things that could prevent it.
3. Separate the parts that depend on your choices (preparation, effort, honesty, how you respond) from those that do not (other people's decisions, timing, luck).
4. When an obstacle does block you, pause and remind yourself that the plan always carried that condition; then ask what the obstacle reveals that you misjudged.
5. Redirect your effort to the next good action still open to you, which may be a revised attempt, a different goal, or the patience, fairness or courage the setback now calls for.

*A team lead has spent a month preparing a client pitch. The day before, the client postpones indefinitely after a change of leadership on their side.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "A month wasted. This always happens to me." She vents to the team, rereads the cancellation email all evening and decides not to bother preparing so carefully next time. | She had told herself from the start that the pitch would happen if nothing on the client's side changed. She is disappointed, but sends a short, warm reply offering to brief the new leadership, asks the team what in the pitch could be reused for two other prospects, and notes that she never asked the client how stable their sponsor was. |

**Why:** The proviso did not make her care less or work less. It meant the setback confirmed a condition she had already accepted, so her attention went to what was still hers to do and what she could learn, not to a story about being cheated.

Ask yourself:
- Have I stated, at least to myself, what could prevent this plan, and am I still willing to give it my full effort?
- Am I treating an outcome that depends on others as if I were owed it?
- Now that I've been blocked, what did I misjudge, and what good action is still open to me?
- Am I using "it was out of my hands" to skip examining the parts that were mine?

### [The view from above](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/view-from-above.md) — Practice

1. Name the specific thing that is gripping you right now, such as a slight, a setback, a rivalry or a wish for praise.
2. Picture yourself rising slowly above the room, the building and the city until you can see whole countries and the many lives going on in them at once.
3. Widen the view in time as well, recalling the people who lived before you and those who will come after, few of whom will ever know of this moment.
4. From that height, look back at your situation and ask how large it really is, and what part of it actually depends on you.
5. Come back down deliberately and choose one concrete, decent action for today, so the wider view ends in conduct rather than detachment.
6. Stop and set the exercise aside if it leaves you feeling that nothing matters, and talk to someone you trust instead.

*A colleague's proposal is chosen over yours in front of the whole department, and you spend the evening composing a sharp reply-all email.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Everyone saw that. I'm sending this tonight so they know the decision was wrong and I won't be pushed aside. | Picture this from far above: one meeting among millions happening today, which few will remember next year. What matters is the project and how I treat people. I'll sleep on it, then ask my manager what would have made my proposal stronger. |

**Why:** Seeing the slight at its real scale lowers the heat of the moment, and coming back down to one useful action keeps the wider view from turning into either revenge or indifference.

Ask yourself:
- Seen from far above and years from now, how big is this, really?
- Am I using the wider view to act better here, or to stop caring about people who are affected?
- Which part of this is about my reputation, and which part is about what is right to do?

## foundations

### [Cleverness](../articles/foundations/cleverness.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- Have I checked whether this goal is worth reaching, or only how to reach it?
- If the people affected could see my plan, would they call it wise or just clever?
- Am I trusting my track record of solving problems to excuse me from asking what this one is for?

### [Folly](../articles/foundations/folly.md) — Vice

*A senior engineer, the most experienced person on the team, wants to push a database change to the live system late on a Friday. A junior colleague asks whether it has been tested against a copy of the real data.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "I've done this kind of change a hundred times. Testing it would take all afternoon, and nothing is going to break." The change goes out unreviewed, fails over the weekend, and the on-call staff spend two days repairing it. | "Fair question. My experience is with the old schema, not this one, so I don't actually know. Let's run it against the staging copy and ship on Monday, when people are around if it goes wrong." The test exposes a locking problem, which is fixed before release. |

**Why:** Expertise in one area tempts people to feel knowledgeable and safe everywhere; asking what this particular action requires, who bears the cost if it fails, and whether a dissenting question has been answered brings confidence back into line with what is actually known.

Ask yourself:
- Do I actually have the specific knowledge or skill this action needs, or am I borrowing confidence from success somewhere else?
- Whose interests, besides mine, does this decision affect, and how will it look in a year rather than this week?
- Am I assuming the consequences won't reach me because of my position, record or cleverness?
- When someone questioned this plan, did I answer their point or just wave it away?

### [Implicit theories of wisdom](../articles/foundations/implicit-theories-of-wisdom.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- When I call someone wise, am I responding to their judgment and care for others, or mainly to their intelligence, confidence or age?
- Whose advice am I about to rely on here, and what picture of wisdom made me choose them?
- Would someone raised with a different idea of wisdom see this choice as wise, and what would they weigh that I am leaving out?

### [Personal and general wisdom](../articles/foundations/personal-and-general-wisdom.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- Am I drawing on what I know about life in general here, or on what I actually know about myself in this situation?
- If a friend described this exact problem to me, what would I tell them, and why am I not following that advice now?
- What does this setback show me about my own strengths and weaknesses that I would rather not look at?
- Am I calling myself wise about this because I give good advice to others, or because I have handled my own version of it well?

### [Practical wisdom](../articles/foundations/practical-wisdom.md) — Virtue

*A team lead's policy is that expense claims filed more than 30 days late are rejected. A reliable new hire files a claim six weeks late, after spending a month away dealing with a family emergency.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Rules are rules. If I make an exception for one person, I'll have to make it for everyone. Rejected. | "The rule exists to keep the books clean and stop sloppy filing, and neither is at stake here. I'll approve it, note the reason on the record, and suggest finance add a hardship clause so the next person isn't left to luck." |

**Why:** Practical wisdom serves the purpose behind a rule rather than its letter, notices what is particular about the case, and still protects fairness by making the reasoning visible and fixing the policy for everyone.

Ask yourself:
- What is the purpose behind the rule or plan I'm about to apply, and does applying it here actually serve that purpose?
- Which particular features of this situation would an experienced, fair-minded person notice that I'm skating over?
- Am I using my cleverness to reach a goal I haven't checked is worth reaching?
- Whose interests, including my own, have I weighed, and whose have I quietly left out?
- Have I asked someone with more experience of situations like this what they would see?

### [Socratic ignorance](../articles/foundations/socratic-ignorance.md) — Principle

*A team lead is asked in a planning meeting whether a new supplier can deliver on time, and has only skimmed their proposal.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Yes, they're solid, I'm sure of it. Let's lock them in. | I've only skimmed it, so I don't actually know. Here's what I'd need to check, and who has worked with them before? |

**Why:** Separating what you know from what you merely feel sure of keeps a confident guess from becoming the team's decision, and invites the people who do know to speak.

Ask yourself:
- Do I actually know this, or am I only confident about it?
- If someone questioned me closely here, where would my account run out?
- Am I treating skill in my own field as if it made me an authority on this question too?

### [Wisdom](../articles/foundations/wisdom.md) — Concept

*A team member with deep technical knowledge is asked whether the team should rebuild a struggling product from scratch or keep repairing it.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I know this codebase better than anyone, so the answer is obviously a rewrite. No need to hear the others. | I know the code well, but not the customers' timelines or the budget. Let me lay out what I'm sure of and what I'm guessing, ask sales and finance what I'm missing, and recommend the option that serves the people who depend on this, not the one I'd enjoy. |

**Why:** Expertise is not yet wisdom; the wiser answer knows the limits of its own knowledge, takes other perspectives in, and aims at the good of everyone affected.

Ask yourself:
- Am I treating how much I know about this as if it were the same as knowing what to do?
- What in this situation am I unsure of, and have I admitted it to myself and to others?
- Would this choice still look good to me if I judged it by the kind of life I want to live, not only by today's goal?

### [Wisdom versus intelligence](../articles/foundations/wisdom-vs-intelligence.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- Am I trusting this plan because the reasoning is clever, or because I have checked it against other people's perspectives and what could go wrong?
- Is this a puzzle with one right answer, or a human problem where interests, values and uncertainty all matter?
- Who, less brilliant than me on paper, might see something about this situation that I am missing?

## judgment-and-decisions

### [Decisions under uncertainty](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/decisions-under-uncertainty.md) — Context

*A team lead is asked to commit to a firm twelve-month sales forecast for a service the company has never sold, in a market nobody on the team knows well.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll build a detailed spreadsheet with one number per month and defend it. Admitting we don't know would look weak. | I'll say plainly what we know and what we don't, give a range with two or three scenarios, start with a small pilot we can scale or stop, and name the signals that would make us revise. |

**Why:** A single precise forecast hides how little is known and invites a big, hard-to-reverse bet; naming the uncertainty and buying information cheaply keeps options open without freezing the team.

Ask yourself:
- Do I actually know the odds here, or am I treating a guess as if it were a measured probability?
- Which of my options still leaves me in a tolerable position if the future turns out quite differently from what I expect?
- What is the cheapest step I could take now that would teach me something before I commit further?

### [Deliberation](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/deliberation.md) — Skill

1. Check that the matter is actually up to you and that its outcome is genuinely uncertain; if not, stop deliberating and either accept it or act on what is known.
2. Say plainly what you are trying to achieve, and ask whether that end is worth pursuing before you plan how to reach it.
3. List at least two or three realistic ways of getting there, not just the first one that occurred to you.
4. For the most promising option, trace backwards from the goal to the very next thing you would have to do, and drop options whose chain hits something impossible.
5. If the decision is important, talk it through with someone who knows this kind of situation.
6. Before trusting a strong gut feeling, ask whether you have had long, well-fed-back experience with situations like this one.
7. Set a point at which deliberation ends, then choose and act promptly, without reopening the question unless new facts appear.

*A manager has to decide within a week whether to move a struggling analyst to a different team or keep coaching her in her current role.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| My gut says she's not cut out for this. I'll announce the move on Monday and be done with it. | Is this really my call, and what am I trying to achieve: her success, or a quieter team? Let me list the options, including a coaching plan with a review date, talk it over with HR and her previous lead, and decide by Thursday. |

**Why:** The wiser response clarifies the goal, widens the options and borrows others' judgment on an important, uncertain matter, but still commits to a decision on time instead of drifting.

Ask yourself:
- Is this decision actually within my power, and is the outcome really uncertain, or am I deliberating to put off accepting something?
- Have I checked that the goal itself is a good one, not just how to reach it?
- Did I consider more than one way forward before settling on the first idea I had?
- Is my confidence here earned by long, well-fed-back experience, or is it just a strong feeling?
- Have I set a point where thinking stops and acting starts?

### [Ethical lenses framework](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/ethical-lenses-framework.md) — Framework

1. Before deciding, ask whether the choice could harm someone, or benefit some people at others' expense, and whether it is about more than legality or efficiency.
2. List the facts you have, the facts you are missing, everyone with a stake, and at least three options, including a creative one.
3. Run each option through the lenses in turn - rights, fairness, overall good and harm, the common good, the person you want to be, and the relationships involved - and note where they disagree.
4. Choose the option that holds up best across the lenses, then imagine explaining it to someone you respect.
5. Act with as much care for those affected as the decision allows, and later review how it turned out and what you would do differently.

*A hiring manager under pressure to fill roles quickly is offered a new screening rule that automatically rejects any applicant with a gap of more than a year in their employment history.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "It's a filter, not a decision about anyone. It saves the team hours, and it's legal, so let's switch it on." | "Who does this screen out? Parents, carers, people who were ill or caring for someone. That's a fairness question, not just an efficiency one. Through the justice and rights lenses it judges people on something unrelated to the job; would I be comfortable explaining it to the candidates? Let's drop the rule and save time with a short structured scorecard instead." |

**Why:** The first response stays inside the technical and legal frame, so the people affected never come into view. The second asks the framework's first question, notices the issue is about more than legality or efficiency, and then lets several lenses and a publicity test shape a better option.

Ask yourself:
- Could this decision damage someone, or benefit some people unevenly, and have I said so in plain words?
- Am I treating this only as a question of legality, cost or efficiency?
- Which lenses favour my preferred option, which count against it, and have I faced the ones that count against it?
- Would I be comfortable explaining this choice to someone whose judgement I respect?
- After acting, did I look back at how it turned out for the people affected?

### [Imaginary future generations](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/imaginary-future-generations.md) — Practice

1. Name the decision and choose a horizon year far enough ahead (often 25–40 years) that most people in the room will no longer be in charge.
2. Ask one person, or a separate group, to imagine they actually live in that year and to speak as its residents, staff or customers, rather than simply forecasting.
3. Have the future side describe what the place is like and what they wish today's decision-makers had done, then list their priorities from that standpoint.
4. Have the present side list its priorities from today's needs, without trying to anticipate the future group.
5. Bring both sides together to negotiate one shared plan, and record where the future side changed it and where it was overruled and why.
6. Rotate the role in later sessions so that everyone has spoken for the future at least once, and end by writing down reasons and advice for your successors.

*A department head is drafting next year's budget and is tempted to cut the apprenticeship programme, whose payoff arrives only after the current team has moved on.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Cut it. Nobody in this meeting will be here when those apprentices are senior, and we need the money for this year's targets. | Before deciding, ask two colleagues to speak as the department's staff in 2045: what will they need from us, and what would they say about this cut? Then weigh their case against this year's pressures, and write down why we chose what we chose. |

**Why:** The first response counts only the people present. The second gives the people who will inherit the decision a voice in the room and leaves a record they can judge us by, without pretending that their interests automatically win.

Ask yourself:
- Who will live with this decision after I have moved on, and has anyone in the room spoken for them?
- If I were a member of that future group, what would I ask today's decision-makers to protect?
- Am I treating my guess about future people's wishes as a certainty, or as one voice to weigh?
- Have I written down my reasons so that my successors can see and judge them?

### [Implementation intentions](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/implementation-intentions.md) — Practice

1. Check that the goal is one you have actually decided on and still endorse; if you are lukewarm about it, deliberate first, because a plan cannot supply commitment you do not have.
2. Identify the critical moment, either the opportunity to act (a time, a place, a recurring event) or the obstacle that usually derails you (a temptation, a distraction, a feeling).
3. Write one plan per cue in the form "If [specific situation], then I will [specific action]", making the cue concrete enough that you will recognise it and the action small enough to start at once.
4. For your usual obstacle, add a shielding plan, such as "If I feel the urge to check messages, then I will note it and keep writing until the timer ends."
5. Say or rehearse the plan a few times so the cue and the response are firmly linked, and remove anything in the setting that competes with it.
6. Afterwards, review whether the cue appeared and the plan fired; revise the plan if circumstances have genuinely changed, not merely because keeping it felt hard.

*A team lead has decided, for good reasons, to stop interrupting colleagues in meetings, but keeps doing it when a discussion gets heated.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "I'll just try harder to listen next week." In the next tense meeting the resolution is forgotten within minutes, and she cuts in twice. | "If I notice I'm about to speak while someone else is mid-sentence, then I'll write my point down and wait for them to finish." In the meeting, the urge to cut in becomes her cue to reach for the notepad. |

**Why:** A general resolution relies on remembering and summoning willpower at exactly the moment emotion is highest. An if-then plan decides in calm advance what the trigger is and what to do, so the situation itself prompts the wiser response.

Ask yourself:
- Have I named the exact moment when I will act, or only the goal I hope to reach?
- What is the cue that usually pulls me off course, and do I have a then-response ready for it?
- Do I still endorse this goal, or am I making plans to avoid admitting I no longer want it?
- If I am about to drop the plan, has something genuinely changed, or has the moment of keeping it simply arrived?

### [Kairos](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/kairos.md) — Concept

*A team lead notices a serious flaw in a project plan. The sponsor is presenting it to senior leaders in ten minutes, and the build does not start for another three weeks.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Either interrupt the presentation to announce the flaw in front of everyone, or say nothing and plan to raise it "once things settle down", which in practice means after the build has started. | Ask the sponsor for twenty minutes later that day, while the plan can still change cheaply, bring the evidence, and suggest how to fix it. |

**Why:** The point is neither "speak up now" nor "wait": it is to find the window in which the concern can be heard and acted on, before it becomes either an ambush or a post-mortem.

Ask yourself:
- Is this the moment when acting will help, or only the moment when I feel the urge to act?
- If I wait, what window closes, and if I act now, what will I not yet know?
- Would this same word land better at a different time, place or with fewer people present?

### [Kill criteria](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/kill-criteria.md) — Practice

1. When you commit to a goal or project, write down two to four signals that would show it is failing, each paired with a date by which you will check it.
2. Make each signal concrete and, where you can, numeric, such as a minimum level of sales, sign-ups, savings or progress, so that someone else could tell whether it has been met.
3. Set a budget for the effort as well as the money, counting hours, weekends and favours, because time spent tends to escape the books.
4. Share the criteria with someone who had no stake in starting the project and ask them to hold you to them, or to make the stop-or-go call themselves.
5. On each review date, compare results with the criteria and ask whether, starting from scratch today, you would begin this.
6. When a criterion is tripped, treat stopping as the default; continue only if you can write down new information that justifies it, not the cost already sunk.
7. When you stop, record what you learned, give credit for the progress made, and name the next goal the freed time and money will go to.

*A café owner started a weekday lunch-delivery service three months ago, buying a used van and training two drivers. Orders are well below what she hoped and the service loses money every week.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| We've bought the van and trained the drivers, so stopping now would waste all that. Let's push on until the summer and see if word of mouth picks up. | When we launched I wrote that we'd stop if we weren't at forty orders a day by the end of month three. We're at eighteen. I'll end deliveries next week, sell the van, and put the two most-ordered dishes on the counter menu. |

**Why:** The first response weighs what has been spent; the second applies a test set before any results came in, when judgment was not yet coloured by investment and pride, and pairs the exit with a next step.

Ask yourself:
- Before I started, did I write down what result, by what date, would tell me to stop?
- Is each criterion concrete enough that someone else could tell whether it has been met?
- Now that a criterion has been tripped, is my reason for carrying on new information, or what I have already put in?
- Am I counting my time and effort against the budget, not only the money?
- Who without a stake in the original decision could check this call?

### [Knowing when to quit](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/knowing-when-to-quit.md) — Skill

*A team lead has spent eighteen months on an internal software tool that users keep ignoring; a cheaper commercial product now does the same job, and the next budget review is in a month.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| We've put too much into this to stop now. One more release and people will come round, so I'll ask for another two developers. | If we were starting today, would we fund this? No. I'll show the review the adoption numbers and the alternative, propose winding it down by the quarter's end, and move the team onto the reporting backlog everyone keeps asking for. |

**Why:** The wiser response judges the project on what it will deliver from here rather than on what it has already cost, which is the pull behind escalation, and it pairs stopping with a clear next goal, which is where the well-being benefits of letting go appear.

Ask yourself:
- If I were deciding today from scratch, with no time or money already spent, would I choose to start this?
- Am I persisting because the goal is still reachable and worth it, or because quitting would mean admitting I was wrong?
- What new goal will this time and energy go to if I let this one go?
- Am I giving up because this is unattainable, or only because the current stretch is hard?

### [Major life decisions](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/major-life-decision.md) — Context

*After eight years in a stable accounting job, Priya has an offer to retrain as a teacher at lower pay, and has been going back and forth for months.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll wait until I'm sure I'll be happier as a teacher. Until then, staying put is the safe choice. | I can't know now what teaching will feel like, and staying is a choice too. I'll test what I can (shadow a class, ask two teachers what surprised them), decide what I'd want to have tried, and set a date. |

**Why:** Waiting for a certainty that can't arrive quietly chooses the status quo, which the evidence suggests people over-favour; gathering what is knowable and naming the real reasons is the wiser path.

Ask yourself:
- Am I treating "not deciding" as if it were not itself a choice?
- Am I assuming the person I'll be in ten years wants exactly what I want today?
- Which part of this can I learn about before committing, and which part can I only learn by living it?

### [Moral algebra](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/moral-algebra.md) — Practice

1. Use the method only for an important choice that is up to you and genuinely two-sided, not for a matter of taste or a change you have already committed to.
2. Frame one specific option (for example, "accept the transfer") and divide a page into two columns headed Pro and Con.
3. Over three or four days, add a short note to either column whenever a reason occurs to you, instead of trying to list everything in one sitting.
4. Bring all the reasons into view and give each a rough weight, such as 1 to 3.
5. Strike out pairs of reasons of equal weight on opposite sides, and let one strong reason cancel two or three weaker ones that together equal it.
6. Ask whether any single remaining reason is decisive on its own, or whether a reason you never wrote down is the one actually driving you.
7. Wait a day or two; if no important new reason appears, decide by the balance and act on it.

*A project manager is offered a two-year posting to the company's office in another city and has to reply within a week.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| It sounds exciting and I hate saying no to leadership. I'll accept in tomorrow's one-to-one and sort out the details later. | I'll keep a Pro and Con page on my phone for the next four days, weigh the reasons on Friday, and see what's left after cancelling the equal ones. If the only big thing on the Con side is my partner's job, that's a conversation to have before I answer, not a line to cancel out. |

**Why:** Gathering reasons over several days stops the mood of one meeting from deciding, weighing rather than counting keeps a single heavy reason from being outvoted by trivia, and the final check surfaces the reason that really matters.

Ask yourself:
- Did I collect reasons over a few days, or only those that occurred to me in one mood?
- Did I weigh the reasons, or just count which column was longer?
- Is there one reason so important that it should settle this on its own, whatever the rest of the list says?
- Am I using the list to choose, or to talk myself out of something I had already decided to do?
- Now that the balance is clear, have I set a time to act on it?

### [Reversible and irreversible decisions](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/reversible-and-irreversible-decisions.md) — Principle

*A team lead has spent two weeks polling everyone about which day the weekly meeting should move to, while the same week she signs off a three-year contract with a new supplier after a single sales call.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Everyone has to be happy with the meeting day, so I'll keep consulting. The supplier deal is just paperwork; I'll sign it so it's off my desk. | The meeting day can change next month if it doesn't work, so I'll pick one today and review it in four weeks. The contract locks us in for three years, so it gets references, a trial period and a second opinion before I sign. |

**Why:** Effort was going to the choice that was cheap to undo and not to the one that was costly to undo; sorting decisions by reversibility puts care where a mistake would last.

Ask yourself:
- If this turns out to be wrong, what would it actually cost, in money, time, trust or relationships, to undo it?
- Am I agonising over something I could simply try and revise, or rushing something I will have to live with?
- Is "I'll keep my options open" helping me learn, or just keeping me from settling into a choice I have already made?
- What part of this choice could I make reversible (a trial, a pilot, a review date) before I commit to the rest?

### [Satisficing](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/satisficing.md) — Practice

1. Before you look at any options, write down the few things the choice must achieve and the minimum each must reach; this is your "good enough" line.
2. Decide in advance how much search the choice deserves, such as a number of options, a deadline or a budget of hours, scaled to how much is at stake and how reversible it is.
3. Look at options one at a time and take the first that clears every line, rather than collecting the whole field to rank.
4. If nothing clears the line within your search budget, lower one standard deliberately; if many options clear it at once, raise one, and say which and why.
5. Once you have chosen, stop comparing; do not keep checking the options you passed up or what others chose.
6. Keep maximizing-style search for the rare choices where a better option is worth the extra effort and the decision is hard to undo.

*A team lead has to replace the team's project-tracking tool within a month, and dozens of products are available.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll trial every serious tool on the market, build a forty-row comparison sheet and keep reading reviews until I'm sure we have the best one. | We need four things: our existing workflows, a single sign-on, an export option and a price under our budget. I'll trial tools from the shortlist IT already approved, adopt the first one that meets all four, and stop shopping once it's in. |

**Why:** Fixing the standard before searching turns an endless comparison into a bounded task, and stopping after the choice spares the team the second-guessing that research links to looking for the best.

Ask yourself:
- Did I decide what "good enough" means before I started comparing, or am I letting each new option move the target?
- Is this choice important and hard to undo enough to justify searching for the best, or would a good option serve just as well?
- Have I set a limit on how many options or how much time I will spend?
- Now that I have chosen, am I still checking what I gave up or what others picked?

### [Seeking counsel](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/seeking-counsel.md) — Practice

1. Name the decision and decide whether it is important and uncertain enough to deserve outside counsel.
2. Write down your own current view and how confident you are, before you ask anyone.
3. Choose two or three advisers who know this kind of situation, including at least one who has lived through it and one likely to disagree with you.
4. Ask each one separately and personally, describe the problem without revealing your preferred answer, and ask what they would do and why.
5. Ask anyone who has already been through the same choice how it actually turned out and felt for them.
6. Compare their answers with your own and move your view meaningfully toward the advice where they know more or agree with each other, noting where and why you still differ.
7. Make the decision yourself, take responsibility for it, and later tell your advisers what you decided and how it went.

*A team lead has a job offer from a fast-growing start-up and must answer by Friday. They are excited and have already half-decided to accept.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll ask my best friend, who always backs me, mention the salary first, and take the yes as confirmation. | I'll write down my own answer first, then ask a former colleague who joined a start-up two years ago how it really went, and a mentor who tends to be cautious. I won't tell them which way I'm leaning, and I'll actually shift my view if they both raise the same worry. |

**Why:** Asking advisers who know the terrain, without steering them, gives the decision a second independent view and the lived experience of someone who has already been there, which people tend to undervalue.

Ask yourself:
- Did I ask people who know this kind of decision, or only people who would agree with me?
- Did I ask without signalling the answer I wanted?
- When the advice differed from my view, did I actually move, or did I explain it away?
- Have I asked anyone who has already lived through this choice how it felt afterwards?

## meaning-and-the-good-life

### [Awe](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/awe.md) — Phenomenon

*After a bruising quarter in which your project slipped, you are on a weekend hike and reach a ridge with a view across a whole valley.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Glance, take a photo, and spend the descent rehearsing the Monday meeting and who is to blame for the delays. | Stop for five minutes, take in how far the valley runs and how long it took to form, and let the project shrink to its real size; on the way down, ask what you would advise a colleague who had this quarter. |

**Why:** Letting a vast scene register can loosen self-focus and the grip of one problem, which leaves room to see the situation and the other people in it more fairly; the effect is modest, so it helps most when you then reflect on purpose.

Ask yourself:
- When did I last let something vast or excellent stop me long enough to change how I saw my own concerns?
- Am I treating awe as a technique to fix a mood, or simply making room to notice what is larger than me?
- After a moment of awe, did I come away more open to other views, or just more certain of my own?

### [Classification of desires](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/classification-of-desires.md) — Principle

*After a good year, a project manager starts eyeing a bigger house, a premium car and a more senior title, and finds herself working weekends and sleeping badly to get all three.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Everyone at my level has these things. Once I have them I'll finally be able to relax. | Which of these would actually hurt to go without? We need more space for the kids, so that's real. The car and the title are mostly about how I look next to others, and I can drop them if they cost my evenings. |

**Why:** Sorting the wants separates a real need from desires driven by comparison, so effort goes where it buys security and the rest stops dictating her time.

Ask yourself:
- Would going without this actually cause me pain, or would I just feel I had fallen behind someone?
- Does this desire have a natural stopping point, or would more always seem better?
- What am I already giving up, in time, health or peace, to chase it?

### [Contentment](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/contentment.md) — Virtue

*A colleague in a similar role announces a promotion and a bigger title, and your own job, which you liked yesterday, suddenly feels small.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I need to start interviewing elsewhere this week; I can't be the one who got left behind. | Is anything about my work actually worse than it was yesterday? If there's something I really want, like the scope or the pay, I'll go after that on purpose. If not, I'll let the comparison pass. |

**Why:** Contentment separates a real, specific need from wanting that was switched on by comparison; it keeps ambition but stops it from setting the price of one's peace.

Ask yourself:
- If nothing changed tomorrow, would my life be enough, and what exactly would be missing?
- Is this want mine, or did a comparison or an advert just switch it on?
- Have I confused being content with giving up on something that genuinely needs fixing?
- When I last got what I wanted, how long did the satisfaction actually last?

### [Gratitude](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/gratitude.md) — Virtue

*A teammate quietly stayed late to fix a broken data feed so your quarterly report could go out on time.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Good, it's fixed. That's their job anyway; on to the next thing. | I'll tell them specifically what their evening saved, mention it to our manager, and keep an eye out for a chance to help them in turn. |

**Why:** Gratitude notices both the benefit and the goodwill behind it, then answers with thanks and goodwill rather than taking it for granted or treating it as a debt to settle and forget.

Ask yourself:
- Have I noticed who made this good thing possible, and the effort or intention it took them?
- Have I actually told them, specifically, rather than only feeling it?
- Is my thanks free, or am I acting out of a sense of debt, guilt or deference I don't really owe?
- Am I using "be grateful" to talk myself, or someone else, out of a legitimate complaint?

### [Hedonic and eudaimonic well-being](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/hedonic-and-eudaimonic-well-being.md) — Framework

*After a draining quarter, a team lead plans a free weekend and wonders how to spend it to feel restored.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Pleasure is shallow; I'll spend the weekend on a self-improvement course so the time counts for something. | I'll do one thing purely for enjoyment, like a long lunch with friends, and one thing I care about, like finishing the bookshelf I'm building with my kid, and see how each leaves me feeling on Monday and a month from now. |

**Why:** Research suggests pleasure-seeking and meaning-seeking pay off on different timescales and work best together, so treating one as the "real" happiness tends to shortchange the other.

Ask yourself:
- Am I treating enjoyment as frivolous, or meaning as a duty, rather than letting each do its own work in my life?
- When I judge whether a choice will make me better off, am I thinking only about how it will feel this week, or also about whether it fits what I value and want to grow into?
- Am I relying on a neat label (pleasure versus meaning) where my actual experience is more mixed?

### [Logotherapy](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/logotherapy.md) — Framework

1. Name one thing you are creating or contributing right now, however small, and who it serves.
2. Name one experience or person you value that you could give more attention to this week.
3. For a hardship you cannot change, decide what stance toward it you want to be able to look back on.
4. Turn your attention from monitoring yourself to the task or person in front of you.

*A team's long-running project is cancelled after a reorganisation, and a project lead feels the past year was wasted.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| None of it mattered. I'll keep my head down and stop caring about the work. | The project is gone, but the people I trained and what we learned stay. What do I want to build next, and how do I want to carry this? |

**Why:** Looking for meaning in contribution, relationships and chosen attitude, rather than only in outcomes you control, keeps a setback from emptying out the whole effort.

Ask yourself:
- Am I asking what I want from life, or what this situation is asking of me?
- Is there anything left for me to create, experience or stand for here, even if the outcome is out of my hands?
- Am I watching myself so closely that it is getting in the way of what I'm trying to do?

### [Meaning in life](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/meaning-in-life.md) — Concept

*After a reorganisation, an analyst's work is folded into another team and she starts to feel that her job, and her weeks, no longer add up to anything.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| None of this matters, so I'll stop caring, coast, and wait until I feel inspired to find my calling. | Which part is missing: does the work no longer make sense to me, have I lost direction, or do I doubt it is worth doing? Let me fix the piece I can, starting with who actually relies on what I do. |

**Why:** Splitting meaning into coherence, purpose and significance turns a vague sense of emptiness into a specific, workable question, and meaning tends to follow connection and contribution rather than waiting for a revelation.

Ask yourself:
- Is what feels missing right now coherence, direction, or a sense that this is worth doing?
- Am I treating a bad week or a low mood as a verdict on my whole life?
- Would I still judge this pursuit worthwhile if I stopped feeling drawn to it, and would I still care about it if no one else valued it?

### [Savoring](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/savoring.md) — Practice

1. Pick one ordinary pleasure in the coming day, such as a meal, a walk, a conversation or a finished task, and decide in advance to give it your full attention.
2. While it is happening, slow down and sharpen your senses; notice one or two specific things that make it good and name to yourself why they appeal to you.
3. Mark the moment so it lasts, for example by telling someone about it, smiling or saying it aloud, or taking a mental snapshot to recall later.
4. Remind yourself that the moment is passing and will not come again in quite this form, and let that make it more precious rather than sad.
5. Later that day or week, spend a few minutes reminiscing about it, or look forward to something good that is coming.
6. Notice when you are dampening a good moment, by finding fault, worrying about what comes next or telling yourself you don't deserve it, and gently return your attention to what is good.

*After months of work, your team ships a project on time and the client sends a warm thank-you note, but you have three new deadlines waiting.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Nice, but that's done. Let's not get complacent; the next quarter looks rough. Archive the email and open the next task. | Before I move on, I'll read this note again, forward it to the team with what each person made possible, and take ten minutes at lunch to enjoy that we pulled it off. The deadlines will still be there at two. |

**Why:** Sharing the success and pausing on it keeps a real achievement from vanishing unnoticed, while the kill-joy reflex of jumping to the next worry spends the good moment before it is felt.

Ask yourself:
- What good thing happened today that I barely noticed, and can I return to it for a minute now?
- Am I talking myself out of enjoying this, by finding fault or rushing to the next worry?
- Who could I share this moment with, so that it lasts longer for both of us?
- Am I savoring to appreciate my life, or using pleasant moments to avoid something I need to face?

### [Self-transcendence](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/self-transcendence.md) — Concept

*A product manager learns that a feature she championed for a year is being cut, and she has to tell the team that built it.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This makes me look bad. I'll keep the announcement short, defend my original call, and move on before anyone asks questions. | This is about the people who poured a year into it and the customers we were trying to help. I'll name what the team achieved, explain what we learned for the next product, and ask what they need from me. |

**Why:** Shifting attention from protecting her image to the team, the users and what comes next turns a bruising moment into one she can handle with generosity and a wider view.

Ask yourself:
- Is my attention right now mostly on how this reflects on me, or on the people and purposes involved?
- Who beyond me, including people not yet here, will live with the consequences of what I decide?
- Am I stepping back to see the bigger picture, or withdrawing because I feel numb or cut off?

### [Simplicity](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/simplicity.md) — Practice

1. Write down the three to five things you most want more of in your life, such as time with family, health, a craft or community.
2. List where your money, hours and attention actually went last month, and mark each item as serving one of those priorities or not.
3. Choose one item that serves none of them and cut or shrink it for a trial month, rather than overhauling everything at once.
4. Redirect what you free up, whether hours or money, deliberately into one of your priorities, preferably something shared with other people.
5. At the end of the month, ask whether you miss what you dropped; keep the change, reverse it, or try the next item.

*A team lead gets a raise and, within weeks, has a larger car loan, three new subscriptions and a habit of browsing for upgrades at lunch, while saying no to a weekly community football game because money now feels tight.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I've earned this. Once the new setup is sorted, I'll have time for the things I actually enjoy. | What did I want the raise for? More breathing room and time with people. The subscriptions and the upgrades aren't buying that, so I'll cancel two, keep the old car another year, and say yes to football. |

**Why:** Simplicity here is not austerity but alignment; cutting spending that serves no priority frees money and attention for the social and personal goods that the evidence links to well-being.

Ask yourself:
- What is this purchase or commitment for, and does it serve something I said I value most?
- If I removed it for a month, would I genuinely miss it or just feel I had fallen behind others?
- Am I simplifying to free time and attention, or just replacing one kind of acquiring and comparing with another?
- Who or what will get the time or money I free up?

## science-of-wisdom

### [Balance theory of wisdom](../articles/science-of-wisdom/balance-theory-of-wisdom.md) — Framework

1. Name the decision and the outcome you are tempted to choose before you start weighing it.
2. List the interests at stake on three levels - yours, those of the specific people around you, and those of the wider team, community or environment.
3. For each option, write down who gains and who pays in the next few weeks and in a few years.
4. Ask whether to adapt to the situation, try to change it, or leave it for a better one, and why.
5. Choose the option that best serves a common good you could defend out loud to everyone affected, and say which value settled the trade-off.

*A team lead is offered a high-profile project that would mean handing a struggling junior colleague's half-finished work to someone else three days before the client deadline.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This is my shot. Take the project, reassign the work tonight and let the junior person sort themselves out. | I want this project, the junior colleague needs support, and the client needs the deliverable. Can I ask for a one-week start date, pair them with a senior for three days, and take the project after? |

**Why:** The first answer serves only one interest in the short term; the second looks for an option that balances personal, interpersonal and wider interests over a longer horizon, which is what Sternberg means by a wise solution.

Ask yourself:
- Whose interests have I left off the list, including people who are not in the room?
- Would this choice still look good to me a year from now, not just this week?
- Am I adapting, trying to change things, or walking away, and is that the right response here?
- Is the common good I am appealing to one the other people affected would recognise, or just my own preference dressed up?

### [Berlin wisdom paradigm](../articles/science-of-wisdom/berlin-wisdom-paradigm.md) — Framework

Ask yourself:
- Have I considered how this problem looks across the person's whole life, past and future, and not only today?
- Have I named what I do not know about this situation, and what I would do if things turn out differently than I expect?
- Am I judging this choice by my own values only, or have I tried to understand the values and priorities behind it?

### [Common wisdom model](../articles/science-of-wisdom/common-wisdom-model.md) — Framework

Ask yourself:
- What do I not know about this situation, and how might the facts change what I think?
- Whose perspective have I not yet considered, and what would this look like from where they stand?
- Is there a way forward that serves both my interests and theirs, rather than one at the other's expense?
- Am I aiming at what is true and fair here, or only at winning the point?

### [Solomon's paradox](../articles/science-of-wisdom/solomons-paradox.md) — Obstacle

*You recently helped a teammate think calmly through a clash with another department. This week a peer quietly reassigns part of your own project without asking you.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This is a power grab, plain and simple. I know exactly what they're up to, and I'm escalating to our director today. | If my teammate described this to me, I'd ask what they actually know about the peer's reasons, what a fair split could look like, and how it might look in a month. I'll ask the peer for ten minutes first. |

**Why:** Treating your own conflict as you would a colleague's brings back the habits you already use as an adviser, namely admitting what you don't know, looking for compromise and expecting the situation to change.

Ask yourself:
- If a friend brought me this exact problem, what would I tell them, and am I following that advice myself?
- Could I state the other person's view in words they would accept as fair?
- Have I admitted what I don't know about their motives and about how this could unfold?
- Am I treating any compromise as a defeat?

### [Teaching for wisdom](../articles/science-of-wisdom/teaching-for-wisdom.md) — Practice

1. Pick a real dilemma from material you already teach (a historical episode, a case, a customer complaint) in which people's interests genuinely conflict.
2. Have learners list whose interests are at stake (their own, other people's, the wider community's) and what each option does to them in the short and the long run.
3. Ask each learner to argue the strongest case for a view they reject, from inside that person's position, then to look for a resolution that takes both sides into account.
4. Ask them to name the values behind their choice and to judge whether the means are acceptable, not only the result.
5. Lay out the three moves open to them (adapt to the situation, change it, or leave it) and have them say which fits best and why.
6. Model the habits yourself by saying what you don't know, changing your mind in front of them when given a good reason, and sharing credit.
7. Grade or praise answers for how well they balance interests and perspectives, then close by asking where a similar choice shows up in learners' own lives.

*A manager is training new shift supervisors. The case for the day: two staff members both asked for the same holiday weekend off, and only one can be released.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The manager explains the leave policy (first to ask gets priority), confirms that the trainees can recite it, and moves on to the next slide. | The manager has the trainees list everyone affected (both employees, the rest of the team, customers), what each option means this month and next year, and the best case for the person who would lose out. They discuss whether the rule should simply be applied, bent or changed, and the manager describes a time they handled a similar request badly and what they learned. |

**Why:** Knowing the rule is not the same as judging well when it collides with people's real interests; practising that judgment on the job's actual content builds the habit the trainees will need on the floor.

Ask yourself:
- When I explained this to someone, did I only hand over the right answer, or did I ask whose interests are at stake and over what time span?
- Did I let the other person argue a view I disagree with, from that side, before I judged it?
- Did I show that I can be wrong, or did I present myself as the one who always knows?
- Which did I reward, the quick and clever answer or the balanced one?

### [Three-dimensional model of wisdom](../articles/science-of-wisdom/three-dimensional-wisdom-model.md) — Framework

Ask yourself:
- Am I trying to understand what is really going on here, including what I cannot know, or only looking for facts that confirm my first reaction?
- Have I looked at this situation, and my own part in it, from at least one other person's point of view?
- Is my response moved by concern for the people affected, or mainly by protecting my ego?

### [Wise reasoning](../articles/science-of-wisdom/wise-reasoning.md) — Skill

*Your team lead announces that a project you have run for a year will be merged into another group's work. You learn about it in the same meeting as everyone else.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| They have clearly sidelined me on purpose. I'll tell the team the plan is a mistake and refuse to hand anything over. | I don't yet know why this was decided or who pushed for it. I'll ask the lead what problem the merger solves, say plainly what I think would be lost, and propose a handover that keeps the parts that work. This may look different in three months. |

**Why:** The wiser response admits what it does not know, takes the other side's reasons seriously, allows that events can still turn several ways, and looks for an arrangement both sides can live with, which are the four elements researchers score.

Ask yourself:
- What don't I know about this situation, and how sure am I really about the other person's motives?
- Could I describe the other side's view in words they would accept as fair?
- How might this look in a month or a year, and what else could happen besides the outcome I fear?
- Is there an arrangement that meets the main concerns on both sides, or am I treating any middle ground as losing?
- Would I reason about this differently if a friend had brought it to me?

## self-knowledge

### [Cognitive defusion](../articles/self-knowledge/cognitive-defusion.md) — Practice

1. Notice when a thought is steering you, especially a harsh verdict about yourself or a prediction of disaster that you are treating as plain fact.
2. Name it as a thought by restating it as "I'm having the thought that…", or label its kind, such as "the not-good-enough story" or "a prediction".
3. Loosen its literal hold for a few seconds by saying its key word aloud quickly and repeatedly, or saying the sentence very slowly or in a silly voice, until it sounds more like sounds than a message.
4. Let it stay without arguing with it, proving it wrong or pushing it away; acknowledge it, for example by thanking your mind for the warning.
5. Ask what you would do next if this thought were just background noise, and take that step while the thought comes along.
6. Stop and seek professional support if a thought is overwhelming, persistent or about harming yourself; defusion is a self-help skill, not crisis care.

*Ten minutes before presenting a proposal to senior leaders, a manager keeps hearing the thought "they're going to see I'm out of my depth".*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| It's true, I'm out of my depth. I'll trim the proposal to the safe parts, skip the risky recommendation and read straight from the slides so nobody can ask me anything. | I'm having the thought that they'll see I'm out of my depth. That's the old not-good-enough story turning up before a big meeting, as it usually does. It can come along; I'll still make the recommendation I believe in and take the questions. |

**Why:** The wiser response neither obeys the thought nor wastes the last ten minutes trying to argue it away; seeing it as a familiar mental event leaves the manager free to act on the judgement they actually hold.

Ask yourself:
- Am I treating this thought as a fact about the world, or as something my mind is saying right now?
- Is this thought helping me act on what matters here, or just making me want to escape?
- Am I using a defusion trick to make the feeling go away, rather than to make room for it?
- Have I checked whether the thought is pointing to a real problem that deserves action?

### [Evening review](../articles/self-knowledge/evening-review.md) — Practice

1. Pick a fixed, short slot at the end of the day, such as the last ten minutes before bed, and keep it whether or not the day went well.
2. Go through the day in order, recalling what you said and did in the main conversations, decisions and tasks, as if replaying it for a fair observer.
3. Ask three questions of each moment that stands out - what did I do well, where did I fall short of my own standards, and what did I leave undone?
4. For each shortfall, name the specific thing you would say or do next time, then let it go as Seneca did, with a pardon rather than a sentence.
5. Write down one good thing that happened and why it happened, so the review does not become a list of faults.
6. Choose a single intention for tomorrow and, every week or so, look back over your notes for patterns that keep recurring.

*During an afternoon planning meeting you cut off a junior colleague who was raising a scheduling concern, and later in the day the concern turned out to be right.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You lie awake replaying the moment, call yourself a terrible manager, check your phone to escape the feeling, and the next morning act as if nothing happened. | In a ten-minute review before bed you note that the meeting was otherwise well run, that you interrupted because you were defending a deadline you had promised, and that the concern was valid. You decide to thank the colleague tomorrow and to ask for objections before closing future planning meetings, then put the notebook away. |

**Why:** A fair, bounded review turns a sting into a specific repair and a better habit, whereas unstructured replay punishes you without changing what happens next.

Ask yourself:
- Am I reviewing today's conduct to improve it tomorrow, or to punish myself for it?
- Have I named what went well today, and why, as well as where I fell short?
- Is there one concrete thing I will say or do differently tomorrow as a result of this review?
- Am I judging myself by my own considered standards, or by one bad moment?

### [Know thyself](../articles/self-knowledge/know-thyself.md) — Principle

*After a project review, a manager is told by two team members that her meetings run long because she rarely lets others finish a point.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| That's not me. I know how I come across, and I'm a good listener. | I don't experience myself that way, but two people saw the same thing. I'll ask a colleague to watch the next meeting and tell me what they notice. |

**Why:** Self-views are least reliable on flattering or unflattering traits, so treating others' observations as evidence rather than an attack is how the maxim is actually followed.

Ask yourself:
- What would someone who knows me well say about me here that I would rather not hear?
- Am I judging myself by my intentions while others can only see my behaviour?
- Have I mistaken a confident story about my motives for real knowledge of them?

### [Learning from experience](../articles/self-knowledge/learning-from-experience.md) — Context

*In the space of a year, three people on a small team you manage have resigned, each giving a different reason on the way out.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You decide it was bad luck and a hot job market, tell yourself the team is stronger for the turnover, and move straight on to hiring replacements. | You lay the three departures side by side, note what you did in the months before each one and what followed, ask one former colleague who left on good terms what they saw, and notice that all three had asked for a change of scope that you postponed. You commit to discussing growth plans with every remaining report this quarter. |

**Why:** The first response is a redemptive story that brings closure but no lesson; the second connects your own actions with their consequences and explores the pattern, which is the kind of processing linked to wisdom.

Ask yourself:
- Can I say what I did, what followed, and how I think the two are connected?
- Am I examining this to understand it, or only to feel better about it and close the chapter?
- Have I looked at what went well and why, not only at what went wrong?
- Whose view of this experience would challenge my own version of it?

### [Life review](../articles/self-knowledge/life-review.md) — Practice

1. Divide your life into chapters - by place, decade, role or relationship - and give each chapter a short title.
2. For each chapter, recall concrete scenes in as much detail as you can, starting with good memories before turning to hard ones.
3. Evaluate each chapter rather than only recalling it - ask what went well, what went badly, what you would do differently and what is still unresolved.
4. For difficult episodes, ask how you got through them and which of those coping skills you could use on a problem you face now.
5. Weigh the whole - name the people, values and threads that run through the chapters, and write a one-paragraph version of your life story as you now understand it.
6. Turn the review toward the future by choosing one or two achievable goals, repairs or conversations that follow from what you found.

*A manager in her late fifties is offered early retirement after a restructuring. She keeps returning to a project that failed ten years ago and a falling-out with a former mentor, and cannot decide what to do next.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| She either avoids thinking about her career at all, or lies awake replaying the failed project and the argument, concluding that her working life was a disappointment, and accepts or refuses the offer on impulse. | Over a few weekends she reviews her career chapter by chapter, recalling the teams she built and the people she trained as well as the failure. She notes what the failed project taught her about raising concerns early, sees that mentoring is the thread she most values, writes a short note to her former mentor, and decides to take the offer only if she can keep coaching new managers part-time. |

**Why:** Evaluating the whole story, rather than replaying its worst scenes, puts setbacks in proportion, extracts usable lessons and links the past to a concrete next step.

Ask yourself:
- Am I evaluating my past and drawing lessons from it, or just replaying the same painful scene?
- Have I given the good chapters as much detailed attention as the bad ones?
- What coping skills from past difficulties could I use on the problem in front of me now?
- Has looking back produced at least one concrete goal, repair or conversation for the future?

### [Reflective journaling](../articles/self-knowledge/reflective-journaling.md) — Practice

1. Pick one experience that still bothers you or that you want to understand, and that you feel able to face now; leave a fresh or overwhelming trauma for another time or for professional support.
2. Set aside 15 to 20 minutes in a private place on three or four days, ideally spread out rather than crammed together.
3. Write continuously about both what happened and how you felt, without editing, judging or worrying about grammar.
4. On later days, move from venting to making sense - ask why it happened, what it connects to and what you now see that you did not before.
5. In a final session, describe the episode as a neutral observer or as the other people involved would, and note one thing you will do differently.
6. Keep analysis for painful or puzzling events; for happy ones, replay and enjoy them rather than dissecting them.

*A project you led for eight months is cancelled at a quarterly review, and you suspect a peer argued against it behind your back.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| For two weeks you replay the meeting in your head every night, draft and delete a long angry message to the peer, and tell yourself you are fine during the day. | You write for twenty minutes on three evenings that week. The first page is pure anger; by the third you have listed what the review actually said, the budget pressure behind it, the warning signs you missed and what you would scope differently next time. You then ask the peer for a short conversation about their concerns. |

**Why:** Writing forces a disorganised memory into a sequence with causes, which tends to calm reactivity and produce lessons, whereas silent replaying keeps the same scene running without resolving it.

Ask yourself:
- Is my writing moving from what happened towards why it happened and what I now understand, or circling the same complaint?
- Am I picking apart a good experience I would be better off simply enjoying?
- After writing, do I feel more able to act, or more stirred up than when I began?
- Have I looked at this episode from the other people's side, at least once?

### [Regret](../articles/self-knowledge/regret.md) — Phenomenon

*Two years ago you decided not to apply for a team-lead role; a colleague got it and is thriving, and the news of their promotion brings the old decision back.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I always bottle it. I keep replaying that week and telling myself what an idiot I was, or I insist I have no regrets at all and change the subject. | I did hold back, and I wish I hadn't. What was I afraid of, and is that still true? The next lead role opens in spring, so this month I'll ask my manager for a stretch project and say plainly that I want to be considered. |

**Why:** The wiser response accepts the regret without self-attack, extracts the lesson and turns it toward an opportunity that is still open, which is when regret is most useful.

Ask yourself:
- Is there still something I can do about this, or is it time to let the goal go and invest in another?
- Am I learning a specific lesson from this regret, or replaying the same scene and blaming myself?
- Would I speak to a friend with this regret the way I am speaking to myself?
- Which choice in front of me now am I most likely to regret not making?

### [Self-deception](../articles/self-knowledge/self-deception.md) — Obstacle

*Six months ago you championed a new onboarding process for your team. Completion rates are flat, and two team leads have told you that new hires find it confusing.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Those two never liked the change, and the numbers are just seasonal. It needs another quarter. | If a peer had launched this and showed me these numbers, what would I tell them? I'll write down now what result would mean it isn't working, and ask two people who weren't involved to go through the data with me. |

**Why:** The first response explains away each unwelcome fact on its own terms. The second fixes a standard before looking and borrows outside eyes, which makes it harder for a wish to steer how the evidence is read.

Ask yourself:
- What evidence would change my mind here, and have I actually gone looking for it?
- Am I holding the evidence that flatters me to the same standard as the evidence that doesn't?
- Who would tell me plainly how this looks from the outside, and have I asked them?
- If I turned out to be wrong about this, what would it cost me, and is that cost shaping what I see?

### [Self-distancing](../articles/self-knowledge/self-distancing.md) — Practice

1. Notice the signs that you are immersed in an upsetting experience, such as replaying the same scene, rehearsing the same sentence or feeling the original anger or shame rise again.
2. Shift your vantage point by picturing the scene from across the room, as a neutral observer would see it, with yourself as one of the people in it.
3. Talk to yourself silently using your own name or "you" instead of "I", for example "Why is Sam so stung by this?"
4. Ask what lies behind the feeling and what you might be missing, rather than recounting again what happened.
5. Widen the frame by asking how the other people involved might see it, and how the situation could change over the coming months.
6. Ask what you would advise a friend in exactly this position, then choose one concrete next step.
7. Stop if the distance is turning into a way of avoiding the feeling or the problem, and seek support if the distress persists.

*Late on a Friday, your manager leaves a blunt comment on your project report saying the analysis "misses the point", and you feel your weekend souring.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I can't believe I got this so wrong. I'll keep rereading that comment, write a defensive reply tonight and hit send. | Why is Jordan so rattled by one line? Jordan doesn't yet know what the manager meant by 'the point'. If a friend showed me this, I'd tell them to ask one clarifying question on Monday, not to send a reply at midnight. |

**Why:** Viewing the moment as an observer cools the sting enough to admit what you don't know and to consider the other person's view, which is the kind of reasoning people find easier about a friend's problem than their own.

Ask yourself:
- Am I reliving this from the inside, or looking at it as an observer would?
- Would I give a friend in my position the advice I am about to follow?
- Have I asked what I might not know and how the other person sees this?
- Is this distance helping me understand the feeling, or helping me avoid it?

### [Self-knowledge](../articles/self-knowledge/self-knowledge.md) — Concept

*A team lead is surprised when an anonymous survey says people hesitate to bring her bad news, because she thinks of herself as approachable.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The survey must be skewed. I know I'm open; I always say my door is open. | I know my intentions, but they see my reactions. I'll ask two colleagues I trust what happens on my face when someone brings me a problem. |

**Why:** People are well placed to know their own inner states but poorly placed to see their visible behaviour and their most flattering or unflattering traits, which is where others' views add the most.

Ask yourself:
- Is the belief about myself that I am relying on here about an inner feeling, which I am well placed to know, or about how I behave, which others may see better?
- Which of my self-descriptions would I most hate to be wrong about, and have I ever tested it?
- When did someone who knows me well last tell me something about myself that surprised me, and what did I do with it?

### [Self-reflection](../articles/self-knowledge/self-reflection.md) — Skill

*A product launch you led slipped by three weeks, and the team retrospective is tomorrow morning.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You spend the evening going over the delay again and again, asking what is wrong with you and what your director must think, and arrive at the meeting tired, defensive and with nothing new to say. | You set aside twenty minutes, write down what you expected, what actually happened and where the gap first appeared, notice that you had dismissed an early warning from a colleague, and bring one change you will make in how you handle risk flags on the next project. |

**Why:** Reflection driven by curiosity and aimed at a lesson produces insight you can act on, whereas anxious self-scrutiny replays the hurt without improving the next decision.

Ask yourself:
- Am I examining this out of curiosity about what happened, or out of fear about what it says about me?
- Has this reflection produced an insight or a next step I can name in one sentence?
- Have I looked at the situation from at least one perspective other than my own?
- Is there someone who saw what happened whose view I should hear before I settle on a lesson?

## thinkers-and-works

### [Big Decisions: Opting, Converting, Drifting](../articles/thinkers-and-works/big-decisions-opting-converting-drifting.md) — Work

*Someone has been offered a two-year post abroad that would mean leaving their partner's home city, a close circle of friends and a settled career track.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This is the biggest choice of my life, so I'll build the perfect spreadsheet and keep refining it until the answer is obvious. | No spreadsheet can tell me who I'll be after two years away. I'll ask whether I can make it smaller first (a three-month secondment, a visit, a return clause), and if I can't, I'll decide on what matters to me now and accept that I'm opting, not calculating. |

**Why:** Ullmann-Margalit's point is that the values used to score a big decision may themselves change, so the reasonable move is to cut the decision down into reversible steps where possible and stop expecting a proof where none is available.

Ask yourself:
- Is this choice really big (life-changing and hard to undo), or am I treating a middle-sized decision as if it were?
- Can I turn this into a trial or a smaller reversible step before committing?
- Am I drifting, letting small commitments add up to a choice I have never actually faced?

## virtues-and-character

### [Aristotle's doctrine of the mean](../articles/virtues-and-character/doctrine-of-the-mean.md) — Principle

*A newly promoted team lead has to tell a long-serving colleague that his reports keep arriving late and incomplete.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Either I let it slide to keep the peace, or I lay it all out bluntly in front of the team so nobody can say I'm soft. | I tend to avoid conflict, so I'll lean a little firmer than feels natural: a private conversation, the specific facts, what I need by when, and a real question about what is getting in his way. |

**Why:** The right degree of firmness depends on this person and this problem, and knowing which way you usually err tells you which way to correct, as Aristotle advised.

Ask yourself:
- Which way do I usually err in situations like this one, too much or too little, and am I correcting for it?
- Am I calling a choice "balanced" just because it sits halfway between two options, rather than because it fits the situation?
- Is the strength of my reaction proportionate to how serious this actually is?

### [Cardinal virtues](../articles/virtues-and-character/cardinal-virtues.md) — Framework

*Your director asks you to present quarterly figures to the board with a forecast you believe is unrealistically rosy. Several colleagues' jobs depend on the board approving the plan.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Either keep quiet because it isn't my call, or stand up in the board meeting and announce that the numbers are fudged. | Check what the data actually support, raise the concern privately with the director first with the evidence and an honest alternative framing, and escalate calmly if it is ignored. |

**Why:** Silence fails courage and justice, while a public outburst is courage without prudence or temperance. Asking all four questions together (what is true, what is owed, what must be faced, what is the right measure) produces a response that is honest, fair, brave and proportionate at once, which is the point of treating the virtues as a set.

Ask yourself:
- What do I actually know here, and what am I only assuming or hoping?
- What do I owe each person affected, including the people who are not in the room?
- Is fear of discomfort stopping me from doing what I already judge to be right?
- Is appetite, anger or the wish to look good pushing me past the right measure?
- Am I leaning hard on one virtue, such as boldness or caution, at the expense of the others?

### [Courage](../articles/virtues-and-character/courage.md) — Virtue

*In a planning meeting, a senior director presents a launch date that you know the test results cannot support. Everyone else is nodding.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Stay quiet now and let the slip speak for itself later; or blurt out, in front of everyone, that the plan is fantasy. | Ask for two minutes, lay out the test data and what it implies for the date, frame it as protecting the launch the director cares about, and follow up privately afterwards. |

**Why:** Silence avoids risk at the cost of the goal, and a reckless outburst takes risk without judgment. The wiser response accepts a real personal cost for a worthy aim, and uses preparation, framing and follow-up so the risk actually serves that aim.

Ask yourself:
- What exactly am I afraid of losing here, and is that fear in proportion to the real stakes?
- Is the cause worth the risk I am about to take, or am I mainly after looking brave?
- If I stay silent, is that a judgment about timing, or am I just avoiding discomfort?
- Have I prepared, framed and planned the follow-up, or am I acting on a surge of anger?

### [Emulating moral exemplars](../articles/virtues-and-character/emulating-moral-exemplars.md) — Practice

1. Pick two or three people whose judgment you trust in a domain that matters to you now (a manager who handles conflict well, a relative who stays fair under pressure), and prefer people close to your own situation over distant heroes.
2. Watch for a specific moment where they acted well, and write down what they noticed, what they did and what they chose not to do.
3. Ask them, or work out, the reasons behind it, so that you learn the ideal the action served rather than only the gesture.
4. When you feel the sting of falling short, name the specific good you lack and one small step toward it this week, instead of looking for faults in them.
5. Try the step in a comparable situation of your own, adapted to your circumstances, then compare the result with what they did and why.
6. Widen and update your set of exemplars over time, and drop any you find yourself copying blindly or defending against all criticism.

*A newly promoted team lead has to tell a long-serving colleague that a project they championed is being cancelled. A senior manager in the next department is known for handling news like this well.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The new lead either copies the senior manager's breezy phrases word for word, or quietly decides that the manager is just naturally charismatic and that there's nothing to learn, and sends a short email. | The lead asks the manager how they handled a similar conversation, learns that they always explain the reason in person, acknowledge what the work achieved and offer the person a real role in what comes next, and plans a face-to-face meeting that does those three things in the lead's own words. |

**Why:** Emulation borrows the reasoning and the ideal behind an experienced person's choices, not their surface style, and it turns admiration into a concrete step the learner can actually take.

Ask yourself:
- Whose judgment would I trust most in exactly this kind of situation, and what would they notice first?
- Am I copying what my role model did, or what they were trying to achieve?
- When I compare myself with someone better at this, am I working out my next step or looking for reasons to dismiss them?
- Is the person I'm learning from close enough to my own circumstances that their example is one I can actually follow?
- Are there flaws in my role model I've stopped being willing to see?

### [Greed](../articles/virtues-and-character/greed.md) — Vice

*A sales lead has already hit this quarter's target. Two days before the books close, a long-standing client asks to move part of an order to next quarter for budget reasons, and a colleague's deal could share a bonus pool with hers.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "A target is a floor, not a ceiling." She leans on the client to keep the full order this quarter, quietly lets the colleague's paperwork slip down the queue, and books the record number. The client is cooler at the next renewal, and the colleague stops sharing leads. | "I've made my number. What would I advise someone else to do here?" She moves the order as the client asked, processes the colleague's deal on time, and writes down what the extra money would actually have changed for her. Her number is lower; the client and the colleague both keep working with her. |

**Why:** Greed is defined by never feeling that enough is enough and by discounting others' losses; naming a point of "enough" in advance and counting the costs to people around you puts back the two checks that the desire for more switches off.

Ask yourself:
- If I got this, would I actually feel I had enough, or would I immediately want the next thing?
- Who loses something if I take this, and would I be comfortable explaining the trade to them face to face?
- Am I treating "more" as a goal in itself, without being able to say what it is for?
- Am I comparing what I have with what someone else has, rather than with what I need?

### [Honesty](../articles/virtues-and-character/honesty.md) — Virtue

*Your manager asks, in front of the team, whether the migration you lead is on track for Friday. It is not; a data problem surfaced yesterday and you have not yet told anyone.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| We're in good shape. There are a few loose ends, but nothing to worry about. | Not for Friday, as things stand. We found a data issue yesterday. I'll have a revised date and options to you by this afternoon. |

**Why:** The first answer contains no outright lie but is built to mislead, and it spends trust the team will need when the slip comes out. The second states the fact plainly, owns the delay, and pairs bad news with a next step.

Ask yourself:
- If the other person later learned everything I know, would they feel I had misled them, even if every word I said was true?
- Am I keeping quiet here out of tact, or out of fear of an awkward moment?
- Is the kind thing I am about to say also accurate, and is the accurate thing I am about to say also kind?
- What am I telling myself about my own part in this that I would not want someone else to check?

### [Intellectual humility](../articles/virtues-and-character/intellectual-humility.md) — Virtue

*In a project review, a colleague points out that the sales forecast you built last month rests on an assumption you never checked.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The model is fine. The market just shifted; nobody could have seen that coming. | You're right, I didn't test that assumption. Let's rerun it with your figure and see how much the forecast moves. Thanks for catching it. |

**Why:** Owning the gap, rather than blaming circumstances or brushing it off, fixes the forecast, shows the team that errors can be raised safely, and treats the critic as an ally in getting it right.

Ask yourself:
- Which part of my view have I actually checked, and which part am I only sure of?
- What evidence or argument would change my mind here, and have I gone looking for it?
- When I was shown a mistake just now, did I feel curiosity or did I feel attacked?
- Am I underselling a strength I really do have, out of false modesty or fear of conflict?

### [Intellectual virtues](../articles/virtues-and-character/intellectual-virtues.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- Before I settled on this view, did I actually look for the strongest case against it?
- Am I being careful and thorough here, or just quick and confident?
- Is my curiosity about this question real, or do I mainly want to be proved right?
- Whose knowledge or testimony am I discounting, and is that because of the evidence or because of who they are?

### [Moral habituation](../articles/virtues-and-character/moral-habituation.md) — Practice

1. Name one virtue you want more of and the concrete situations where you currently fall short of it (for courage, perhaps raising concerns in meetings or declining unreasonable requests).
2. List those situations from mildest to hardest, and pick one at the bottom that is uncomfortable but clearly manageable.
3. Do the act on purpose, for the reason the virtue cares about, not for show; rehearse it first if it helps.
4. Afterwards, note what you did, how it felt, and how it landed, and adjust what you will do differently next time.
5. Repeat at the same level until it feels fairly easy and even satisfying, then move one rung up.
6. Check periodically with someone whose judgment you trust whether you are hitting the mean or drifting toward its excess, such as rashness or rigidity.

*A project coordinator notices that she agrees in meetings to deadlines she knows are unrealistic, then works late to cover the gap. She wants to become someone who says what she actually thinks.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| She reads a book on assertiveness, resolves to be braver, and at the next big review abruptly tells a senior sponsor in front of everyone that the whole plan is unworkable, then retreats into silence for weeks after it goes badly. | She starts small: this week she asks one clarifying question about scope in her own team's stand-up; next week she proposes a realistic date for a minor task; later she flags a risk to her manager one to one. After each, she jots down what happened and what to tweak, and only takes on the sponsor review once the smaller acts feel natural. |

**Why:** Character changes through repeated, well-aimed acts rather than through resolutions or one dramatic leap. Graded practice with reflection builds both the habit and the judgment to use it at the right time and in the right measure.

Ask yourself:
- What small act this week would be a genuine rung up the ladder for me, rather than a leap or no step at all?
- Am I doing this because it is the right thing to do here, or mainly to look brave, generous or disciplined?
- Did I reflect after the last attempt, or am I just repeating it by rote?
- Does this kind of action feel easier or more satisfying than it did a month ago, and if not, is the step the right size?
- Am I sliding past the mean into the opposite fault, such as rashness instead of courage?

### [Moral virtues](../articles/virtues-and-character/moral-virtues.md) — Concept

*A team lead is generous by temperament. When a struggling colleague asks for a second extension on a deliverable that other people are waiting on, she feels the familiar pull to say yes again.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "Of course, take another week." She absorbs the extra work herself, the downstream team slips, and the colleague never hears that the pattern is a problem. | She asks what is actually blocking the work, offers a specific piece of help and a firm new date, and tells the downstream team plainly what has changed. |

**Why:** Kindness of temperament is what Aristotle would call natural virtue: a good starting point that can still misfire. It becomes a full virtue only when practical wisdom aims it at what genuinely helps everyone involved, at the right time and in the right way.

Ask yourself:
- Am I doing the right thing gladly, grudgingly, or only because I would be embarrassed not to?
- Is my good impulse here being guided by judgment about this situation, or just by my usual temperament?
- Would I act the same way if the setting changed, if I were rushed, unobserved or in a different mood?
- Which of my virtues reliably show up at work but not at home, or the other way round?

### [Temperance](../articles/virtues-and-character/temperance.md) — Virtue

*The night before you present to a client, your team takes them to dinner with an open bar. Colleagues are ordering round after round.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Either keep pace with the table so you don't look stiff, or refuse everything and sit through the evening tense and joyless. | Enjoy the meal and a drink with the group, switch to water when the rounds speed up, and leave at a sensible hour with a friendly word to the host. |

**Why:** Matching the table hands your judgment to the room, and grim refusal mistakes temperance for having no pleasure at all. The temperate response enjoys what fits the occasion while keeping tomorrow's aim in view, which is what Aristotle means by desiring what one ought, as and when one ought.

Ask yourself:
- Am I choosing this pleasure, or is habit or the people around me choosing it for me?
- Will I still be glad of this amount tomorrow, given what I need to do and who depends on me?
- Am I treating every pleasure as suspect, mistaking joylessness for discipline?
- Where could I change my surroundings so I meet this temptation less often, instead of relying on resisting it?

### [Weakness of will](../articles/virtues-and-character/weakness-of-will.md) — Obstacle

*You decided on Sunday, for good reasons, to finish a grant report before answering the week's routine email. On Monday morning the inbox shows forty unread messages.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "I'll just clear these quickly so my head is free, then start the report." By lunch the report is untouched, and you tell yourself that answering email was the responsible choice after all. | "When I sit down at nine, I open the report file before the inbox." You close the mail app, work for ninety minutes, and then deal with email in a block you had already scheduled. |

**Why:** The first response quietly rewrites a sound resolution at the moment temptation is strongest. The second decides in advance when and where the better judgment will be acted on, and removes the cue that would trigger the lapse.

Ask yourself:
- Am I changing my plan because I have learned something new, or only because the moment of keeping it has arrived?
- If I asked myself yesterday, when I made this resolution, would I accept the reason I am now giving for dropping it?
- What is the specific cue that usually pulls me off course here, and have I planned what I will do when it appears?
- Am I telling myself a story that makes the easier option look like the wiser one?

## wisdom-at-work

### [After-action review](../articles/wisdom-at-work/after-action-review.md) — Practice

1. Hold the review soon after the event, while memories are fresh, and keep it short; for a long project, review at each milestone rather than only at the end.
2. Agree at the start that the purpose is learning, not grading or blame, and that what is said will not be used to punish anyone.
3. Establish what was supposed to happen, using the original plan, goals or standards.
4. Reconstruct what actually happened from several viewpoints and from any objective records, before anyone explains or defends.
5. Ask why the gap opened, looking for specific causes you could influence, including your own decisions.
6. Review what went well and why, not only what went wrong.
7. Agree on one to three concrete things to sustain or change, name who will apply each, and check at the start of the next similar task that they were applied.

*A small team's bid for a client contract has just been turned down, two weeks after an intense push to finish the proposal.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The manager sends a one-line message blaming the client's budget, the team moves straight to the next bid, and nobody mentions that the pricing section was finished in a rush on the last night. | The manager books thirty minutes the next morning, says the aim is to learn rather than to find fault, and asks what the plan was, what actually happened and why. The team notices that the scope was settled late and the pricing was never reviewed, also notes that the client praised the case studies, and agrees that next time pricing gets a review two days before the deadline, with one person responsible. |

**Why:** A specific, blame-free look at both what worked and what failed turns a disappointment into a lesson the team will actually use, instead of a convenient external excuse or a quiet repeat of the same mistake.

Ask yourself:
- Am I looking at what happened in order to learn from it, or to find someone to blame, including myself?
- Have I heard how this looked to the other people involved before deciding why it went the way it did?
- Did I ask what went right and why, or only what went wrong?
- What is the one concrete thing I will do differently next time, and when will I check that I did it?

### [Building a developmental network](../articles/wisdom-at-work/developmental-network.md) — Practice

1. Write down your goals for the next few years and the kinds of help you will need — skill-building, sponsorship, honest feedback, emotional support, a view of how decisions get made.
2. List everyone who currently takes an active interest in your development, note which part of your life each comes from (employer, school, profession, community, family) and how close the tie is.
3. Look for gaps and redundancy — needs nobody covers, or several advisers who all see the world from the same vantage point.
4. Approach one or two new people to fill the biggest gap, asking for something specific and thinking about what they might gain or learn from you in return.
5. Tend the ties you have with regular, low-stakes contact, and offer help to others rather than only taking it.
6. Revisit the map whenever your role, goals or circumstances change, letting some relationships become lighter and adding the ones the next stage needs.

*A mid-career analyst is weighing whether to move into a product-management role and has always relied on one trusted former manager for career advice.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll ask my old boss — he's always been right about my career. If he thinks I should stay an analyst, I'll stay. | My old boss knows my strengths, so I'll still talk to him, but he's never worked in product. I'll also ask a peer who made the same switch last year what the first months were really like, and a product lead in another company what they look for. Then I'll decide. |

**Why:** The wiser response keeps a valued strong tie but stops treating one person's vantage point as the whole picture; advisers from different settings supply information a single mentor cannot, which is what the developmental network perspective predicts matters most for career changes.

Ask yourself:
- Who, beyond my manager or one mentor, actively takes an interest in my growth right now?
- Do my advisers all come from the same place and see things the same way?
- What need do I have — feedback, sponsorship, support, inside knowledge — that nobody in my network currently meets?
- What am I offering the people who help me, and whom am I helping develop in turn?
- Has my network kept pace with where I am now, or is it still built for an earlier stage of my life?

### [Ethical fading](../articles/wisdom-at-work/ethical-fading.md) — Obstacle

*Two days before quarter end, a sales manager is short of target. A customer has verbally agreed to a deal but will not sign until next month. Her director suggests "pulling it forward" and booking it now.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "It's just a timing question, everyone does it, and finance can sort out the paperwork. I'm not going to lose my bonus over a few days." | "Let me say it plainly first: we'd be reporting revenue we don't have yet. I'll show my director the gap honestly, explain what is likely to close next month, and ask how we want the shortfall to be read." |

**Why:** The first response treats the choice as a timing and money problem, framed as a loss, so the ethical question never comes up. Naming the act in plain words and slowing down puts the moral dimension back on the table before the decision is made.

Ask yourself:
- If I described this decision in plain words, without the jargon, would it still sound acceptable?
- Am I calling this "just a business decision" so that I don't have to ask whether it is right?
- Is this a small step further along a line I would not have crossed all at once?
- What ethical implications might this decision have, and for whom?
- Am I deciding in a hurry, or to avoid a loss, in a way that is keeping me from thinking it through?

### [Hubris](../articles/wisdom-at-work/hubris.md) — Vice

*A department head has delivered three strong years in a row and been praised in the trade press. A senior analyst warns that a proposed expansion rests on optimistic demand figures.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "I've called it right every time so far, and the numbers will catch up with the vision. We're not slowing down for a spreadsheet." The analyst stops raising concerns, and the plan goes ahead unchanged. | "My recent wins are a reason to check myself, not to skip the check. Walk me through your demand figures, and let's have finance stress-test both versions before the board sees either." The plan is approved a quarter later at a smaller scale, with a review point. |

**Why:** Recent success and praise are exactly the conditions under which leaders overpay and overreach; deliberately inviting the dissenting case and an independent check restores the feedback that power tends to cut off.

Ask yourself:
- Am I dismissing this objection because of its evidence, or because of who raised it and how well things have gone for me lately?
- When did someone last change my mind on something important, and did I make it easy or costly for them?
- Who, if anyone, can still stop me from making this decision, and have I given them what they need to do so?
- Am I treating my organisation's success as proof of my own judgment?

### [Humble leadership](../articles/wisdom-at-work/humble-leadership.md) — Practice

1. When you get something wrong or don't know the answer, say so plainly and early, and say what you will do about it.
2. Name specific contributions by specific people, in their hearing and when reporting upward; say "we" for shared wins and "I" for your own errors.
3. Ask the team for feedback on one concrete thing you did, listen without defending, and tell them later what you changed.
4. Let people with more expertise on a task teach you, in front of others if possible.
5. Keep making clear decisions; in a crisis or under tight deadlines, act first and invite reflection afterwards.
6. Check that the humility is believed, not read as a tactic, by keeping your private conduct consistent with what you say in meetings.

*A project lead's schedule for a product launch slips by three weeks, partly because she under-estimated the testing work, and an analyst on the team had flagged the risk early.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The vendors let us down and testing turned out harder than anyone could have predicted. We'll push harder. | I under-estimated testing, and Priya flagged it in March; I should have listened. Here's the revised plan. Priya, can you walk me through how you sized it so I get it right next time? |

**Why:** Owning the error, crediting the person who saw it and asking to learn from her makes it safe for others to raise risks early, and it fixes the estimating method rather than just the date.

Ask yourself:
- When did I last tell my team, in so many words, that I was wrong about something?
- Who did the real work on this result, and have the people above me heard their names?
- Am I asking for input because I will use it, or to look open?
- Does this situation call for listening and learning, or for a fast, clear decision that I can review with the team later?

### [Leading others](../articles/wisdom-at-work/leading-others.md) — Context

*A newly promoted team lead, formerly the team's strongest engineer, sees a colleague's design for a new service and is sure her own approach would be faster to build.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'm the lead now, so we'll do it my way. I'll rewrite the plan tonight and hand out the tasks tomorrow. | Ask the colleague to walk the team through the design and its trade-offs, put the alternative on the table as one option rather than an order, ask who sees risks in either, and agree how the choice will be made and reviewed. |

**Why:** Relying on title wins compliance at best and silences the people closest to the work; inviting their view and weighing it treats authority as responsibility for the decision rather than a licence to skip other people's knowledge.

Ask yourself:
- Am I weighing my colleagues' advice as seriously as I would have before I had authority over them?
- When nobody objected in that meeting, did I check whether they agreed or just stayed quiet?
- Whose interests does this decision touch beyond the targets I am measured on?
- Am I still doing the work myself because it is needed, or because it is where I feel competent?

### [Moral awareness](../articles/wisdom-at-work/moral-awareness.md) — Skill

*A product analyst is asked to tune a subscription sign-up page so that fewer people notice the button for cancelling their free trial. The request arrives as an ordinary ticket, labelled "conversion optimisation".*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "It's an A/B test like any other. Legal will flag it if there's a problem, and my job is to move the metric." | "Before I build it: who is affected, and how? Customers would be charged because we made it harder to see how to cancel. That's a fairness question, not just a design one. I'll raise it with my lead and propose a test that improves sign-ups without hiding the exit." |

**Why:** The first response accepts the technical frame of the ticket, so the harm never registers as a moral issue. The second asks who could be harmed and names it in plain moral terms, which is exactly what research finds switches moral awareness on, before any judgment about what to do.

Ask yourself:
- Who could be harmed or wronged by this, even slightly, and would they agree to it if they knew?
- Am I describing this choice only in technical, financial or procedural terms?
- Would most of my colleagues, or the people affected, see an ethical issue here that I am not seeing?
- Is there a rule, promise or duty in play that I am treating as a mere formality?
- Do I feel a flicker of unease about this, and have I stopped to ask what it is telling me?

### [Moral case deliberation](../articles/wisdom-at-work/moral-case-deliberation.md) — Practice

1. Choose a real, current case from your own work that leaves you or your team unsure what is right, and ask someone trained and not involved in the decision to facilitate.
2. State the moral question as a concrete choice between two options, and name the harm each option would do.
3. Ask factual questions until everyone can stand in the case presenter's shoes, without judging how they handled it.
4. List everyone with a stake in the case and the values and norms that pull each of them towards one option or the other.
5. Look for alternatives beyond the original two options.
6. Each write down privately what you would do, the value behind it, what you would sacrifice, how you would limit that damage and what you would need to act on it.
7. Compare the answers in dialogue, exploring the reasons behind disagreements rather than voting or trying to win.
8. Close by asking what has changed in each person's view, who will act on what, and what to improve in the next session.

*A home-care team disagrees about an elderly client who refuses help with washing. Some nurses want to insist for hygiene and dignity; others say his refusal must be respected. Tension is growing and each shift handles it differently.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The team lead sends an email setting a rule, help with washing twice a week whether he likes it or not, and treats further objections as insubordination. | The team lead books an hour with a trained facilitator. The nurse closest to the client presents the case, the team names what is at stake on each side (his autonomy, his health, the relationship, staff integrity), finds an option nobody had considered, and agrees a shared approach to try and review in a month. |

**Why:** The rule settles the behaviour but not the moral question, so the disagreement and distress go underground. Deliberation surfaces the values in play, draws on everyone's knowledge of the client and lets the team own a considered judgment it can revisit.

Ask yourself:
- Can I state the dilemma as two concrete options, and say what harm each would do and to whom?
- Have I understood the case from the position of the person who has to act, before judging it?
- Whose values and perspective are missing from how we have framed this case?
- Am I trying to understand my colleagues' reasons, or only to win them over to my view?
- Who will act on what we concluded, and when will we look again at how it went?

### [Servant leadership](../articles/wisdom-at-work/servant-leadership.md) — Practice

1. Before a decision that affects your team, write down who it serves and what each person directly affected needs from it.
2. Hold a regular one-to-one with each person that is about their growth (skills, goals, what is getting in their way), not only about tasks.
3. Remove one concrete obstacle per week that is slowing someone else down, and tell them it is done.
4. Persuade rather than order where time allows - explain the reasons for a decision and listen to objections before closing it.
5. Ask how the decision lands on people outside the team (customers, other departments, the community), and on the least powerful people it touches.
6. Keep setting direction and holding standards, and say no to requests that would serve one person at the cost of the team or the work.
7. Protect your own capacity - agree with your own manager what you can take on, and get support when serving others starts to drain you.

*A team lead is asked to move a capable junior analyst onto a high-visibility project that the lead could take on personally, and the analyst has said she wants to build client-facing skills.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll keep the client project myself - it's my chance to shine, and she can stay on the reporting work she's good at. | This project is the growth she asked for. I'll give it to her, sit in on the first two client calls, debrief her after each one, and make sure her name is on the result when we report upward. |

**Why:** The wiser response treats the analyst's development as a goal in its own right while still guarding the quality of the work, which is the balance servant leadership asks for; it also avoids using the role mainly to advance oneself.

Ask yourself:
- Are the people I lead growing - more capable, more independent - because of how I lead them?
- Am I serving their real needs, or just pleasing them and avoiding hard calls?
- Who outside my team, especially with the least power, is affected by this decision, and have I considered them?
- Is serving others leaving me exhausted or torn between conflicting demands, and have I asked for the support I need?

### [Speaking up at work](../articles/wisdom-at-work/speaking-up-at-work.md) — Context

*The evening before a board meeting, a junior analyst spots that a revenue forecast in her director's presentation double-counts one large contract.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| He's the director and he's been doing this for twenty years. If I say something I'll look like I'm second-guessing him, so I'll leave it. | Check the numbers twice, then message him privately tonight: show the two rows, say what the corrected total would be, and offer to update the slide — leaving the decision with him. |

**Why:** The error is concrete, the cost of silence is high and falls on others, and raising it privately, early and with a fix respects his authority while still giving him the truth he needs.

Ask yourself:
- Is my silence here a judgment about this issue, or a habit of assuming speaking up is never safe or worthwhile?
- Have I checked my facts and my motives before raising it?
- Who bears the cost if I stay quiet, and who if I speak?
- Am I choosing a time, place and framing in which this person can actually hear it?

### [Stewardship](../articles/wisdom-at-work/stewardship.md) — Virtue

*An engineering manager can hit this quarter's delivery target by skipping overdue maintenance on a system the team will hand over to another group next year.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Ship the features and skip the maintenance. The target is mine; the next team can deal with the debt. | Show my director what skipping the work would cost the team that inherits the system, propose a smaller feature set plus the essential fixes, and write a clear handover note either way. |

**Why:** The first response treats the system as a means to one person's quarterly result. The stewardly response treats it as held in trust for its future users and owners, accepts a visible short-term cost, and is honest with the people who set the target.

Ask yourself:
- If I handed this role, team or budget to someone tomorrow, would it be in better or worse shape than when I received it?
- Who will live with the consequences of this decision after I have moved on, and have I counted their interests?
- Am I taking responsibility for this outcome, or quietly protecting my own record?
- Am I calling it stewardship when I am really keeping control that others should share?

### [Wisdom in the professions](../articles/wisdom-at-work/wisdom-in-professions.md) — Context

*A customer-support agent is measured on call length. A distressed caller has been wrongly billed for months and is clearly confused by the standard explanation the script provides.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Read the scripted answer, open a ticket, and end the call inside the six-minute target. The metric is what my review looks at. | Take the extra few minutes to understand what went wrong, explain it in plain terms, and fix or escalate it properly; afterwards, tell my team lead the script failed this case and suggest how to change it. |

**Why:** The script and the target exist to serve customers, so following them against that purpose gets the letter right and the job wrong. The wiser agent also treats the misfit as information for the system, rather than quietly working around it every time.

Ask yourself:
- What is this job for, and would the people it serves recognise what I am about to do as serving that purpose?
- Is this a case the rule was written for, or one its authors did not foresee?
- Am I departing from the protocol because the case demands it, or because it is inconvenient or I am overconfident?
- Am I doing this because it is right for the person in front of me, or because it moves the number I am paid on?
- Who could I talk this case through with before acting, and will I report the misfit so the rule can improve?

### [Wise leadership](../articles/wisdom-at-work/wise-leadership.md) — Concept

*A team lead is told to ship a feature by Friday to hit a quarterly target; testing has found that it occasionally shows one customer's order history to another.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Ship it. The bug is rare, the target is mine, and we'll patch it quietly next sprint. | Lay out who is affected (customers, the support team, the company's reputation, the people chasing the target), tell my manager plainly what we know and don't know about the bug, propose shipping without the faulty part, and walk the junior engineer who found it through how I'm weighing it. |

**Why:** The wiser response weighs everyone the decision touches rather than one metric, is honest about uncertainty, and uses the moment to build someone else's judgment.

Ask yourself:
- Whose interests does this decision touch, and have I weighed them or only the ones I'm measured on?
- What do I not know here, and have I said so to the people relying on me?
- Is my confidence coming from the facts of this case, or from my position and past success?
- What will the people who watched me decide this learn about how to decide?

## wisdom-traditions

### [Bhagavad Gita](../articles/wisdom-traditions/bhagavad-gita.md) — Work

*A project lead has spent months on a proposal that goes to a review board next week, and a promotion may ride on the board's verdict.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Everything depends on their yes. I'll cut the risky analysis they might question, and if they say no, the whole year was wasted. | My part is to make this proposal as sound and honest as I can, including the analysis they might question. I want a yes, but I'll judge my work by whether I did it well, not only by what the board decides. |

**Why:** The wiser response keeps caring about the outcome while letting the quality of the action, which is in your hands, guide what you do, which is close to what the Gita's teaching on the fruits of action asks.

Ask yourself:
- Am I doing this because it is the right thing for me to do here, or only for what I hope to get from it?
- If the result goes against me, will I still be able to say I acted well?
- Is my calm steadiness, or have I quietly stopped caring about the people my action affects?

### [Book of Proverbs](../articles/wisdom-traditions/book-of-proverbs.md) — Work

*In a team meeting, a colleague confidently repeats a misleading claim about why a project failed and invites you to argue about it.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I always shut this kind of thing down on the spot, so I'll tear the claim apart in front of everyone, whatever it costs. | Will answering help the room see the facts, or just drag me into a shouting match? Here the claim will shape a decision, so I'll correct it briefly and calmly, with the data, and leave the rest for a private conversation. |

**Why:** Proverbs sets two opposite sayings about answering a fool side by side, which pushes the reader to judge each situation rather than apply one rule mechanically.

Ask yourself:
- Am I applying a saying or rule of thumb as if it fit every situation, or have I checked whether it fits this one?
- Is my way of seeing this right only in my own eyes, and whose counsel have I actually asked?
- Will my words here calm the situation or stir up more anger?

### [Impermanence](../articles/wisdom-traditions/impermanence.md) — Principle

*A project manager's team is reorganised; her favourite colleague moves to another group and the weekly rhythm she relied on disappears.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This was the best team I've ever had and they've wrecked it. I'll keep doing things the old way and wait for it to be put back. | That team was always going to change; people move on, and so will this new setup. I'll thank my old colleague properly, stay in touch, and figure out what this new group needs from me now. |

**Why:** The wiser response grieves what was good without demanding that it stay fixed, and puts energy into the situation as it actually is.

Ask yourself:
- Am I treating something that is already changing as if it should stay exactly as it is?
- If this difficult situation is temporary, how would I act differently today?
- Is this good thing worth savouring more because it will not last?
- Am I using "nothing lasts" to avoid caring or committing, rather than to see clearly?

### [Instruction of Hardjedef](../articles/wisdom-traditions/instruction-of-hardjedef.md) — Work

*A manager in her forties keeps postponing a will, a conversation with her family about her wishes, and a handover plan for the team she built, because work always feels more urgent.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Thinking about the end is morbid, and there's plenty of time; I'll deal with it when things calm down. | While things are going well is exactly when I can do this calmly. This month I'll draft the will, tell my family what I'd want, and start training a successor. |

**Why:** Hardjedef's advice ties preparing for death to the prosperous middle of life, when there is time and means to do it well, and treats that preparation as a service to the living.

Ask yourself:
- Am I putting off a task that only matters because I will not be here forever, and is now actually the calm moment to do it?
- If I left tomorrow, who would carry on what I care about, and have I made it possible for them to?
- Am I counting on heirs or successors to remember and look after things I could arrange myself?

### [Philosophical hermeneutics](../articles/wisdom-traditions/philosophical-hermeneutics.md) — Tradition

Ask yourself:
- Which of my pre-judgments about this person or problem am I bringing in without noticing?
- Have I listened to the other view as if it might be right, or only waited to reply?
- What question is this situation actually putting to me, before I reach for a rule?
- After this conversation, has my own view changed at all, or did I only try to change theirs?

### [The four immeasurables](../articles/wisdom-traditions/four-immeasurables.md) — Framework

1. Name the situation in front of you: someone is doing well, someone is suffering, someone is simply there, or the outcome is out of your hands.
2. Choose the quality that fits it, goodwill for the ordinary encounter, compassion for suffering, sympathetic joy for another's success and equanimity for what you cannot change.
3. Check the quality for its look-alike, asking whether goodwill has become clinging, compassion has become your own distress, joy has become excitement or calm has become not caring.
4. Widen the circle one step, offering the same attitude to someone neutral or difficult, not only to people you already like.
5. Afterwards, notice which of the four came easily and which you avoided, and practise the neglected one next time.

*A peer you trained wins the promotion you also applied for, on the same day another teammate tells you their project has just been cancelled.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I congratulate the winner through gritted teeth, then spend lunch with the other teammate agreeing that management is hopeless, and leave work feeling worse about both of them. | I let the disappointment register, then congratulate my peer properly and mean it. With the teammate I listen and ask what would help, without stoking the grievance, and I accept that the promotion decision is made and no longer mine to change. |

**Why:** Each person needed a different response; sympathetic joy keeps envy from souring the success, compassion meets the setback, and equanimity stops your own loss from colouring both.

Ask yourself:
- Can I be glad about this person's success, or am I quietly keeping score?
- Is what I am calling compassion here help for them, or my own distress at their pain?
- Is my calm the steadiness of seeing clearly, or have I simply stopped caring?
- Would I offer this same goodwill to someone I find difficult, or only to people on my side?
