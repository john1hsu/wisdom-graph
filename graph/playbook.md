## adversity-and-mortality

### [Memento mori](../articles/adversity-and-mortality/memento-mori.md) — Practice

1. Pick a fixed cue for the reflection, such as waking up, a commute, or the last few minutes before sleep.
2. Bring to mind, concretely and in your own case, that your life will end and that you do not know when.
3. Look back over the last day or week and ask how you actually spent your time and attention.
4. Ask what you would regret leaving undone or unsaid, and whom you would think of first.
5. Choose one concrete action for today that reflects what matters, and let go of one petty grievance or craving.
6. Return your attention to the task in front of you; if the reflection leaves you more anxious than clear, shorten it or stop.

*You have spent a week stewing over a curt email from a colleague and are drafting a sharp reply late in the evening while your family is in the next room.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll send a reply that puts them in their place, then keep going through my inbox until midnight. | If my time were shorter than I assume, this exchange would not be worth another evening. I'll send a short, civil reply tomorrow and spend tonight with the people in the next room. |

**Why:** Remembering that time is limited and uncertain shifts attention from winning a minor status contest to the relationships and values you would actually want to have served.

Ask yourself:
- If my time were shorter than I assume, would this still be worth my anger or effort?
- Am I using the thought of death to clarify what matters, or to feed dread or reckless indulgence?
- What would I want to have said or done for the people involved here?

## compassion-and-relationships

### [Compassionate reappraisal](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/compassionate-reappraisal.md) — Practice

1. Sit somewhere quiet and settle your breathing for a minute or two before you start.
2. Recall one specific time a particular person hurt or wronged you, and name plainly what they did and what it cost you.
3. Picture the offender as a whole person with a history, fears and other sides to them, not only as the author of this act.
4. Treat the wrong as a sign that this person needs to grow or change, without telling yourself it was acceptable.
5. Try, for about two minutes, to sincerely wish them something good, such as healing or becoming someone who would not do this again.
6. Notice your thoughts, feelings and body as you do this, and repeat the exercise when you catch yourself replaying the hurt.
7. Decide separately, on the facts, what boundary, conversation or accountability the situation still needs.

*A teammate missed a handover deadline and then told your manager the delay was your fault, and you lie awake replaying it.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I keep going over every word they said to the manager. They're two-faced, that's all there is to it, and I'll be cold to them from now on. | They were wrong to blame me, and I'll correct the record with the manager tomorrow. They were scared of being blamed, which doesn't excuse it, and I hope they learn to own their mistakes. I can wish them that and still watch my back. |

**Why:** Replaying the offence keeps anger and arousal high, while seeing the person as flawed and in need of change lets the anger ease without denying the wrong or giving up the practical response.

Ask yourself:
- Am I wishing this person well, or am I telling myself that what they did was fine?
- Is this wrong still going on and within my power to change, so that action rather than reappraisal is the priority?
- After the exercise, what concrete step, if any, does fairness to me or others still require?
- Am I trying this because I'm ready, or because I feel I ought to have moved on by now?

### [Empathy](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/empathy.md) — Skill

*A colleague tells you, close to tears, that their project has been cancelled after months of work, and you have your own deadline in an hour.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I absorb their upset so fully that I feel sick about it too, then I either pile on about how unfair management is or quietly avoid them for the rest of the week because it is too much. | I take a minute to really listen and say it back: 'Months of work, and it's gone. That's a real loss.' I notice the heaviness in me, remind myself it is their loss, not mine, and ask what would help. Later I check in and offer a concrete hand with the next project. |

**Why:** Feeling with someone opens the door, but staying clear about whose feeling it is and turning it into warm concern and practical help keeps you from either drowning in their distress or backing away from it.

Ask yourself:
- Have I actually tried to understand how this looks from their side, or am I assuming I already know?
- Am I feeling with them, or am I now caught up in my own distress about their distress?
- Is my empathy here going only to the person who is closest, most like me or most vivid, while others affected stay invisible?
- After understanding what they feel, what would actually help them, as opposed to what would relieve my discomfort?

### [Forgiveness](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/forgiveness.md) — Virtue

*A colleague presented your analysis as their own to a client, later admitted it and apologised, and you still have to work together on the account.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll be polite, but I'm keeping score, and I'll make sure the client finds out what they're really like. | I'll tell them plainly that it was wrong and that I'll put my name on my own work from now on. Then I'll stop rehearsing it and treat them fairly on this project. |

**Why:** Forgiving here means dropping the campaign of payback while keeping the judgement that it was wrong and the safeguards that protect you; it is neither pretending nothing happened nor trusting blindly.

Ask yourself:
- Am I holding on to this to protect myself or someone else, or mainly to make them pay?
- Have I separated forgiving from excusing, forgetting and reconciling, so I know which of these I'm actually being asked to do?
- If they keep doing this, what boundary would self-respect require alongside any forgiveness?
- Have I given myself time to name the hurt before deciding whether, and how, to forgive?

### [Perspective-taking](../articles/compassion-and-relationships/perspective-taking.md) — Practice

1. Before you respond, name your own first reading of the situation so you can set it aside for a moment.
2. Imagine the other person's day, constraints and goals, and ask what they know that you don't and what they want from this.
3. Write down two or three guesses about how they see it, and mark each one as a guess.
4. Check the guesses by asking open questions ("How does this look from where you sit?") and listen without rebutting.
5. Revise your reading with what you heard, then decide what to do, looking for an option that serves both of you.

*A team lead notices that a usually reliable colleague has missed two deadlines in a row and seems curt in meetings.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I decide they've stopped caring, picture how I'd feel if I were slacking, and send a sharp message about accountability. | I sketch what their fortnight probably looked like, note that I'm guessing, then ask privately how the project is going from their side. It turns out a second project landed on them without my knowing; we reprioritise together. |

**Why:** Imagining their side softened my reaction, but only asking told me what was actually going on; taking and getting perspective together led to a fair and workable fix.

Ask yourself:
- Am I imagining how they feel, or how I would feel in their place?
- Which of my beliefs about their view have I actually checked with them?
- Have I asked a question I don't already know the answer to?
- Is there an option that meets what they want as well as what I want?

## emotions-and-equanimity

### [Equanimity](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/equanimity.md) — Virtue

*An hour before you present to leadership, you learn that the client your team spent three months courting has chosen a competitor.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This is a disaster. I'll open with who dropped the ball, and frankly I'm not sure this team can win anything. | That hurts, and I'll let myself feel it. What's in my hands now is an honest account of what we learned and what we'll try differently, so that's what I'll present. |

**Why:** The loss is still felt, but it no longer chooses your words; attention moves from blame and panic to the part of the situation you can actually shape.

Ask yourself:
- Am I reacting to what actually happened, or to the story I am telling about it?
- Is my calm here steadiness, or am I simply refusing to feel or to care?
- If this turned out well, or badly, would I still be able to act on my values?
- What part of this is up to me, and am I putting my energy there?

### [Negative visualization](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/negative-visualization.md) — Practice

1. Choose one thing you value and tend to take for granted, such as a relationship, your health, your job or an ordinary comfort.
2. Remind yourself plainly of its nature, starting small, for example that this mug can break and that the people you love are mortal.
3. For a minute or two, imagine concretely what your life would be like without it, or how it might never have come about.
4. Return to the present and notice that you do still have it; name one way you will use or appreciate it today.
5. Before a risky plan, list what could go wrong, separate what is up to you from what is not, and prepare for the parts you can influence.
6. Stop if the exercise turns into dread or repetitive worry, and shorten it or set it aside.

*Your manager asks you to present to senior leadership next week, and you also have a job you mostly enjoy but have started to complain about.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll keep replaying how badly the presentation could go every night until then, and keep grumbling about this job. | Once, I'll list what could go wrong in the presentation, prepare for the questions I can foresee, and accept that the reaction isn't fully up to me. And I'll take a minute to picture not having this job at all, then notice what I'd miss. |

**Why:** A brief, bounded rehearsal turns vague fear into preparation and restores appreciation, whereas dwelling on the worst only adds suffering before anything has happened.

Ask yourself:
- Am I rehearsing a possible loss in order to prepare and appreciate, or am I just worrying?
- After imagining this loss, did I come back to what I still have and what I can do now?
- Which parts of the outcome I fear are actually up to me?

### [Rumination](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/rumination.md) — Vice

*In your annual review, your manager says your project updates are often late. That night you cannot stop thinking about it.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "Why do I always do this? What does she really think of me? I'm probably the weakest person on the team." Two hours later you are still replaying the meeting and have decided nothing. | "If a colleague told me this, I'd ask what exactly went wrong and what they'd do next. The last three updates slipped because I waited for complete data. I'll propose a short interim update every Friday and check that works for her." Then you put it down for the night. |

**Why:** Asking what happened and how to act, from a step back, turns the same worry into a plan, whereas asking why in a self-critical loop keeps the hurt fresh and leaves the problem untouched.

Ask yourself:
- Has this round of thinking produced a new insight or a next step, or am I replaying the same scene?
- Am I asking how to deal with this, or only why it happened and what it says about me?
- What would I advise a friend in exactly this position, and can I take that advice tonight?
- Have I tested the fear I keep circling, or only rehearsed it?

### [The view from above](../articles/emotions-and-equanimity/view-from-above.md) — Practice

1. Name the specific thing that is gripping you right now, such as a slight, a setback, a rivalry or a wish for praise.
2. Picture yourself rising slowly above the room, the building and the city until you can see whole countries and the many lives going on in them at once.
3. Widen the view in time as well, recalling the people who lived before you and those who will come after, few of whom will ever know of this moment.
4. From that height, look back at your situation and ask how large it really is, and what part of it actually depends on you.
5. Come back down deliberately and choose one concrete, decent action for today, so the wider view ends in conduct rather than detachment.
6. Stop and set the exercise aside if it leaves you feeling that nothing matters, and talk to someone you trust instead.

*A colleague's proposal is chosen over yours in front of the whole department, and you spend the evening composing a sharp reply-all email.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Everyone saw that. I'm sending this tonight so they know the decision was wrong and I won't be pushed aside. | Picture this from far above: one meeting among millions happening today, which few will remember next year. What matters is the project and how I treat people. I'll sleep on it, then ask my manager what would have made my proposal stronger. |

**Why:** Seeing the slight at its real scale lowers the heat of the moment, and coming back down to one useful action keeps the wider view from turning into either revenge or indifference.

Ask yourself:
- Seen from far above and years from now, how big is this, really?
- Am I using the wider view to act better here, or to stop caring about people who are affected?
- Which part of this is about my reputation, and which part is about what is right to do?

## foundations

### [Implicit theories of wisdom](../articles/foundations/implicit-theories-of-wisdom.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- When I call someone wise, am I responding to their judgment and care for others, or mainly to their intelligence, confidence or age?
- Whose advice am I about to rely on here, and what picture of wisdom made me choose them?
- Would someone raised with a different idea of wisdom see this choice as wise, and what would they weigh that I am leaving out?

### [Socratic ignorance](../articles/foundations/socratic-ignorance.md) — Principle

*A team lead is asked in a planning meeting whether a new supplier can deliver on time, and has only skimmed their proposal.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Yes, they're solid, I'm sure of it. Let's lock them in. | I've only skimmed it, so I don't actually know. Here's what I'd need to check, and who has worked with them before? |

**Why:** Separating what you know from what you merely feel sure of keeps a confident guess from becoming the team's decision, and invites the people who do know to speak.

Ask yourself:
- Do I actually know this, or am I only confident about it?
- If someone questioned me closely here, where would my account run out?
- Am I treating skill in my own field as if it made me an authority on this question too?

## judgment-and-decisions

### [Deliberation](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/deliberation.md) — Skill

1. Check that the matter is actually up to you and that its outcome is genuinely uncertain; if not, stop deliberating and either accept it or act on what is known.
2. Say plainly what you are trying to achieve, and ask whether that end is worth pursuing before you plan how to reach it.
3. List at least two or three realistic ways of getting there, not just the first one that occurred to you.
4. For the most promising option, trace backwards from the goal to the very next thing you would have to do, and drop options whose chain hits something impossible.
5. If the decision is important, talk it through with someone who knows this kind of situation.
6. Before trusting a strong gut feeling, ask whether you have had long, well-fed-back experience with situations like this one.
7. Set a point at which deliberation ends, then choose and act promptly, without reopening the question unless new facts appear.

*A manager has to decide within a week whether to move a struggling analyst to a different team or keep coaching her in her current role.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| My gut says she's not cut out for this. I'll announce the move on Monday and be done with it. | Is this really my call, and what am I trying to achieve: her success, or a quieter team? Let me list the options, including a coaching plan with a review date, talk it over with HR and her previous lead, and decide by Thursday. |

**Why:** The wiser response clarifies the goal, widens the options and borrows others' judgment on an important, uncertain matter, but still commits to a decision on time instead of drifting.

Ask yourself:
- Is this decision actually within my power, and is the outcome really uncertain, or am I deliberating to put off accepting something?
- Have I checked that the goal itself is a good one, not just how to reach it?
- Did I consider more than one way forward before settling on the first idea I had?
- Is my confidence here earned by long, well-fed-back experience, or is it just a strong feeling?
- Have I set a point where thinking stops and acting starts?

### [Major life decisions](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/major-life-decision.md) — Context

*After eight years in a stable accounting job, Priya has an offer to retrain as a teacher at lower pay, and has been going back and forth for months.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll wait until I'm sure I'll be happier as a teacher. Until then, staying put is the safe choice. | I can't know now what teaching will feel like, and staying is a choice too. I'll test what I can (shadow a class, ask two teachers what surprised them), decide what I'd want to have tried, and set a date. |

**Why:** Waiting for a certainty that can't arrive quietly chooses the status quo, which the evidence suggests people over-favour; gathering what is knowable and naming the real reasons is the wiser path.

Ask yourself:
- Am I treating "not deciding" as if it were not itself a choice?
- Am I assuming the person I'll be in ten years wants exactly what I want today?
- Which part of this can I learn about before committing, and which part can I only learn by living it?

### [Moral algebra](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/moral-algebra.md) — Practice

1. Use the method only for an important choice that is up to you and genuinely two-sided, not for a matter of taste or a change you have already committed to.
2. Frame one specific option (for example, "accept the transfer") and divide a page into two columns headed Pro and Con.
3. Over three or four days, add a short note to either column whenever a reason occurs to you, instead of trying to list everything in one sitting.
4. Bring all the reasons into view and give each a rough weight, such as 1 to 3.
5. Strike out pairs of reasons of equal weight on opposite sides, and let one strong reason cancel two or three weaker ones that together equal it.
6. Ask whether any single remaining reason is decisive on its own, or whether a reason you never wrote down is the one actually driving you.
7. Wait a day or two; if no important new reason appears, decide by the balance and act on it.

*A project manager is offered a two-year posting to the company's office in another city and has to reply within a week.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| It sounds exciting and I hate saying no to leadership. I'll accept in tomorrow's one-to-one and sort out the details later. | I'll keep a Pro and Con page on my phone for the next four days, weigh the reasons on Friday, and see what's left after cancelling the equal ones. If the only big thing on the Con side is my partner's job, that's a conversation to have before I answer, not a line to cancel out. |

**Why:** Gathering reasons over several days stops the mood of one meeting from deciding, weighing rather than counting keeps a single heavy reason from being outvoted by trivia, and the final check surfaces the reason that really matters.

Ask yourself:
- Did I collect reasons over a few days, or only those that occurred to me in one mood?
- Did I weigh the reasons, or just count which column was longer?
- Is there one reason so important that it should settle this on its own, whatever the rest of the list says?
- Am I using the list to choose, or to talk myself out of something I had already decided to do?
- Now that the balance is clear, have I set a time to act on it?

### [Seeking counsel](../articles/judgment-and-decisions/seeking-counsel.md) — Practice

1. Name the decision and decide whether it is important and uncertain enough to deserve outside counsel.
2. Write down your own current view and how confident you are, before you ask anyone.
3. Choose two or three advisers who know this kind of situation, including at least one who has lived through it and one likely to disagree with you.
4. Ask each one separately and personally, describe the problem without revealing your preferred answer, and ask what they would do and why.
5. Ask anyone who has already been through the same choice how it actually turned out and felt for them.
6. Compare their answers with your own and move your view meaningfully toward the advice where they know more or agree with each other, noting where and why you still differ.
7. Make the decision yourself, take responsibility for it, and later tell your advisers what you decided and how it went.

*A team lead has a job offer from a fast-growing start-up and must answer by Friday. They are excited and have already half-decided to accept.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll ask my best friend, who always backs me, mention the salary first, and take the yes as confirmation. | I'll write down my own answer first, then ask a former colleague who joined a start-up two years ago how it really went, and a mentor who tends to be cautious. I won't tell them which way I'm leaning, and I'll actually shift my view if they both raise the same worry. |

**Why:** Asking advisers who know the terrain, without steering them, gives the decision a second independent view and the lived experience of someone who has already been there, which people tend to undervalue.

Ask yourself:
- Did I ask people who know this kind of decision, or only people who would agree with me?
- Did I ask without signalling the answer I wanted?
- When the advice differed from my view, did I actually move, or did I explain it away?
- Have I asked anyone who has already lived through this choice how it felt afterwards?

## meaning-and-the-good-life

### [Gratitude](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/gratitude.md) — Virtue

*A teammate quietly stayed late to fix a broken data feed so your quarterly report could go out on time.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Good, it's fixed. That's their job anyway; on to the next thing. | I'll tell them specifically what their evening saved, mention it to our manager, and keep an eye out for a chance to help them in turn. |

**Why:** Gratitude notices both the benefit and the goodwill behind it, then answers with thanks and goodwill rather than taking it for granted or treating it as a debt to settle and forget.

Ask yourself:
- Have I noticed who made this good thing possible, and the effort or intention it took them?
- Have I actually told them, specifically, rather than only feeling it?
- Is my thanks free, or am I acting out of a sense of debt, guilt or deference I don't really owe?
- Am I using "be grateful" to talk myself, or someone else, out of a legitimate complaint?

### [Logotherapy](../articles/meaning-and-the-good-life/logotherapy.md) — Framework

1. Name one thing you are creating or contributing right now, however small, and who it serves.
2. Name one experience or person you value that you could give more attention to this week.
3. For a hardship you cannot change, decide what stance toward it you want to be able to look back on.
4. Turn your attention from monitoring yourself to the task or person in front of you.

*A team's long-running project is cancelled after a reorganisation, and a project lead feels the past year was wasted.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| None of it mattered. I'll keep my head down and stop caring about the work. | The project is gone, but the people I trained and what we learned stay. What do I want to build next, and how do I want to carry this? |

**Why:** Looking for meaning in contribution, relationships and chosen attitude, rather than only in outcomes you control, keeps a setback from emptying out the whole effort.

Ask yourself:
- Am I asking what I want from life, or what this situation is asking of me?
- Is there anything left for me to create, experience or stand for here, even if the outcome is out of my hands?
- Am I watching myself so closely that it is getting in the way of what I'm trying to do?

## science-of-wisdom

### [Berlin wisdom paradigm](../articles/science-of-wisdom/berlin-wisdom-paradigm.md) — Framework

Ask yourself:
- Have I considered how this problem looks across the person's whole life, past and future, and not only today?
- Have I named what I do not know about this situation, and what I would do if things turn out differently than I expect?
- Am I judging this choice by my own values only, or have I tried to understand the values and priorities behind it?

### [Solomon's paradox](../articles/science-of-wisdom/solomons-paradox.md) — Obstacle

*You recently helped a teammate think calmly through a clash with another department. This week a peer quietly reassigns part of your own project without asking you.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This is a power grab, plain and simple. I know exactly what they're up to, and I'm escalating to our director today. | If my teammate described this to me, I'd ask what they actually know about the peer's reasons, what a fair split could look like, and how it might look in a month. I'll ask the peer for ten minutes first. |

**Why:** Treating your own conflict as you would a colleague's brings back the habits you already use as an adviser, namely admitting what you don't know, looking for compromise and expecting the situation to change.

Ask yourself:
- If a friend brought me this exact problem, what would I tell them, and am I following that advice myself?
- Could I state the other person's view in words they would accept as fair?
- Have I admitted what I don't know about their motives and about how this could unfold?
- Am I treating any compromise as a defeat?

### [Three-dimensional model of wisdom](../articles/science-of-wisdom/three-dimensional-wisdom-model.md) — Framework

Ask yourself:
- Am I trying to understand what is really going on here, including what I cannot know, or only looking for facts that confirm my first reaction?
- Have I looked at this situation, and my own part in it, from at least one other person's point of view?
- Is my response moved by concern for the people affected, or mainly by protecting my ego?

## self-knowledge

### [Know thyself](../articles/self-knowledge/know-thyself.md) — Principle

*After a project review, a manager is told by two team members that her meetings run long because she rarely lets others finish a point.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| That's not me. I know how I come across, and I'm a good listener. | I don't experience myself that way, but two people saw the same thing. I'll ask a colleague to watch the next meeting and tell me what they notice. |

**Why:** Self-views are least reliable on flattering or unflattering traits, so treating others' observations as evidence rather than an attack is how the maxim is actually followed.

Ask yourself:
- What would someone who knows me well say about me here that I would rather not hear?
- Am I judging myself by my intentions while others can only see my behaviour?
- Have I mistaken a confident story about my motives for real knowledge of them?

### [Self-distancing](../articles/self-knowledge/self-distancing.md) — Practice

1. Notice the signs that you are immersed in an upsetting experience, such as replaying the same scene, rehearsing the same sentence or feeling the original anger or shame rise again.
2. Shift your vantage point by picturing the scene from across the room, as a neutral observer would see it, with yourself as one of the people in it.
3. Talk to yourself silently using your own name or "you" instead of "I", for example "Why is Sam so stung by this?"
4. Ask what lies behind the feeling and what you might be missing, rather than recounting again what happened.
5. Widen the frame by asking how the other people involved might see it, and how the situation could change over the coming months.
6. Ask what you would advise a friend in exactly this position, then choose one concrete next step.
7. Stop if the distance is turning into a way of avoiding the feeling or the problem, and seek support if the distress persists.

*Late on a Friday, your manager leaves a blunt comment on your project report saying the analysis "misses the point", and you feel your weekend souring.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I can't believe I got this so wrong. I'll keep rereading that comment, write a defensive reply tonight and hit send. | Why is Jordan so rattled by one line? Jordan doesn't yet know what the manager meant by 'the point'. If a friend showed me this, I'd tell them to ask one clarifying question on Monday, not to send a reply at midnight. |

**Why:** Viewing the moment as an observer cools the sting enough to admit what you don't know and to consider the other person's view, which is the kind of reasoning people find easier about a friend's problem than their own.

Ask yourself:
- Am I reliving this from the inside, or looking at it as an observer would?
- Would I give a friend in my position the advice I am about to follow?
- Have I asked what I might not know and how the other person sees this?
- Is this distance helping me understand the feeling, or helping me avoid it?

## virtues-and-character

### [Aristotle's doctrine of the mean](../articles/virtues-and-character/doctrine-of-the-mean.md) — Principle

*A newly promoted team lead has to tell a long-serving colleague that his reports keep arriving late and incomplete.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Either I let it slide to keep the peace, or I lay it all out bluntly in front of the team so nobody can say I'm soft. | I tend to avoid conflict, so I'll lean a little firmer than feels natural: a private conversation, the specific facts, what I need by when, and a real question about what is getting in his way. |

**Why:** The right degree of firmness depends on this person and this problem, and knowing which way you usually err tells you which way to correct, as Aristotle advised.

Ask yourself:
- Which way do I usually err in situations like this one, too much or too little, and am I correcting for it?
- Am I calling a choice "balanced" just because it sits halfway between two options, rather than because it fits the situation?
- Is the strength of my reaction proportionate to how serious this actually is?

### [Intellectual humility](../articles/virtues-and-character/intellectual-humility.md) — Virtue

*In a project review, a colleague points out that the sales forecast you built last month rests on an assumption you never checked.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The model is fine. The market just shifted; nobody could have seen that coming. | You're right, I didn't test that assumption. Let's rerun it with your figure and see how much the forecast moves. Thanks for catching it. |

**Why:** Owning the gap, rather than blaming circumstances or brushing it off, fixes the forecast, shows the team that errors can be raised safely, and treats the critic as an ally in getting it right.

Ask yourself:
- Which part of my view have I actually checked, and which part am I only sure of?
- What evidence or argument would change my mind here, and have I gone looking for it?
- When I was shown a mistake just now, did I feel curiosity or did I feel attacked?
- Am I underselling a strength I really do have, out of false modesty or fear of conflict?

## wisdom-at-work

### [Hubris](../articles/wisdom-at-work/hubris.md) — Vice

*A department head has delivered three strong years in a row and been praised in the trade press. A senior analyst warns that a proposed expansion rests on optimistic demand figures.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "I've called it right every time so far, and the numbers will catch up with the vision. We're not slowing down for a spreadsheet." The analyst stops raising concerns, and the plan goes ahead unchanged. | "My recent wins are a reason to check myself, not to skip the check. Walk me through your demand figures, and let's have finance stress-test both versions before the board sees either." The plan is approved a quarter later at a smaller scale, with a review point. |

**Why:** Recent success and praise are exactly the conditions under which leaders overpay and overreach; deliberately inviting the dissenting case and an independent check restores the feedback that power tends to cut off.

Ask yourself:
- Am I dismissing this objection because of its evidence, or because of who raised it and how well things have gone for me lately?
- When did someone last change my mind on something important, and did I make it easy or costly for them?
- Who, if anyone, can still stop me from making this decision, and have I given them what they need to do so?
- Am I treating my organisation's success as proof of my own judgment?

### [Humble leadership](../articles/wisdom-at-work/humble-leadership.md) — Practice

1. When you get something wrong or don't know the answer, say so plainly and early, and say what you will do about it.
2. Name specific contributions by specific people, in their hearing and when reporting upward; say "we" for shared wins and "I" for your own errors.
3. Ask the team for feedback on one concrete thing you did, listen without defending, and tell them later what you changed.
4. Let people with more expertise on a task teach you, in front of others if possible.
5. Keep making clear decisions; in a crisis or under tight deadlines, act first and invite reflection afterwards.
6. Check that the humility is believed, not read as a tactic, by keeping your private conduct consistent with what you say in meetings.

*A project lead's schedule for a product launch slips by three weeks, partly because she under-estimated the testing work, and an analyst on the team had flagged the risk early.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The vendors let us down and testing turned out harder than anyone could have predicted. We'll push harder. | I under-estimated testing, and Priya flagged it in March; I should have listened. Here's the revised plan. Priya, can you walk me through how you sized it so I get it right next time? |

**Why:** Owning the error, crediting the person who saw it and asking to learn from her makes it safe for others to raise risks early, and it fixes the estimating method rather than just the date.

Ask yourself:
- When did I last tell my team, in so many words, that I was wrong about something?
- Who did the real work on this result, and have the people above me heard their names?
- Am I asking for input because I will use it, or to look open?
- Does this situation call for listening and learning, or for a fast, clear decision that I can review with the team later?
