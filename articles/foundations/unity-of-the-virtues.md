# Unity of the virtues

The unity of the virtues is the old claim that you cannot have one virtue of character in the full sense while lacking the rest. Its best-known form, from Aristotle, is a reciprocity thesis: no one is good in the strict sense without practical wisdom, and no one is practically wise without moral virtue, so once practical wisdom is present all the virtues come with it (*Nicomachean Ethics* VI.13).[^s1] Philosophers today usually distinguish this "standard" unity, where having one virtue implies having all the others, from a stronger claim that the virtues are in some sense one and the same thing.[^s5]

## Origin

The question goes back to Socrates. Aristotle reports that Socrates thought all the virtues were forms of knowledge; Aristotle judged him wrong about that but right that every virtue involves practical wisdom (VI.13).[^s1] Aristotle then met the obvious objection that people are naturally better suited to some virtues than others, so they might acquire one without the rest. He granted this for "natural" virtues, the good dispositions some people have from birth, but denied it for virtue in the strict sense, which needs reason and choice and therefore practical wisdom (VI.13).[^s1] In the preceding chapter he separated practical wisdom from cleverness, the knack of reaching whatever aim one sets, good or bad (VI.12).[^s1] The Stanford Encyclopedia sums up the division of labour: character makes the goal right, practical wisdom finds the way to it, and ethical virtue is complete only when the two are combined.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The thesis explains a familiar experience: a trait we admire can go wrong. Contemporary virtue ethicists note that someone can be generous, honest, compassionate and brave and still not be a good person, because each trait can misfire without judgment.[^s3] Ordinary good dispositions are, in one summary, "a proto version of full virtue awaiting perfection by phronesis or practical wisdom".[^s3] Read practically, the unity thesis warns against building your self-image on a single strength. Honesty without kindness becomes cruelty; loyalty without justice becomes complicity; courage without judgment becomes recklessness. The same judgment that steers one virtue is the one that resolves apparent clashes between virtues, which on this view only a practically wise person can see through.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Treat each virtue you are proud of as incomplete until it has been checked by the others. Before acting on it, ask what fairness, kindness, temperance and courage would add, and whether your skill at getting your way is doing the job that judgment should do.[^s1][^s3] When two virtues seem to conflict, such as candour and compassion, look first for a response that satisfies both, since the classical view holds that the conflict is usually only apparent to someone with enough understanding of the case.[^s3] And be honest about where your judgment is weaker: modern versions of the thesis allow that a person can be wise and good at work yet much less so at home.[^s5]

## Common misreadings

The thesis does not say that a person with one good trait is a saint, or that anyone who lacks one virtue has none. Aristotle allows natural virtues one at a time; only full virtue is unified.[^s1] Nor does it require that all the virtues arrive at once. Christopher Toner, defending a full unity thesis, answers exactly that objection and stresses that he aims to show the view remains defensible, not to prove it.[^s4] It is also not the claim that the virtues are identical; that stronger thesis is a separate position, though Alan Wilson argues it has merit for the intellectual virtues.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The unity of the virtues is a philosophical thesis judged by argument, and philosophers disagree about its strength. Neera Badhwar's "limited unity" view holds that practical wisdom is not all or nothing, so a virtue in one domain implies the other virtues only within that domain; others accept a weak version in which one virtue implies only decency elsewhere.[^s5][^s4] Toner argues that the main arguments for limited or weak unity fail.[^s4] Situationist philosophers drawing on social psychology go further, arguing that robust, consistent character traits are rare or absent; defenders reply that virtues are demanding multi-track dispositions that such studies do not test.[^s3]

Psychology offers only indirect evidence. In a study of 1,241 German-speaking adults, the six "core virtues" of the VIA classification correlated positively but weakly and largely functioned independently, though people with at least one strength in every virtue cluster were rated higher in good character, with small to medium effects.[^s6] The study used new self- and informant-report scales, a single culture and the VIA's list rather than Aristotle's, and it did not test practical wisdom as an integrating capacity, so it bears on trait co-occurrence rather than on the thesis itself.[^s6]

## Connections

The thesis is **rooted in Aristotelian virtue ethics** and sits under **virtue ethics** more broadly.[^s2][^s3] It is **explained by practical wisdom**, the capacity every full virtue requires, and it was **influenced by Socratic philosophy**, whose claim that the virtues are knowledge Aristotle corrected (*NE* VI.13).[^s1] It contrasts **cleverness**, which serves any goal, with the judgment that unites the **moral virtues** (VI.12).[^s1][^s2] Badhwar's **limited unity of virtue** is the main **alternative** in recent philosophy.[^s5] It is **challenged by situationism**, which doubts robust traits, and, weakly and indirectly, by the **study of character strength co-occurrence**, which found the VIA virtues only loosely linked.[^s3][^s6] Wilson extends the debate to the **intellectual virtues**.[^s5]
