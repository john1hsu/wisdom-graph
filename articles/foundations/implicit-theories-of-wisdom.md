# Implicit theories of wisdom

Implicit theories of wisdom are the working pictures of wisdom that people hold without ever having been taught a definition: what a wise person does, who counts as one, and how anyone gets there. Psychologists call them *implicit* because they are intuitive and people often cannot say where they came from, and they set them against *explicit* theories, the formal models researchers build and test, such as the Berlin wisdom paradigm.[^s4] Studying these folk theories is one of the main routes by which modern psychology has tried to answer the old question of what wisdom is.[^s6]

## Origin

An early study by Clayton and Birren (1980) asked 83 people to rate how similar various descriptors were to the word "wise"; the answers grouped into cognitive, reflective and affective qualities, a three-part picture that later shaped Monika Ardelt's three-dimensional model.[^s4][^s5] In 1985 Robert Sternberg asked professors of art, business, philosophy and physics, and laypeople, to list and rate the behaviours of an ideally intelligent, creative or wise person.[^s1] Sorting those behaviours produced six facets of wisdom: reasoning ability, sagacity, learning from ideas and the environment, judgment, quick use of information, and perspicacity.[^s1] Research since then has used three broad methods (rating lists of descriptors, studying real wise people, and experiments) to ask what wisdom is, who is wise, when wisdom shows itself and how it develops.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Folk theories are not idle opinions; people use them. In Sternberg's studies, adults rated simulated letters of recommendation, and their judgments of how wise each candidate was tracked the prototype of wisdom that other people had described.[^s1] So whenever we choose a mentor, back a leader or decide whose advice to take, a mostly unexamined theory of wisdom is doing the choosing. Lay pictures also help researchers find the common core of the concept: reviews of Western and Eastern definitions keep returning to knowledge, understanding, and connecting with and considering other people.[^s4]

## What people think wisdom is

Most lay conceptions agree that wisdom has a cognitive core: life knowledge, experience and the ability to see complexity.[^s2] They differ over how much the heart counts. In a German magazine survey of 1,955 readers, Judith Glück and Susan Bluck found that about 38 percent held a *cognitive* conception of wisdom, while 62 percent held an *integrative* one that added empathy, benevolence and self-reflection.[^s2] The integrative view was far more common among people over 30 than among teenagers and people in their twenties, and both groups believed that wisdom grows mainly from a wide range of good and bad experiences and from learning from wise people, rather than from simply getting older.[^s2]

Culture shapes the picture too. Asked to compare descriptors, American and Australian participants grouped "wise" with "experienced" and "knowledgeable", whereas Indian and Japanese participants linked it with "discreet"; the authors read this as a Western emphasis on cognition and an Eastern one on cognition and affect together.[^s3] Studies of Taiwanese, Japanese, Korean and mainland Chinese samples have found further factors, such as modesty and unobtrusiveness, benevolence, and a spiritual or disengaged side of wisdom.[^s4][^s5] Asked to name wise figures, North Americans pointed to practical, philosophical and benevolent prototypes, and political leaders are often among the exemplars people nominate.[^s5]

## How to use the idea

Treat your own implicit theory as something to examine. Notice whom you instinctively nominate as wise and why; Sternberg's raters linked wisdom closely to intelligence, so it is easy to mistake a quick, articulate person for a wise one.[^s1] Before relying on someone's counsel, ask whether they show the qualities that recur across cultures, such as understanding of people and real consideration for them, and not only expertise.[^s4] When colleagues or family disagree about what the wise course is, it can help to ask whether one side is weighing knowledge and the other compassion; Glück and Bluck suggest treating these as differently weighted parts of one whole rather than as rival definitions.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The core findings recur across methods, but much of the evidence rests on small, educated or university-based samples, including the original Clayton and Birren study and the magazine readers in Glück and Bluck.[^s4][^s2] The popular "analytic West, holistic East" contrast is a broad average: Glück and Bluck found large differences within a single Western country, and researchers warn that Western-derived models may not fit other cultures well.[^s2][^s5] Eastern folk conceptions have also been studied far less than Western ones.[^s4] Finally, a folk theory describes what people *believe* wisdom is, not who actually reasons or acts wisely, so it is a starting point for the science rather than a measure of it.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

Implicit theories are a central topic in the **psychology of wisdom**, which treats them as one of its main sources of evidence.[^s6] They are **demonstrated by** Sternberg's 1985 studies of intelligence, creativity and wisdom,[^s1] by Glück and Bluck's study of cognitive and integrative conceptions,[^s2] and by Takahashi and Bordia's cross-cultural comparison.[^s3] They **contrast with** explicit expert models such as the **Berlin wisdom paradigm**, which start from theory and expert opinion rather than lay intuition; the cognitive and integrative lay views roughly mirror the divide between cognition-focused models like Berlin's and integrative ones like the **three-dimensional wisdom model**.[^s4][^s2] Sternberg, who helped launch the study of folk theories, later proposed an explicit model of his own, the **balance theory of wisdom**.[^s1][^s4] Because lay theories decide whose advice we trust, they bear directly on **seeking counsel**.[^s1]
