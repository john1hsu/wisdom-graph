# Socratic ignorance

Socratic ignorance is the idea that the first step toward wisdom is recognising what you do not know. In Plato's *Apology*, Socrates concludes that he is wiser than a man who thought himself wise only in this small respect: neither of them knows anything truly fine and good, but the other man thinks he does, while Socrates does not think he knows what he does not know.[^s1] Socrates contrasts this modest insight, the most a human can claim, with the wisdom of the god.[^s1]

## Origin

The teaching comes from Plato's account of Socrates' defence speech at his trial in Athens in 399 BCE. Socrates tells the jury that his friend Chaerephon asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and the priestess answered that no one was.[^s1] Puzzled, Socrates tested the oracle by questioning people reputed to be wise: a politician, poets and craftsmen.[^s1] The politician thought himself wise but was not; the poets could not explain their own works; and the craftsmen, who really did know many useful things, assumed that this skill also made them experts on the highest matters.[^s1] Socrates concluded that the oracle was using him as an example: human wisdom is worth little or nothing, and the wisest person is the one who, like Socrates, recognises this.[^s1]

Our picture of Socrates comes almost entirely from others (Plato, Xenophon and the comic playwright Aristophanes), and separating the historical man from these portraits is the long-standing "Socratic problem".[^s2] Socrates' habit of professing to know nothing important while steering every conversation was part of his famous, much-debated irony.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Socrates' tests point to a pattern that is easy to recognise today: expertise in one area quietly inflating into confidence about everything.[^s1] He also applied the principle to fear of death, arguing that dreading death pretends to a knowledge no one has, since nobody knows whether death is a harm.[^s1] The point is practical: the same speech insists that an unexamined life is not worth living.[^s1]

Modern wisdom research gives the idea an empirical counterpart. Psychologists list intellectual humility, including awareness of the limits of one's knowledge, as a core feature of wise reasoning, alongside considering wider perspectives and seeking compromise.[^s4] A 2022 review defines intellectual humility by the same metacognitive core: recognising the limits of one's knowledge and being aware that one can be wrong.[^s3] People higher in it tend to be more motivated to learn, better at telling strong arguments from weak ones (including arguments against their own views), more tolerant of opposing views and more forgiving after conflict.[^s3]

## How to apply it

It is a stance rather than a technique, but it can be practised:

- **Sort knowing from feeling sure.** Before you assert something that matters, ask what you have actually checked and what you are filling in.[^s1]
- **Notice expertise drift.** Like Socrates' craftsmen, be wary when skill in your own field makes you feel entitled to opinions outside it.[^s1]
- **Let questions test you.** Socrates' method was question and answer, not lecturing; inviting others to probe your reasoning is a way of finding where it runs out.[^s2]
- **Step back.** Reflecting on a problem as an outside observer would, or as if advising a friend, increases this kind of humble reasoning in studies, and self-distanced reflection is the best-supported intervention in the intellectual-humility literature.[^s3][^s4]

At work, this looks like saying "I don't know yet, here's how we'll find out" in a meeting. In relationships, it looks like holding your account of a conflict loosely enough to hear the other person's.[^s3][^s4]

## Common misreadings

- **"I know that I know nothing."** This famous line is a later paraphrase, not a sentence in the *Apology*. There, Socrates' claim is narrower: he does not think he knows what he does not know, especially about what is fine and good.[^s1] In the same speech he says plainly that he does know that wrongdoing and disobeying one who is better are evil and shameful, so he was not claiming to know nothing at all.[^s1] What exactly he claimed, and whether it was sincere or ironic, remains a live scholarly debate.[^s2]
- **Ignorance as a virtue.** The teaching does not praise not knowing; it praises not pretending to know. Socrates' response to his ignorance was relentless inquiry.[^s1][^s2]
- **False modesty.** Performing self-deprecation while refusing to change your mind is the opposite of the point; the test is whether you update when shown you are wrong.[^s3]
- **Paralysis.** Admitting uncertainty is not the same as refusing to act. The review cautions that intellectual humility may not help in every context, for example when decisions must be made urgently.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

As a philosophical teaching, Socratic ignorance is judged by argument and long practice, so its status here is normative.[^s1][^s2] The empirical work on intellectual humility supports the spirit of the idea but is not a test of the Socratic teaching itself.[^s3] Most findings are cross-sectional, so it is unclear how much intellectual humility causes better learning and relationships rather than merely accompanying them; self-report measures are prone to bias; and apart from self-distancing, most interventions have small, unreplicated samples or mixed results.[^s3]

## Connections

Socratic ignorance was **introduced by Socrates**, whose views reach us mainly through Plato,[^s2] and is set out in the **Apology of Socrates**.[^s1] It is **rooted in Socratic philosophy**, the question-and-answer **Socratic method** of examining others' claims about courage, piety and the good life.[^s2] It **develops intellectual humility**, which modern psychology defines by the same recognition of the limits of one's knowledge.[^s3] It is a core ingredient of **wise reasoning** and can be strengthened through **self-distancing**.[^s4] Because it begins with an honest look at one's own understanding, it is closely related to **know thyself** and to the self-examination Socrates urged on his jurors, and it offers a starting point for any account of **wisdom**.[^s1]
