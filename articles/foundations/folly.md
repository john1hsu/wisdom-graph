# Folly

Folly, or foolishness, is the practical opposite of wisdom: acting as if one knows more, controls more and is safer from consequences than is really the case, usually while discounting everyone else's interests.[^s4][^s3] It is not simply a lack of brains. Intelligence, rationality and wisdom are distinct, so a very able person can still act very foolishly.[^s3][^s7]

## Origin

In Plato's *Philebus* (48c–49c), Socrates calls the ridiculous a kind of vice, the opposite of the Delphic command to know oneself. People are ridiculous when they think themselves richer, better-looking or, most often, wiser and more virtuous than they are. When such self-ignorance is joined to power it stops being comic and becomes hateful and dangerous to others.[^s1] In Hebrew wisdom literature, above all Proverbs, the fool (*kesil*, *ewil*) is the counterpart of the wise person. Folly there is chiefly a moral and religious failing rather than a shortfall of intellect. The fool is described as conceited, quarrelsome, quick to anger and a hater of knowledge, and folly is said to harm others and lead to ruin. The related word *nabal*, often translated "fool", is closer to a scoundrel.[^s8]

In modern psychology, Robert Sternberg made foolishness a topic of its own. He argued that smart, successful people are especially susceptible because they come to believe their intelligence exempts them from ordinary mistakes.[^s4][^s5] Balazs Aczel's chapter in *The Cambridge Handbook of Wisdom* treats foolishness as "low levels of wisdom" and distinguishes two ways of studying it: as a departure from norms of logic and probability, and as whatever ordinary people perceive as foolish.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Because folly is not the same as low ability, intelligence and training are no guarantee against it. Stanovich and West report that many classic reasoning errors, including myside bias, framing effects and neglect of base rates, are largely independent of measured intelligence, and that correlations with intelligence on other reasoning tasks are only modest.[^s7] Sternberg's point is sharper: the very success that intelligence brings feeds the illusions behind foolish choices.[^s4][^s5] Plato had already warned that self-ignorance joined to power harms others.[^s1]

## How to spot it

Sternberg names a set of fallacies that mark foolish thinking. Unrealistic optimism assumes a plan will work because one is clever enough. Egocentrism weighs only one's own interests. Omniscience assumes that expertise in one area covers everything. Omnipotence assumes one can do whatever one wants. Invulnerability assumes one can escape the consequences.[^s4][^s5]

Ordinary judgments of foolishness point in the same direction. Aczel and colleagues collected 180 news stories and student diaries of "stupid" acts and had 154 Hungarian undergraduates rate them. Three kinds of situation emerged.[^s2][^s6] The most harshly judged was *confident ignorance*: taking a risky action without the knowledge or skill it needs. Next came lack of control, such as obsessive or compulsive behaviour. The most forgivable was absent-mindedness or impracticality.[^s6] Acts were judged more foolish when the person was more responsible for them and the consequences were more serious.[^s2]

Everyday warning signs follow: confidence running ahead of competence, other people's costs dropping out of view, a run of success treated as proof the next move is safe, and correction brushed aside, which Proverbs treats as the fool's mark.[^s4][^s2][^s8]

## How to fix it

- **Match confidence to competence.** Before a consequential act, ask what knowledge or skill it requires and whether you actually have it. Confident ignorance is the pattern people find most foolish.[^s2][^s6]
- **Widen the circle of interests and the time horizon.** Sternberg's remedy is wisdom as balance: weigh your own, other people's and wider interests over the short and the long term, rather than optimising for yourself now.[^s4][^s5]
- **Practise taking other points of view.** Sternberg argues that considering other perspectives can be taught within ordinary school subjects, and that knowledge and intelligence alone are not enough.[^s5]
- **Know the limits of your own knowledge.** Plato presents the ridiculous as the opposite of the Delphic command to know oneself, so the corresponding remedy is an honest look at how wise and virtuous you really are.[^s1]
- **Welcome correction.** In the wisdom literature the fool hates knowledge and is conceited and quick to anger. Treating a dissenting question as information is the reverse of that habit.[^s8]
- **Learn specific reasoning tools rather than trusting raw intelligence.** Knowledge such as how base rates work protects judgment in ways that intelligence alone does not.[^s7]

## Evidence and limits

The claim that intelligence and good judgment come apart has wide support. Many bias effects show little relation to cognitive ability.[^s7] The study of foolishness itself is much thinner. Sternberg's fallacies are an argument built from cases and theory, not an experimentally validated taxonomy.[^s4] The folk-perception study drew its ratings from a single group of Hungarian students, used a small diary sample and included some deliberately altered stories, so its generality is unknown.[^s6] Aczel's chapter calls for more work on where foolishness arises and how to educate wisdom against it; tested ways of reducing folly are still lacking.[^s3] The word also shifts meaning: self-ignorance in Plato, a moral failing in Proverbs, cognitive error in modern psychology.[^s1][^s8][^s4]

## Connections

Folly **contrasts with** wisdom: the Cambridge handbook frames it as wisdom at its lowest levels.[^s3] It is often **confused with** low general intelligence, but the evidence on rational thinking shows the two are largely separate.[^s7] In the *Philebus* it **contrasts with** *know thyself*, the Delphic maxim of which ridiculous self-ignorance is the opposite.[^s1] Sternberg's account **explains** it by the **balance theory of wisdom**: folly is what happens when intelligence serves only one's own short-term interests.[^s4] People's ordinary understanding of it is **demonstrated by** Aczel, Palfi and Kekecs's study of what people call stupid.[^s2] As a moral failing contrasted with the wise, it is **rooted in** **Hebrew wisdom literature**.[^s8] Related nodes: **hubris** (its form in the powerful), **Socratic ignorance** and **intellectual humility** (counters to the illusion of knowing), **wise reasoning** and **practical wisdom** (what it lacks), **Robert Sternberg** (its main modern theorist) and **seeking counsel** (the correction it resists).[^s1][^s4][^s5]
