# Wisdom versus intelligence

Intelligence is the capacity to reason, learn and solve problems quickly and accurately, the kind of ability that IQ tests sample through puzzles, vocabulary and processing speed. Wisdom is the use of knowledge and judgment to deal with hard, uncertain problems of life and conduct, usually ones that involve other people and competing interests.[^s2][^s3] The two overlap, and people often run them together, but the research of the past four decades finds that they are separate qualities: being smart helps a person become wise without making them wise.[^s2][^s4][^s7]

## Origin

The modern question began with how ordinary people think. In 1985 Robert Sternberg asked laypeople to rate how typical various behaviours were of an ideal intelligent, creative or wise person. The prototypes of the wise and the intelligent person were highly similar, correlating at .75, far more than either was with creativity, and raters found the two hardest to tell apart.[^s1] Yet wisdom carried features absent from intelligence: listening to others and weighing their advice, dealing well with many kinds of people, learning from other people's mistakes, and making fair, sensible judgments with an eye on the long term.[^s1]

Researchers then asked whether measured intelligence predicts measured wisdom. The Berlin group's answer was that wisdom-related performance, judged from how people think aloud about difficult life problems, is "not simply another variant of intelligence or personality."[^s2]

## Why it matters

If wisdom were simply high intelligence, the cleverest person in the room would be the best adviser, leader or friend in a crisis. The evidence says otherwise, and so does experience: bright people make foolish decisions, and some people of ordinary ability show remarkable judgment.[^s6][^s2] Keeping them apart changes whom we consult and promote, and how far we trust our own reasoning when it feels most brilliant.

The distinction also matters for ageing. Fluid intelligence, the speed and flexibility of reasoning, declines across adulthood, but wisdom-related performance did not follow it downward between roughly 25 and 75 in the Berlin studies, and in a sample of healthy adults averaging 76 a wisdom scale showed almost no link to processing speed.[^s2][^s5]

## What the evidence shows

**Intelligence explains a small slice of wisdom.** In Staudinger, Lopez and Baltes's study, summarised by Baltes and Staudinger, 33 indicators from 14 tests of intelligence, personality and their interface were used to predict wisdom-related performance. Intelligence on its own accounted for only about 2 percent of unique variance, the same as personality, while measures sitting between the two, such as creativity and thinking style, uniquely explained about 15 percent. No single indicator explained more than 18 percent.[^s2]

**Knowledge is a floor, not a ladder.** Judith Glück and Andreas Scherpf tested whether intelligence is necessary but not sufficient for wisdom in 318 people of three age groups and 155 adults across the lifespan. The relationships were triangular: people low in crystallised intelligence, accumulated knowledge and vocabulary, rarely scored high on wisdom tasks, but many people with high crystallised intelligence were not wise. Fluid intelligence mattered only below a threshold, and mainly in the young; with self-report wisdom scales it showed no relationship at all.[^s4]

**In later life the two come apart.** Lindbergh and colleagues, in a preregistered study of 141 healthy older adults, found that fluid intelligence explained less than 5 percent of the variance in self-reported wisdom.[^s5]

**They predict different outcomes.** In Grossmann and colleagues' study of 241 Michigan adults, wise reasoning about social conflicts predicted less negative feeling, better relationships and less depressive brooding, while tests of processing speed, memory span, comprehension and vocabulary did not predict well-being.[^s3]

**Rationality is a third thing.** Keith Stanovich and Richard West argue that IQ tests also miss rational thinking: myside bias is virtually unrelated to intelligence, and many reasoning tasks correlate with it only modestly.[^s6]

## How to use the distinction

- Treat expertise and quick reasoning as inputs to judgment, not proof of it. Ask whether a plan has been tested against other perspectives, uncertainty and long-term effects on the people involved.[^s1][^s3]
- When you seek counsel on a human problem, look for the marks laypeople attach to wisdom, listening, fairness and learning from others' errors, as well as for subject knowledge.[^s1]
- Keep learning. Since accumulated knowledge appears to be a necessary floor, broad experience and reading still matter; they just do not finish the job.[^s4]
- Bring different strengths together on big problems. Glück argues that intelligence, creativity and wisdom develop differently, are unevenly distributed across people, and are all needed for complex real-world problems.[^s7]

## Common misreadings

- *"Wise people are not intelligent."* The research supports overlap, especially with crystallised knowledge, not opposition.[^s1][^s4]
- *"Intelligence is irrelevant to wisdom."* Low knowledge seems to cap wisdom-related performance; it is high intelligence that guarantees little.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The findings agree in direction but are limited in design. Most studies are cross-sectional, use modest samples from a few Western countries, and measure wisdom in different ways; open-ended performance tasks and self-report scales do not behave the same way with intelligence.[^s4][^s3] The variance figures rest on the Berlin group's own measure, and Grossmann's findings are correlational, so they cannot show that wise reasoning causes better lives.[^s2][^s3] The evidence status is therefore mixed: the separation of wisdom from intelligence is well supported, but how much intelligence wisdom needs, and at which ages, is still being worked out.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

The distinction is **studied in the psychology of wisdom**, where the Berlin group's **psychometric location of wisdom study demonstrates** how little unique variance intelligence explains.[^s2] Sternberg's **implicit theories study supports** it from lay conceptions, and connects it to **implicit theories of wisdom**.[^s1] Glück and Scherpf's **nonlinear study**, Lindbergh and colleagues' **wisdom and fluid intelligence study** (see **wisdom and age**) and Grossmann's **route to well-being study** (see **wisdom and well-being** and **wise reasoning**) each **demonstrate** part of the split.[^s4][^s5][^s3] It **requires** the concept of **wisdom** and sits beside **general intelligence**, **cleverness**, **folly** (the bright person's characteristic failure), **intellectual humility**, **practical wisdom**, the **Berlin wisdom paradigm** and Sternberg's **balance theory of wisdom**.[^s2][^s6]
