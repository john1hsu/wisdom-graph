# Virtue ethics

Virtue ethics is one of the three main approaches in normative ethics, alongside deontology, which starts from duties and rules, and consequentialism, which starts from outcomes.[^s1] Every serious theory says something about character; what sets this one apart is that virtue and vice are its foundation rather than being defined through some other notion.[^s1] Faced with someone in need, a utilitarian points to the good that helping will do and a deontologist to a moral rule, while a virtue ethicist points to what charity or kindness asks of us.[^s1] Its central question is less "Which act is right?" than "How should I live, and what kind of person should I become?"[^s2]

## Origin

The approach goes back to Plato and Aristotle in the West and to Confucius and Mencius in China.[^s1] It dominated Western moral philosophy until at least the Enlightenment, faded in the nineteenth century, and returned with Elizabeth Anscombe's 1958 essay "Modern Moral Philosophy", which argued that the dominant theories of duty and utility had neglected character, moral education, friendship, happiness and the emotions.[^s1][^s2] Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre, Bernard Williams and Rosalind Hursthouse developed it further.[^s2]

Three ancient Greek ideas organise most versions: *aretē* (excellence or virtue), *phronesis* (practical wisdom) and *eudaimonia* (flourishing).[^s1] Aristotle held that moral virtue grows from habit, so that we become just by doing just acts, as builders become builders by building; he defined virtue as a settled state concerned with choice, lying in a mean fixed by reason as a person of practical wisdom would fix it.[^s3]

## Why it matters

In virtue ethics, wisdom is load-bearing. On Aristotle's account, good intentions are only *natural virtue*, a promising start that stays incomplete until practical wisdom supplies the experience to see which features of a situation matter most.[^s1] Even deontologists now concede that rules cannot apply themselves, so this judgment is needed whichever theory you hold.[^s1]

A virtue is a deep, multi-track disposition that shapes what you notice, value and feel as well as what you do; an honest person turns down a dishonest option because it is dishonest, not merely because it is risky.[^s1] Virtue comes in degrees, and even fairly good people have blind spots.[^s1]

## Using it in everyday decisions

Virtue ethics offers no algorithm, since many defenders think ethics too varied to codify, but it does guide action.[^s2] The simplest form is to ask what a good and wise person would do in your situation, and to learn from people you admire.[^s2] A second is what the literature calls *v-rules*: do what is honest, kind and fair; avoid what is dishonest, cruel or petty.[^s1] Because the vocabulary of vices is rich, naming the vice an option would express (spiteful, cowardly, arrogant) is often the quickest test.[^s1] A third is habituation: since character is built by repeated action, small honest or patient acts now shape the person who will face the bigger choices later.[^s3][^s2]

At work, this shifts the question from "Is this allowed?" to "Is this what a fair and candid colleague would do?" In relationships, it explains why kindness that comes from duty alone can feel hollow to the person receiving it, a problem the literature calls self-effacement.[^s1] Current work extends it to Confucian, Buddhist and Hindu ethics and to business and medical ethics.[^s1]

## Common misreadings

One misreading is that virtue ethics is selfish because it centres on the agent's own flourishing. Defenders reply that virtues such as justice and compassion are other-regarding, and that virtuous people act for reasons such as relieving suffering, not self-interest.[^s1][^s2] A second is that it is hopelessly relative because cultures prize different traits; defenders answer that rules and ideas of happiness vary just as much, and some, following Martha Nussbaum, argue that the core virtues are not culturally relative even when local understandings differ.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Virtue ethics is judged by argument and long practice rather than experiment, but it assumes something testable: that people have stable traits of character. The situationist philosophers John Doris and Gilbert Harman argued that social psychology undermines this.[^s4] They cited studies such as Isen and Levin's experiment, in which 88% of people who had just found a dime helped a stranger pick up dropped papers against 4% of others, and Milgram's obedience studies.[^s4] Some of this evidence has weakened: the dime study had replication problems, and a later study of CCTV footage found that at least one bystander intervened in about nine in ten real aggressive incidents.[^s4] Defenders add that Aristotle always held full virtue to be rare, and that the traits tested were not the demanding, wisdom-guided dispositions virtue ethics describes.[^s4] Others, such as Christian Miller, conclude that most people have *mixed* traits, partly good and partly bad.[^s4]

Newer field data give modest support to the idea that moral behaviour is consistent. Bollich and colleagues, with Doris as a co-author, recorded snippets of 186 people's everyday conversation and found that individual differences in behaviours such as gratitude, affection and criticism were at least as stable over weeks as their ordinary language habits.[^s5] The authors caution that this does not show consistency across very different situations, and that stable behaviour could reflect stable surroundings.[^s5] Character, in short, looks real but uneven, which fits virtue ethics' emphasis on lifelong practice.

## Connections

Virtue ethics was shaped above all by **Aristotle**, and its modern revival was **influenced by Elizabeth Anscombe**.[^s1] It **contrasts with deontology** and **contrasts with consequentialism**, its rivals within **normative ethics**.[^s1] It is **challenged by situationism**,[^s4] and is modestly **supported by the "Eavesdropping on character" study** of everyday moral behaviour.[^s5] **Practical wisdom** and **eudaimonia** are its central concepts, and **deliberation** is how practical wisdom works out what to do.[^s1][^s3] Its best-known form is **Aristotelian virtue ethics**, whose **doctrine of the mean** places each virtue between two vices;[^s3] **Stoicism** is a version that holds virtue sufficient for flourishing,[^s1] and **Confucianism** is one of its oldest roots.[^s1] The **cardinal virtues** and **intellectual humility** are among the traits it studies.
