# Personal and general wisdom

Psychologist Ursula Staudinger and colleagues separate two things that the single word "wisdom" tends to blur. **General wisdom** is insight into life and people in general, the kind of judgment you show when you think through a stranger's dilemma or advise a friend. **Personal wisdom** is insight into your own life: how well you understand yourself, your history and your blind spots, and how you handle your own problems.[^s2] Staudinger calls this a *meta-conception*: rather than a rival theory of what wisdom is, it is a lens that can be applied to any existing theory, by asking whether a given measure or claim concerns the first person or the third.[^s4]

## Origin

Staudinger dates the idea of personal or self-related wisdom to the late 1990s, when she and her colleagues tried to join research on the self, on how people steer their own development, on personality growth and on wisdom into one psychological account of the art of living.[^s5] It grew out of the Berlin wisdom paradigm, which defines wisdom as expertise in the fundamental pragmatics of life and scores how people reason about fictional characters' problems; Berlin is still regarded as the main established test of general wisdom.[^s1] Mickler and Staudinger's personal-wisdom interview (2008) mirrors it: instead of a stranger's dilemma, people think aloud about themselves as a friend, and trained raters score the answers.[^s6] The five scoring criteria parallel Berlin's: rich self-knowledge, heuristics for growth and self-regulation, interrelating the self (seeing how one's life connects with history and with other people), self-relativism, and tolerance of ambiguity.[^s2] In the first validation, with 161 German adults aged 20 to 80, personal wisdom correlated with general wisdom, cognitive measures and life events, but different variables predicted each kind, which is the pattern a genuine distinction should show.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Folk wisdom has long noticed that it is easier to give wise advice than to take it, and Staudinger argues that the two kinds of wisdom are linked but each carries variance of its own, with different correlates, age trajectories and responses to training.[^s5] Reasoning about your own dilemmas runs under different rules and constraints from reasoning about life in general, because your goals, emotions and self-image are at stake.[^s4] Experiments on Solomon's paradox show the gap directly: across three studies, people reasoned more wisely about a friend's relationship problem than about the same problem as their own, being readier to admit limits to what they knew, look for compromise and consider other views.[^s3]

The distinction also untangles a long-running puzzle about age. Older adults often do better than younger ones on strangers' dilemmas, yet in Grossmann and Kross's study younger (20–40) and older (60–80) adults reasoned equally well, and equally poorly, about conflicts in their own lives; the authors suggest that "with age comes wisdom" may hold mainly for other people's problems.[^s3] In Glück's 2013 comparison of five measures, none showed the wisest scorers to be older than the rest.[^s1]

## Using the distinction

For everyday life, the main lesson is not to assume that skill in one form transfers to the other. Someone who mediates colleagues' disputes well may still handle their own career crisis poorly, and someone with deep self-knowledge may give clumsy advice. One way to borrow general wisdom for personal problems is self-distancing: imagining your situation as a friend's or describing it in the third person. It made reasoning about one's own conflict as wise as reasoning about someone else's in one study and narrowed the gap in another.[^s3] Personal wisdom, in turn, is built from honest reflection on your own experience, the introspective material that self-report measures such as the Self-Assessed Wisdom Scale try to capture.[^s1]

At work, the same leader may show general wisdom in coaching others and lack personal wisdom about how their own ambition is shaping a decision. In relationships, the distinction explains why the friend everyone confides in can still repeat the same pattern in their own marriage, the old observation that wise advice is easier to give than to apply to oneself.[^s5]

## Common misreadings

Staudinger's handbook chapter lists several confusions it set out to clear up: personal wisdom does not simply mean wisdom linked to personality, wisdom that draws on personal experience, wisdom without concern for others, or insights unique to one individual.[^s4] Nor are the two cleanly separate in practice: Glück and colleagues found that even personal-wisdom measures seemed to tap a broader "wisdom syndrome" that includes concern for others, and proposed a third, other-related form centred on empathy.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but modest. Measures of wisdom correlate only weakly with one another, and in Glück's study the Berlin general-wisdom task correlated only about .23 to .30 with self-report measures, which mostly target personal wisdom.[^s1] Mickler and Staudinger's personal-wisdom measure showed essentially zero correlations with indicators of subjective and psychological well-being, so being wise about yourself is not the same as feeling good about your life.[^s2] Staudinger herself notes that most studies never say which form they are measuring, that differences in assessment method can masquerade as differences between the two kinds of wisdom, and that longitudinal studies are still needed to show how they develop together.[^s5] Treat the distinction as a well-motivated research framework rather than a settled map.

## Connections

The concept was introduced and developed by Ursula Staudinger, as her 2013 chapter on its history describes.[^s5] It is demonstrated by Mickler and Staudinger's personal-wisdom performance measure, which predicted different correlates from general wisdom,[^s6] and by Glück and colleagues' comparison of five wisdom measures, whose content analysis found the personal/general split captured real differences between them.[^s1] Grossmann and Kross's study of Solomon's paradox demonstrates the gap between wisdom about others and wisdom about oneself, and shows self-distancing as one way to close it.[^s3] The distinction is a central topic in the psychology of wisdom, with a dedicated chapter in the field's main handbook.[^s4] It sits beside the Berlin wisdom paradigm (the standard general-wisdom test) and the three-dimensional wisdom model, whose reflective dimension taps personal wisdom.[^s1]
