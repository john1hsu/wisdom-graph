# Cleverness

Cleverness, in the sense Aristotle gave the word (*deinotēs*), is the ability to work out and carry out the steps that lead to whatever goal you have set yourself.[^s1][^s3] It is a real excellence of the mind, but a morally neutral one. When the goal is noble, cleverness deserves praise; when the goal is bad, Aristotle says, it is only smartness.[^s1] The concept matters to anyone who wants to be wise because it names the most common counterfeit of wisdom: being very good at getting what you want without ever checking whether it is worth wanting.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Aristotle draws the distinction in Book VI of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, while answering the objection that practical wisdom is useless to people who are already good.[^s1] He replies that virtue makes the goal right and practical wisdom makes right the things that lead to it.[^s3] He then introduces cleverness as the faculty of doing the things that tend towards a chosen mark and hitting it. Practical wisdom is not that faculty, he says, but it cannot exist without it; which is why we call even practically wise people clever.[^s1]

What practical wisdom adds is a correct view of the goal itself, and that depends on character. Aristotle likens practical wisdom to an eye of the soul that does not reach its mature state without virtue, because wickedness distorts our sense of the starting points of action. His conclusion is blunt: it is "impossible to be practically wise without being good".[^s1] Richard Kraut's reading in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* makes the same point from the other side: people of poor character may have exactly the rational skill Aristotle calls cleverness, and their defect lies not in their reasoning but in the training of their passions, which leads them to seek worthless ends.[^s3]

Chapter 13 extends the parallel. As cleverness is to practical wisdom, so natural good dispositions are to full virtue: a generous or brave temperament without reason can do harm, like a strong body that stumbles because it moves without sight.[^s1] In Book VII Aristotle adds that nothing stops a clever person from being weak-willed, whereas the practically wise cannot be. The two capacities are close in their reasoning but differ in their purpose.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The distinction explains a familiar puzzle: why capable, quick, well-informed people so often make choices that harm themselves and others. Their problem-solving is excellent; what is missing is the judgment about which problems to solve and for whose sake.[^s1][^s3] A manager who hits every quarterly target by gaming the metrics, an employee who wins every meeting by out-arguing colleagues, or a partner who is skilled at winning quarrels rather than resolving them all display cleverness that has come loose from a good end.

Robert Sternberg's modern account of foolishness tells a similar story in psychological terms. He argues that smart people who act foolishly typically fall into fallacies of egocentrism, omniscience, omnipotence and invulnerability, and that being rewarded for their intelligence can make them lose sight of their humanity.[^s4] His summary, "Intelligence and knowledge are not enough", is the modern echo of Aristotle's point.[^s4] In a 2017 talk he described people who are bright but lack wisdom as "smart fools", and defined wisdom as using knowledge and skill toward a common good.[^s5]

## Common misreadings

*Cleverness is bad.* Aristotle does not condemn it. Practical wisdom needs it, and when it serves a noble goal it is praiseworthy.[^s1] The aim is not to become less clever but to put cleverness under the direction of good ends.

*Practical wisdom is just cleverness with good intentions.* Kraut notes that working out how to reach a good end is often not purely instrumental: deciding what justice requires in a particular dispute is itself a question about the goal, not only about means.[^s3]

*Clever people are wise people.* Aristotle's account and modern research both separate the two, as the next section shows.[^s1][^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The distinction is a philosophical one, judged by argument and long reflection rather than experiment. Psychology offers indirect support. Reviewing the research, Igor Grossmann reports that wise reasoning about real-life problems shows only a modest positive relationship with crystallised intelligence and is relatively independent of cognitive ability; in one earlier study, standard intelligence and personality measures together accounted for only about 14% of the variance in wisdom-related performance.[^s6] He also notes that wise reasoning varies a great deal across situations and that its link with life satisfaction fluctuates across samples and methods, so the measures are imperfect.[^s6] Sternberg's fallacies of foolishness are a theoretical argument rather than a tested taxonomy, and neither line of research measures Aristotle's concept directly.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Cleverness **contrasts with practical wisdom**, which needs it but adds a right view of the goal, and was **introduced by** **Aristotle** in the ***Nicomachean Ethics***.[^s1] It is **rooted in Aristotelian virtue ethics**, where the reciprocity of character and judgment explains why cleverness alone falls short.[^s3] It is often **confused with wisdom**, the mistake Sternberg traces in smart but foolish people, which makes it a short step from **folly**.[^s4][^s5] It **contrasts with wise reasoning**, which is only modestly related to intelligence and is **studied in the psychology of wisdom**.[^s6] It sits under the broader notion of **intelligence**, alongside **general intelligence** as psychologists measure it.[^s6] It is the non-virtuous partner of the **intellectual virtues** and the **moral virtues**,[^s1] can coexist with **weakness of will**,[^s2] serves **deliberation** without settling its ends,[^s3] and differs from **theoretical wisdom** in aiming at action rather than understanding.[^s1] In leadership, cleverness without good ends is what **wise leadership** guards against.[^s5]
