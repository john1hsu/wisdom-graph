# Practical wisdom

Practical wisdom is the capacity to see what a particular situation calls for and to act well on it, for your own good and the good of others. Aristotle called it *phronesis* and defined it as a reasoned and true state of capacity to act with regard to human goods.[^s2] It is an intellectual virtue that cannot be learned from general rules alone; it develops through practice, along with the skills that apply general understanding to the case in front of you.[^s1] Medieval philosophers translated it as *prudentia*, so older English texts call it prudence: the same virtue under its Latin name.[^s5]

## Origin

The idea is set out in Book VI of Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, which names it as one of five intellectual virtues alongside scientific knowledge, intuitive understanding (*nous*), craft and theoretical wisdom (*sophia*).[^s1] Theoretical wisdom contemplates the things highest by nature, while practical wisdom deals with what can be otherwise, the only things anyone deliberates about, and so must grasp particulars as well as principles.[^s2] His example is Pericles, who could see what was good for himself and for people generally; the young, he noted, can master mathematics yet are rarely practically wise, because only time brings experience.[^s2]

Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas defined prudence as right reason about things to be done, tying it more closely than Aristotle had to fixed principles of natural law that it applies to particular acts.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Practical wisdom is what turns good intentions into good actions. In Aristotle's words, "virtue makes us aim at the right mark, and practical wisdom makes us take the right means."[^s2] Without it, a generous or brave temperament is only *natural virtue*, promising but unreliable; with it, that temperament becomes virtue in the full sense.[^s2] Aristotle went further: one cannot be fully good without practical wisdom, nor practically wise without moral virtue.[^s2]

It is also what rules cannot replace. Psychologist Barry Schwartz argues that institutions that answer failure with ever more detailed rules and incentives erode wisdom: rules remove the chance to build judgment, and incentives can turn a moral question into a financial one.[^s4]

## What good looks like

Kristjánsson and colleagues' Aristotelian Phronesis Model breaks the virtue into four functions.[^s3] *Constitutive*: perceiving the ethically salient features of a situation. *Integrative*: weighing values that pull in different directions, such as honesty and kindness, and blending them or deciding which gives way. *Blueprint*: a working sense of what a flourishing life involves, which gives judgment direction and motive. *Emotional regulation*: bringing feelings into line with an accurate reading of the situation, infusing emotion with reason rather than suppressing it.[^s3]

Schwartz describes the same virtue more simply as *moral will*, wanting to achieve the real aims of an activity, plus *moral skill*, the ability to see what a situation needs and improvise a response.[^s4] His example, drawn from research by Dutton, Wrzesniewski and colleagues, is a hospital custodian who recleaned a room for a grieving father. Nothing in his job description required it; he understood his work as caring for patients and families.[^s4]

It differs from *cleverness*, the knack of reaching whatever goal you set, which is admirable only when the goal is good. Practical wisdom needs cleverness but is not the same thing.[^s2][^s1]

## How to practise it

Because practical wisdom grows from experience with particular cases, it can be cultivated but not memorised.[^s1] Aristotle advised paying attention to the unproven judgments of experienced and older people, because experience has given them an eye for seeing correctly.[^s2] In practice that means seeking counsel from people who have handled situations like yours, and deliberating until you are right about the end, the manner and the timing, which is how Aristotle defines good deliberation.[^s2]

Before applying a rule, ask what it is for and whether following it here serves that purpose. Schwartz argues that rules work best as guides rather than scripts, and that organisations make room for wisdom by keeping rules few and general, giving people discretion and time to act, see results and adjust, and keeping the purpose of the work in view.[^s4] Look for the particulars that make this case unlike the last, and check whether your reasoning serves a good end or only the end you already wanted.[^s1]

## Common misreadings

One misreading, that it is mere shrewdness, is answered by the contrast with cleverness above.[^s2] Another is that it means having no rules; Aristotle treats some prohibitions, such as those against murder and theft, as absolute, and practical wisdom fills the space no rulebook covers.[^s1] A third is that it simply comes with age: experience is necessary, but it builds on a good upbringing that first trains the feelings.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Practical wisdom is a philosophical ideal, judged by argument and long practice rather than experiment, but psychologists have begun to measure it. A pilot study of the Aristotelian Phronesis Model found that its four components loaded on a single higher-order factor, which in turn was related to prosocial behaviour, used as a stand-in for moral action.[^s3] The authors call this interim, needing replication, and concede that no current model explains how the virtue develops or how best to teach it.[^s3] Schwartz calls his case against rules and incentives a plausibility argument built from studies and case reports, not a direct test.[^s4]

## Connections

Practical wisdom is **rooted in Aristotelian virtue ethics**[^s1] and was **introduced by Aristotle** in the ***Nicomachean Ethics***.[^s2] One of the **intellectual virtues**, it **contrasts with theoretical wisdom**, which does not concern human goods, and with **cleverness**, means-end skill without good ends.[^s1][^s2] It is **applied in leading others**, as with Pericles,[^s2] and in **professional work**, where Schwartz argues it must complement rules.[^s4] It is **explained by the Aristotelian Phronesis Model**, which **complements the Common Wisdom Model** and belongs to the **psychology of wisdom**, alongside research on **wise reasoning**; its blueprint aims at **eudaimonia**.[^s3] **Deliberation** is its mark and **seeking counsel** one of its tools.[^s2] It fixes the mean in the **doctrine of the mean**, is central to **virtue ethics** and underlies **wise leadership**;[^s1] **folly** is its failure.
