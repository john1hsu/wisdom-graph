# Wisdom

Wisdom is, broadly, doing well in what one knows and believes about how to live, and acting on it. Philosophers in the analytic tradition agree on that much: the wise person is in good epistemic shape with respect to living well, though they disagree about exactly what this requires.[^s1] Psychologists who study it have converged on a similar core, describing wisdom as the "morally-grounded application of metacognition to reasoning and problem-solving": thinking about one's own thinking, in the service of good ends.[^s3] The other articles in this graph look at its parts and its practice; this one sketches the idea itself.

## Origin

Ancient philosophers already distinguished kinds of wisdom. In Book VI of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle separates theoretical wisdom (*sophia*), scientific knowledge joined to intuitive reason about the highest things, from practical wisdom (*phronesis*), which grasps particulars and comes only with experience.[^s2] Thinkers like Thales and Anaxagoras, he says, know things "remarkable, admirable, difficult, and divine, but useless", which is why they are called wise but not practically wise.[^s2] In Plato's *Apology*, Socrates' claim that his only wisdom lay in not thinking he knew what he did not know has been read as a humility theory of wisdom.[^s1] Plato and Aristotle treat wisdom as the ruling virtue that directs the others, and the Stoics identify virtue itself with the sage's right reason.[^s1] Other traditions link wisdom to related qualities: the *Analects*, the *Daodejing* and the *Zhuangzi* tie it to intellectual humility, and the *Bhagavad Gītā* to mastery of one's own mind.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Wisdom names what knowledge, intelligence and good intentions each lack on their own. In a two-round Delphi survey of 30 international experts, most agreed that wisdom is a rare, uniquely human form of advanced cognitive and emotional development, driven by experience, that can be learned and measured; they distinguished it from intelligence on 46 of 49 items and from spirituality on 31.[^s4] It is the quality people look for in an adviser, a leader or a friend in a hard moment, because it joins understanding of life with judgment about what to do in this case.[^s1][^s3]

## What philosophers disagree about

Current debates, as the Stanford Encyclopedia sets them out, turn on four questions.[^s1]

- **Is humility enough?** A pure humility theory (you are wise to the extent you believe you are not) admits honest beginners and excludes a wise adviser who rightly trusts their own judgment, so few philosophers hold it alone.[^s1]
- **What is wisdom about?** Some include important academic knowledge; others restrict it to living well. Grimm, for example, requires knowing what is good for a life, where one stands relative to it and how to get there.[^s1]
- **Knowledge or reasonable belief?** Sharon Ryan argues that requiring knowledge would make wisdom hostage to the science of one's era, ruling out Socrates or Hypatia, so justified belief should suffice.[^s1]
- **Must the wise live well and be good?** Aristotle and the Stoics say yes; Whitcomb argues that a deeply evil or deeply depressed person could still be wise, and others reply that wisdom includes valuing a good life and seeking help to reach it.[^s1]

## Using the idea

Treat wisdom as a way of handling hard questions rather than a stock of answers. The psychological consensus model points to two habits: monitor the limits of your own knowledge and the other perspectives in play, and keep the moral aim of the decision in view.[^s3] Philosophers add reflection on whether your life matches your values, and openness, empathy and imagination as supporting virtues.[^s1] Seek out people with relevant experience, since practical wisdom grows from particulars rather than rules.[^s2]

## Common misreadings

- *Wisdom is knowing a lot.* Aristotle's Thales knew a great deal and was still not practically wise.[^s2]
- *Wisdom is high intelligence.* Experts separate the two sharply, and measured wisdom shows weak or no links to reasoning or vocabulary tests.[^s4][^s5]
- *Wisdom comes automatically with age.* Experts in the Delphi survey expected it to rise with age, but in a direct comparison none of five measures showed the top scorers to be older than other participants.[^s4][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

Psychologists still disagree about how to measure wisdom. When Glück and colleagues gave four established measures to 170 adults, including people nominated as wise, the correlations between them were mostly in the 0.20s, with little sign of one common factor; which test a study uses can shape its findings.[^s5] The 2019 Toronto meeting behind the common wisdom model was an attempt to resolve this, and its authors list conceptual and methodological questions still open.[^s3] Some philosophers doubt that any empirical scale can capture a normative ideal.[^s1]

## Connections

The **common wisdom model** is the psychologists' current consensus account of what wisdom involves.[^s3] The **Berlin wisdom paradigm** and the **three-dimensional wisdom model** are two older models, each measured in its own way and only weakly related to one another.[^s5] Wisdom **contrasts with general intelligence**, from which experts sharply distinguish it.[^s4] The **expert-consensus Delphi study** supports treating wisdom as a distinct, learnable quality.[^s4] Wisdom is **studied in** the **psychology of wisdom** and in **epistemology**, where analytic philosophers ask what epistemic standing it requires.[^s3][^s1] It is close to **intellectual humility**, which many traditions treat as part of it, and to **eudaimonia**, the flourishing life that wisdom aims at; **seeking counsel** and **virtue ethics** apply it to decisions and to character.[^s1][^s2]
