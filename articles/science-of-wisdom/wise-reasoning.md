# Wise reasoning

Wise reasoning is the name psychologists give to a particular way of thinking through messy, uncertain problems, especially conflicts between people. In the version most widely used in research it has four parts: intellectual humility, or recognising the limits of what you know; looking at the issue from perspectives broader than your own; recognising that situations, and relationships, can change; and searching for a compromise or a way to integrate different opinions.[^s1] Researchers observe it in how a person reasons about a specific problem, and it shifts from one situation to the next.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The construct grew out of work by Igor Grossmann and colleagues, who had people talk through conflicts from newspaper stories and advice-column letters and had trained coders score the transcripts.[^s3] An earlier American study by the same group had found that older adults used these reasoning patterns more than younger ones.[^s3] The dimensions were drawn from philosophical accounts of wisdom; Grossmann has described them as admitting the limits of one's knowledge, approaching a dilemma from several angles, seeking compromise and weighing short- and long-term consequences.[^s5] Later work turned the coded dimensions into a questionnaire, the Situated Wise Reasoning Scale, which asks people to recall a recent conflict and rate how they reasoned in it, and which adds a fifth facet: taking an outsider's vantage point.[^s2]

In 2019 a group of wisdom researchers met in Toronto to look for common ground across the field's competing definitions. Their consensus model describes wisdom as morally grounded metacognition applied to reasoning and problem-solving, a framing in which the reflective, perspective-balancing habits of wise reasoning form the metacognitive core.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Wise reasoning is not the same as being clever. It shows only a small positive relation to crystallised intelligence, openness and agreeableness, so bright, knowledgeable people can still reason unwisely about a quarrel or a setback.[^s1] It also varies a great deal by situation: the same person may reason more wisely about a friend's conflict than about a dispute at work, and Grossmann argues that how wisely someone reasons in a particular situation predicts their reactions, such as forgiving rather than retaliating, better than a global wisdom rating does.[^s1][^s2] The practical question is therefore not whether you are wise, but whether you are reasoning wisely about this problem now.[^s1]

Culture and life experience shape it too. In random samples from the Tokyo area and from Michigan, aged 25 to 75, older Americans reasoned more wisely about conflicts than younger Americans, while in Japan there was no such age trend; younger and middle-aged Japanese adults out-scored their American peers.[^s3] Cultural norms about harmony and conflict seem to influence when such reasoning develops.[^s3]

## What good looks like

Someone reasoning wisely about a conflict says what they do not know, especially about other people's motives, and treats their first reading as provisional.[^s1] They can describe how the other parties see things, imagine several ways events might unfold, and look for a resolution that serves more than one side.[^s1][^s3] Often they step back and view the situation as an outsider might, the facet the newer scale measures directly.[^s2] It is not indecision or agreeing with everyone: compromise means integrating legitimate concerns, not splitting every difference.[^s1]

## How to practise it

The best-supported method is to put distance between yourself and the problem, viewing it as an outside observer would or as if advising a friend. In experiments, graduating students in a recession who pictured their careers this way reasoned more wisely than controls, and couples who viewed a conflict through an outsider's eyes more often recognised their partner's view and sought compromise.[^s1] In month-long diary studies with 555 North American adults, people who wrote about each day's interpersonal conflict in the third person showed wiser reasoning over time than those who reflected in the first person or received no guidance, with the clearest gains in intellectual humility and in acknowledging others' perspectives.[^s5]

Before an important conversation or decision, write down what you don't know, one fair statement of the other person's view, two or three ways things could develop, and an outcome both sides could accept; afterwards, review the conflict with the same four questions.[^s1][^s2] When a conflict brings lasting distress, talking with a trusted person or a professional is a wiser step than relying on any reasoning technique alone.

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but bounded, so its status here is *mixed*. Situated wise reasoning is only moderately stable: over two years or more the scale's test-retest correlation was about .48, and people scored higher when recalling conflicts with friends than conflicts at work.[^s2] Unlike global self-rated wisdom scales, which correlated with self-deception and impression management, the situated measure was nearly unrelated to these response biases, which suggests it captures something less flattering-to-oneself than asking people whether they are wise.[^s2] Still, it relies on self-reports of recalled events, and agreement with observer ratings was modest (r ≈ .19).[^s2] The age findings are cross-sectional, come from two regions, and could partly reflect differences between generations; the age gain did not appear in Japan at all.[^s3] The consensus paper itself lists open questions about measurement, development and overlap with other constructs.[^s4]

## Connections

Wise reasoning is a narrower, testable facet of **wisdom** and is **part of the common wisdom model**, which places such metacognitive habits at the centre of the concept.[^s4] It is **studied in the psychology of wisdom**.[^s4] It was **introduced by Igor Grossmann** and colleagues and **demonstrated by** the cross-cultural **"Aging and wisdom: Culture matters" study**.[^s3] It is mostly measured and **applied in interpersonal conflict**, including disputes at home and at work.[^s2] It **contrasts with general intelligence**, to which it is only weakly related.[^s1] **Perspective-taking** is a prerequisite and one of its components; **intellectual humility** is another.[^s1] **Solomon's paradox** describes how it weakens when the problem is our own, and **self-distancing** is the practice that restores it.[^s1][^s5] The **Berlin wisdom paradigm** and the **three-dimensional wisdom model** are rival ways of measuring wisdom, and **deliberation** and **seeking counsel** put wise reasoning to work.[^s4]
