steps:
  - Pick a real dilemma from material you already teach (a historical episode, a case, a customer complaint) in which people's interests genuinely conflict.
  - Have learners list whose interests are at stake (their own, other people's, the wider community's) and what each option does to them in the short and the long run.
  - Ask each learner to argue the strongest case for a view they reject, from inside that person's position, then to look for a resolution that takes both sides into account.
  - Ask them to name the values behind their choice and to judge whether the means are acceptable, not only the result.
  - Lay out the three moves open to them (adapt to the situation, change it, or leave it) and have them say which fits best and why.
  - Model the habits yourself by saying what you don't know, changing your mind in front of them when given a good reason, and sharing credit.
  - Grade or praise answers for how well they balance interests and perspectives, then close by asking where a similar choice shows up in learners' own lives.

    The manager has the trainees list everyone affected (both employees, the rest of the team, customers),

# Teaching for wisdom

Teaching for wisdom is the practice of building wise thinking into ordinary lessons rather than leaving it to chance. Its premise is that conventional schooling trains the cognitive skills that tests measure, which matter but are not enough: students also need to learn to use their intelligence, creativity and knowledge for a common good.[^s2] In practice, learners work on dilemmas in which interests conflict, weigh short- against long-term outcomes and their own interests against other people's and the wider community's, and practise seeing a problem from more than one side.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

The approach was developed by the psychologist Robert Sternberg and colleagues as the classroom application of his balance theory of wisdom.[^s2] Sternberg traces his interest in wisdom to watching very smart people act foolishly.[^s8] In a 2002 essay he cited corporate scandals and educated perpetrators of violence as evidence that schools were turning out knowledgeable people who did not know how to use what they knew.[^s4] With funding from the W. T. Grant Foundation, his team infused wisdom into American history lessons: students examined events from several perspectives (one group's "settlers" are another's "invaders") and were to be compared with classes taught the same history conventionally.[^s4] A later chapter frames the curriculum around everyday dilemmas, such as a parent deciding between a care home and taking in an elderly relative, that knowledge and analysis alone cannot settle.[^s3]

Sternberg set out sixteen principles for such teaching.[^s8] A 2026 review condenses them to eight: explore what a good life is; teach how people depend on one another and on their environment; act as a role model and assign readings about wise judgment; help students recognise and balance their own and others' interests and values; show that the means matter as well as the ends; teach when to adapt to, change or leave a situation; encourage dialectical, dialogical, reflective and multi-perspective thinking; and reward pursuit of the common good.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Sternberg argues that clever people are prone to four fallacies: egocentrism, a sense of omniscience, a sense of omnipotence and a feeling of invulnerability. Wiser people, by contrast, know the limits of what they know and consider everyone they answer to.[^s4] On this view, more knowledge alone does not protect against foolish choices; the habits of balancing interests and taking other perspectives have to be practised on real content.[^s4][^s2] Researchers have since carried the idea into professional education, where students learn rules and routines that will one day collide with real people's needs.[^s1]

## How to apply it

The steps above translate the principles into something a teacher, parent, mentor or manager can run with one case. Researchers working in higher education distil a similar core: challenge beliefs, ask people to state their values, encourage self-development and self-reflection, and cultivate the moral emotions, supported by reading narrative or didactic texts and by a community of inquiry.[^s6] In professional higher education, researchers have tried related methods such as Neo-Socratic dialogue and learning through work placements.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Bolting it on.** Sternberg warns against separate "wisdom exercises"; the thinking has to run through the regular curriculum.[^s8]
- **Letting tests set the agenda.** Standardised tests do not assess wisdom, and teachers are rarely trained for it, so it is easily squeezed out.[^s8][^s4]
- **Keeping it teacher-centred.** Sternberg's principles describe what the teacher does; critics add that learners' own reflection and integration of experience matter just as much.[^s1]
- **Mistaking a part for the whole.** Gains in empathy or emotional control are not the same as becoming wiser overall.[^s7]

## Evidence and limits

Direct tests are scarce. A 2026 review treats Sternberg's principles as a theoretical account and notes that empirical research on wisdom teaching in higher education is only beginning.[^s1] The clearest classroom study, by Bruya and Ardelt, compared 131 students in five wisdom-oriented introductory philosophy courses with 176 students in seven ordinary philosophy and psychology courses over one semester. On the self-report three-dimensional wisdom scale, scores fell in the comparison courses, held steady under one wisdom curriculum and rose under the other.[^s5] That is a promising proof of concept from a single study of whole courses, not a replicated effect. A 2020 meta-analysis of 57 randomised trials (7,096 participants) found that interventions targeting components of wisdom raised prosocial behaviour and spirituality, though the gain in emotional regulation lost significance after correction for publication bias. Most outcomes were self-rated, heterogeneity was high, and no included trial targeted overall wisdom.[^s7]

## Connections

Teaching for wisdom **addresses** folly: Sternberg presents it as the response to the fallacies that lead clever, well-educated people astray.[^s4][^s8] It was **introduced by** Robert Sternberg and is **explained by** his balance theory of wisdom, which defines wisdom as using one's abilities for a common good by balancing interests and time horizons.[^s2] It aims to **develop** wise reasoning, through the dialectical, dialogical and multi-perspective thinking at the centre of its principles.[^s1] It is **supported by** Bruya and Ardelt's proof-of-concept classroom study, which measured growth with Monika Ardelt's three-dimensional wisdom scale.[^s5] Related nodes: **perspective-taking** and **intellectual humility** (habits it practises), the **distanced self-reflection diary** (a self-guided route to wiser reasoning) and the **Berlin wisdom paradigm** (a neighbouring psychological model of wisdom).[^s2][^s6]
