# Lifespan developmental psychology

Lifespan developmental psychology studies how people change from conception into old age, rather than treating childhood as the only time development happens. It looks at what most people share, at how individuals differ, and at how much any one person could still change, and it holds that adulthood involves acquiring, maintaining, transforming and losing capacities all at once.[^s3] For anyone interested in wisdom, it supplies the frame for a hard question: what, if anything, gets better as we get older, and how should we live with what gets worse?[^s1]

## Origin

The idea is older than the discipline: early writers such as Tetens (1777), Carus (1808) and Quetelet (1835) took a whole-life view, and twentieth-century figures including Charlotte Bühler, Carl Jung and Erik Erikson kept it alive.[^s1] Systematic empirical work began only around the late 1960s, pushed by ageing populations, the growth of gerontology and the ageing of participants in the classic longitudinal child studies; a West Virginia conference series from 1970 helped name the field.[^s1]

Paul Baltes's 1987 paper set out its core propositions.[^s1] Development is **lifelong**, with both cumulative and genuinely new change at every age. It is **multidirectional**: different abilities move in different directions at the same time. Every change brings **gains and losses**, and "pure gain" never happens. There is **plasticity**, meaning a person's capacities depend on conditions and practice, within limits. It is **embedded in history**, and shaped by three kinds of influence: **age-graded** ones most people meet at similar ages, **history-graded** ones such as wars or modernisation, and **non-normative** events that strike individuals unpredictably.[^s1] Finally, no single discipline can explain it; biology, sociology, anthropology and history are all needed.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Its best-known finding is a two-part picture of intelligence. The *mechanics*, biologically based processing speed and precision, decline steadily through adulthood. The *pragmatics*, culturally acquired knowledge, can hold steady or grow until around the sixth or seventh decade, falling mainly in very old age.[^s3] Baltes placed wisdom, expertise in the fundamental pragmatics of life, on the pragmatic side, which is why the Berlin wisdom research began inside this field.[^s1] His later data suggested a sobering limit: wisdom-related performance held up in older adults until about age 70 and then declined too, so the field never promised that old age guarantees wisdom.[^s2]

The field also offers a frame for living with loss. Baltes argued that as life goes on, the biological benefits of evolution fade, the need for cultural supports (education, technology, medicine, other people) rises, and those supports work less efficiently.[^s2] Accordingly, the resources a person devotes to *growth* in childhood shift toward *maintenance and recovery* in adulthood and toward *regulating loss* in old age.[^s2][^s3] Seeing losses as a normal part of development, not personal failure, is itself a step toward realistic self-understanding.[^s1]

## Selection, optimisation and compensation

The field's most practical idea is the model of **selective optimisation with compensation (SOC)**. *Selection* means choosing which goals to pursue, by preference or because a loss forces it; *optimisation* means investing practice, effort and skill in those goals; *compensation* means finding new means, aids or strategies when old ones fail.[^s2] Baltes's favourite illustration was the pianist Arthur Rubinstein, who explained in a television interview at about 80 that he played fewer pieces, practised them more, and slowed down before fast passages so that they sounded quicker by contrast.[^s2][^s3] SOC is a general strategy, not one tied to old age: any life with limited time involves choosing, investing and adapting.[^s2]

## What the field suggests you can do

- In a demanding season (a new role, caring for a relative, an injury), name the two or three goals that matter most and consciously let some others go, rather than spreading yourself evenly across everything.[^s2]
- Put deliberate practice and attention into the goals you keep, and plan in advance what aids, help or workarounds you will use when a usual way of doing things stops working.[^s2][^s5]
- When judging ageing, yours or someone else's, ask what has been gained as well as lost.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Evidence for SOC is real but modest. A meta-analysis of two decades of workplace research found that reported SOC use correlated with job engagement (about .38), job satisfaction (.25) and job performance (around .21 to .23, including ratings by others), but barely to age, and called for better measurement.[^s4] Two studies of adults aged roughly 70 to 90 found that SOC strategies mattered most when personal resources were limited, buffering their effect on well-being among the oldest participants, and that individual strategies differed in how helpful they were.[^s5] These are mostly correlational, self-report studies: they show association, not that adopting SOC causes better outcomes.[^s4][^s5]

Lifespan research is also methodologically hard. Comparing age groups mixes age with cohort: generational gains in test scores and the selective survival of healthier people can make older samples look better or worse than ageing itself would.[^s3] Baltes himself called wisdom research only a beginning in 1987, and the field's broad propositions work better as a guiding frame than as tightly testable predictions.[^s1] Persistent difficulty coping with illness, loss or decline is a reason to seek professional support, not something these frameworks replace.

## Connections

The field took its modern shape from **Paul Baltes**, and draws on earlier whole-life thinkers such as **Erik Erikson**, whose stage theory named wisdom as a goal of late adulthood.[^s1][^s3] It is **applied in** understanding **ageing**, where its account of shifting resources and growing need for cultural support is central.[^s2] Its SOC model is **supported by** a workplace meta-analysis and by studies of older adults, within the limits above.[^s4][^s5] The **psychology of wisdom** and the **Berlin wisdom paradigm** grew out of its interest in the pragmatics of intelligence, and **wisdom and age** is one of its central questions.[^s1][^s2] **Socioemotional selectivity theory** and the **positivity effect in ageing** are studied within it, and its stress on maintenance and recovery links it to **resilience**.[^s3]
