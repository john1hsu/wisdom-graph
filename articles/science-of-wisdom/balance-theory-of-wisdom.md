# Balance theory of wisdom

The balance theory of wisdom is the psychologist Robert Sternberg's account of what makes a judgment wise. On this view wisdom is not a store of knowledge or a high IQ but a way of using intelligence, creativity, knowledge and experience, steered by positive ethical values, toward a **common good**.[^s1][^s2] A wise solution balances three kinds of interest, over both the short and the long run, and makes a sensible choice among three ways of responding to one's surroundings.[^s2]

## Origin

Sternberg says he turned to wisdom after watching very smart people do foolish things, and concluding that analytical ability can even work against good judgment when clever people assume they cannot err.[^s6] After an earlier study of how ordinary people understand wisdom, he set out the balance theory in a 1998 paper in *Review of General Psychology*, then extended it to education, arguing that schools should teach for wisdom as well as knowledge.[^s1] A 2004 article in *The ANNALS* restated it as a definition and asked how wisdom could be assessed and developed.[^s2] In 2019 he published an "augmented" version in the *Cambridge Handbook of Wisdom*, which keeps the core definition, treats wisdom as a learned skill and asks a further question: why people often prefer clever, self-serving "wise guys" to people who are actually wise.[^s5]

## Why it matters

The theory puts the emphasis on what a person does with their abilities rather than on how able they are. Sternberg argues that the failures of leaders and citizens usually reflect a shortage of wisdom more than a shortage of intelligence.[^s1] As Grossmann's group summarises him, thinking skill on its own is neither wise nor unwise; what matters is the ends it serves.[^s3] That is why the theory treats ethics as part of wisdom rather than an optional extra: as Sternberg puts it, an action that is not ethical cannot be wise, however clever it is.[^s6]

It also gives a usable checklist for hard decisions at work and at home, because it names the things people most often leave out: the people not in the room, the long run and the option of changing or leaving the situation rather than putting up with it.[^s2][^s7]

## How it works and how to use it

Sternberg's theory has four moving parts.[^s1][^s2]

- **Three interests.** *Intrapersonal* interests concern only you (identity, prestige, power, pleasure, growth). *Interpersonal* interests concern your relationships with particular people. *Extrapersonal* interests concern wider groups: an organisation, a community, a country or the environment.[^s1]
- **Two time horizons.** Each option is judged by its short-term and long-term consequences.[^s1][^s2]
- **Three responses to the environment.** You can *adapt* to the situation, *shape* it, or *select* a different one. Sternberg illustrates this with his own career: after failing both to fit into a poor job and to change it, he left.[^s6]
- **Values and tacit knowledge.** The interests are not weighted equally; the weights depend on how far each option advances a common good, and that judgement draws on values and on tacit, experience-based knowledge rather than only on explicit rules.[^s1]

Sternberg is candid that there is no agreed definition of the common good. Working out what it is in a particular case, and persuading others, is itself a large part of wisdom.[^s1][^s7] In his later account this means looking beyond one's own family or "tribe" and listening actively and reflectively to people whose interests differ from one's own.[^s7]

In practice this means slowing down long enough to list who is affected, at each of the three levels, and how each option plays out now and later, before deciding whether to adapt, shape or select.[^s2][^s6]

## Common misreadings

- **"Balance means splitting the difference."** The theory weights interests by the common good, so a wise answer may favour one party heavily if that serves the wider good.[^s1]
- **"It is a measure of wisdom."** It is a conceptual model. Sternberg describes how wisdom might be assessed, but the theory itself is not a validated test.[^s2][^s3]
- **"Clever people are naturally wiser."** The theory predicts the opposite risk: Sternberg lists unrealistic optimism, egocentrism and false feelings of omniscience, omnipotence and invulnerability as typical traps for the able and powerful.[^s1]

## Evidence

The balance theory has been more influential as a framework than as a directly tested model; Sternberg himself has described the theory as a small-to-medium contribution.[^s6] Its central idea has, however, been taken up by later research. Brienza and colleagues noted that the link between wisdom and balance had remained largely theoretical, and that popular wisdom questionnaires assumed balance without ever testing it.[^s3] Across ten samples (about 4,400 people), they found that a situation-based measure of wise reasoning was positively related to balance-related outcomes, such as weighing cooperative against self-protective goals, while broad self-report wisdom scales were unrelated or even negatively related to them.[^s3] That supports balance as a criterion of wisdom while warning that how it is measured matters.[^s3]

The theory's emphasis on values that serve others gets partial support from four studies by Glück and colleagues. People across the political spectrum described wise people as high in benevolence, universalism and self-direction and low in power and achievement, rather than simply projecting their own values.[^s4] Self-report wisdom scales correlated with these self-transcendent values, but performance measures such as the Berlin paradigm mostly did not, and the samples were mainly German-speaking and Western.[^s4] More generally, wisdom research still relies heavily on self-report, which invites socially desirable answers, and it increasingly treats wisdom as dependent on context rather than as a fixed trait.[^s8]

## Connections

The theory was **introduced by Robert Sternberg** and sits within the **psychology of wisdom**.[^s2] It **addresses folly**, which Sternberg explains as intelligence put to egocentric, short-term use.[^s1] It **complements** Grossmann's **wise reasoning**, which treats balancing interests and perspectives as a test that wise reasoning should pass,[^s3] and is **supported by** the validation of the **situated wise reasoning scale**[^s3] and by the **wisdom and value orientations study**.[^s4] Its adapt-shape-select choice is **applied in major life decisions** such as whether to stay in a job,[^s6] and, more tentatively, in **leading others**.[^s7] It requires **perspective-taking**, since one cannot balance interests one has not imagined.[^s7] Related nodes: **teaching for wisdom** (its classroom application), the **Berlin wisdom paradigm** and the **three-dimensional model of wisdom** (other psychological models), **implicit theories of wisdom** (Sternberg's earlier work), the **common wisdom model**, **practical wisdom**, **knowing when to quit** and **wise leadership**.[^s1][^s8]
