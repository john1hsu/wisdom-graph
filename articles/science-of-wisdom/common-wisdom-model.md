# Common wisdom model

The common wisdom model is a shared definition of wisdom proposed in 2020 by an international group of psychologists and cognitive scientists. Its core claim is that the many empirical approaches to wisdom converge on one idea: wisdom is metacognition put to work in reasoning and problem-solving, and anchored in moral concern.[^s1] Put more plainly, a wise person thinks well about their own thinking when a problem is messy, and does so for good ends.[^s2]

The model has two pillars. **Perspectival metacognition** covers recognising the limits of what one knows, considering several perspectives, balancing and integrating viewpoints, and adapting one's reasoning to the context.[^s2][^s6] **Moral aspirations** cover a commitment to seeking the truth, prosocial goals, balancing one's own interests with other people's, and an orientation toward shared humanity.[^s2][^s5]

## Origin

By the late 2010s, wisdom research had what its practitioners called a jingle-jangle problem: different labels for the same idea and the same label for different ideas, with blurred lines between notions such as epistemic and intellectual humility.[^s2] In summer 2019, the University of Waterloo psychologist Igor Grossmann convened a task force of wisdom scholars in Toronto, including Monika Ardelt, Michel Ferrari, Howard Nusbaum and Nic Weststrate, and surveyed researchers on several continents about the constructs they studied.[^s2] The result was published in *Psychological Inquiry* in 2020, alongside a review of measurement, development, training and cultural variation and a list of open questions.[^s1] The authors described the model as a common denominator of existing theories rather than a complete theory of its own.[^s5]

## Why it matters

A shared definition lets studies add up. If one lab's "wisdom" is self-rated compassion and another's is the quality of reasoning about a stranger's dilemma, their findings cannot be compared; the task force hoped a common core would make research cumulative and help explain how wise people actually solve problems in daily life.[^s2] For readers, the model offers a compact answer to an old question: wisdom is neither cleverness alone nor good intentions alone, but disciplined thinking in the service of decent ends.[^s1][^s6]

The model also treats wisdom as something that shows up more in some situations than others. Grossmann describes wisdom as a dynamic quality that varies with culture, experience and circumstance, and does not simply grow with age.[^s6] The task force added that whether a choice was wise can often be judged only afterwards, with reflection and knowledge of the context.[^s2]

## How it works and how to use it

The perspectival pillar is about *how* you think, not *which* view you hold. In Grossmann's words, the model does not mean "that you should be applying a particular perspective".[^s2] The habits it names are portable: ask what you might be missing, picture the situation from the standpoint of each person involved, look for a resolution that reconciles their interests, and adjust to the setting rather than applying a rule mechanically.[^s6][^s2]

The moral pillar supplies the direction. Wise reasoning, on this account, balances short-term and long-term interests and personal, communal and wider ones, seeking outcomes that help oneself while also serving others.[^s6] A workplace illustration: a manager who discovers that a well-liked colleague has padded a small expense claim could react with an automatic rule ("report it, end of story") or automatic loyalty ("let it go"). The model points to a third path: find out what happened before judging, weigh how the colleague, the team and the organisation each see it, and pursue a response that is honest about the wrong while treating the colleague as a person.[^s3][^s2]

## Common misreadings

- **"It is a finished theory."** Its own authors and sympathetic critics call the moral pillar underspecified, and the model leaves open how its two pillars interact and which emotions matter.[^s5][^s4]
- **"Perspective-taking is enough."** Lees and Young point out that the model sometimes treats the pillars as independent and sometimes as causally linked, and argue that perspectival metacognition should explicitly include theory of mind: reasoning about what other people think and feel.[^s3]
- **"Considering every view means having no convictions."** Even within the task force, Ardelt preferred "moral grounding" to "moral aspirations", arguing that wise people act on morality rather than merely aspire to it.[^s2]

## Evidence

The model is a theoretical synthesis, not an experimental finding, and should be judged by how well it organises research. It rests on a survey of researchers and a review of existing measures, and Judith Glück suggested testing it by checking its consistency with established models such as Ardelt's three-dimensional model and her own MORE life experience model.[^s2] Critics have raised substantive gaps. Glück noted that it lacks an account of emotion regulation and real-world application.[^s2] Kristjánsson and colleagues argue that perspectival metacognition is inert and unmotivating, that "prosociality" is a behavioural rather than a moral term, and that the model cannot say how competing moral considerations are weighed; their neo-Aristotelian phronesis model adds an emotional-regulation function and a "blueprint" of the good life to fill those gaps.[^s4] Arvanitis extends it with self-determination theory to explain why anyone would strive for wisdom, but offers no new empirical test and calls for one.[^s5]

## Connections

The model was **introduced by Igor Grossmann** and his co-authors[^s2] **in** *The science of wisdom in a polarized world*,[^s1] and was **influenced by** existing frameworks such as Ardelt's **three-dimensional model of wisdom**, whose measures the task force reviewed.[^s2] It is **studied in** the **psychology of wisdom**, which it attempts to unify.[^s6] It is **challenged by** the **Aristotelian phronesis model**, which its authors argue better explains morally motivated action.[^s4] It **complements perspective-taking**: Lees and Young argue that reasoning about other minds belongs inside its metacognitive pillar.[^s3] Perspectival metacognition is central to Grossmann's **wise reasoning**,[^s6] **intellectual humility** is one of its named components,[^s2] and the moral pillar's balancing of self-interest with others' interests resembles the concerns of Sternberg's **balance theory of wisdom**, while its critics draw on the classical idea of **practical wisdom**.[^s5][^s4]
