# Positive psychology

Positive psychology is the scientific study of what goes right in human lives. Its founders described it on three levels: valued *experiences* such as contentment, hope, flow and happiness; positive *individual traits* such as courage, perseverance, forgiveness, interpersonal skill and **wisdom**; and *civic virtues and institutions* that help people become better citizens.[^s1] It matters for anyone trying to live wisely because it put wisdom and character back on psychology's research agenda, and because its own history is a useful lesson in how attractive ideas about the good life can outrun their evidence.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

The movement was named and launched by Martin Seligman and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi in a 2000 special issue of *American Psychologist*, which introduced fifteen articles.[^s1][^s6] Their argument was that after the Second World War psychology had become mainly a science of healing. New funding through the Veterans Administration and the National Institute of Mental Health rewarded the study and treatment of mental illness, which brought real gains, but two of the discipline's pre-war missions, making ordinary lives more fulfilling and nurturing high talent, were largely forgotten.[^s1] They aimed to build strengths as well as repair damage, with prevention as a major goal.[^s1]

They acknowledged humanistic psychology, associated with Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers, as a forerunner with a generous vision, but faulted it for lacking a cumulative empirical base and for spawning self-help movements, and promised to use mainstream research methods instead.[^s1] Critics have since replied that this origin story understated how much earlier research on optimal functioning already existed and was then overlooked.[^s6]

## Why it matters

For the study of wisdom, the movement mattered in two ways. First, it treated wisdom as a legitimate scientific topic: the launch issue summarised Baltes and Staudinger's Berlin wisdom paradigm, which describes wisdom as expert knowledge about the fundamental questions of life.[^s1] Second, its classification of character strengths, the VIA (Values in Action) scheme, groups 24 strengths under six broad virtues, and the first of these is *wisdom and knowledge*: creativity, curiosity, judgment, love of learning and perspective.[^s2]

The data on these strengths are instructive rather than flattering. In a sample of more than 12,000 US adults, perspective and curiosity correlated moderately with life satisfaction (about .37 and .39), while judgment, love of learning and creativity correlated only weakly (.12 to .15); zest and hope were the strongest correlates (.54).[^s2] These are cross-sectional, self-report correlations, so they show that some wisdom-related strengths travel with satisfaction, not that cultivating them causes it.[^s2]

## What the field offers in practice

Positive psychology interventions are brief exercises, such as gratitude letters, savouring or acts of kindness, aimed at raising positive feelings, thoughts or behaviour rather than treating symptoms.[^s3] A meta-analysis of 39 randomised trials with about 6,100 participants found small effects at the end of the programmes: d = 0.34 for subjective well-being, 0.20 for psychological well-being and 0.23 for depression. Effects on well-being remained, smaller, three to six months later, but the effect on depression was no longer significant.[^s3] A wise way to use such exercises is as modest aids to reflection, chosen because they fit your values, rather than as cures, and persistent low mood or distress is a reason to seek professional support, not a gap that self-help exercises should fill.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence base has been sharply revised. The authors of the meta-analysis above rated almost every included trial as low or medium quality, found signs of publication bias, and saw some effects shrink after correction.[^s3] A later reanalysis that corrected both major meta-analyses for small-sample bias found an effect on well-being of roughly r = .10, and an effect on depression that was inconsistent, depended on outliers and was generally not significant.[^s4]

The field's most famous cautionary tale is the "critical positivity ratio", the claim by Fredrickson and Losada (2005) that people flourish when positive emotions outnumber negative ones by more than 2.9013 to 1. Nicholas Brown, Alan Sokal and Harris Friedman showed that the figure came from equations borrowed from fluid dynamics without justification, with arbitrary parameters and no matching data, and called it entirely unfounded; they did not dispute that positive emotions can be good for people.[^s5]

Carol Ryff's review adds wider concerns: redundant well-being measures, samples that are mostly Western and privileged (only about 2% of interventions had been tested with vulnerable or multicultural groups), and a heavily commercialised industry of costly training and coaching with little oversight.[^s6] A "second wave" of positive psychology responds by studying positive and negative experiences together, arguing that difficult states can also contribute to flourishing.[^s6] Readers can take a wiser path by asking of any positive-psychology claim how large the effect is, who was studied, and whether it has been replicated.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

Positive psychology was **introduced by** Martin Seligman and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, and was **influenced by** humanistic psychology even as its founders set themselves against that movement's methods.[^s1] Its intervention research is **supported by** the Bolier meta-analysis, within the limits that study itself lists, and **challenged by** the reanalysis showing much smaller effects.[^s3][^s4] The positivity-ratio claim that once circulated widely in the field is **challenged by** Brown, Sokal and Friedman's critique.[^s5] It overlaps with the **psychology of wisdom** and draws on the **Berlin wisdom paradigm**, while its **VIA character strengths** place wisdom first among the virtues.[^s1][^s2] Its research on **hedonic and eudaimonic well-being**, **meaning in life**, **savoring** and **wisdom and well-being** is linked here, and **second-wave positive psychology** is its main internal corrective.[^s2][^s6]
