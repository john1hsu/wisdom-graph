# Aristotelian phronesis model

The Aristotelian phronesis model (APM) is an attempt to turn Aristotle's idea of practical wisdom into something psychologists can describe, measure and teach. It treats phronesis not as one global trait but as four functions that work together: perceiving what is ethically at stake, adjudicating between competing values, steering by a "blueprint" of a flourishing life, and regulating emotion so that feeling and judgment agree.[^s1] It was set out in 2019 by Catharine Darnell, Liz Gulliford, Kristján Kristjánsson and Panos Paris of the Jubilee Centre for Character and Virtues at the University of Birmingham.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The model grew out of a long-running puzzle in moral psychology, which its authors call the "gappiness problem": people often know what is right and still do not do it.[^s1] Lawrence Kohlberg's tradition assumed that better moral reasoning would carry people to better action, but Augusto Blasi's reviews found that reasoning explained only around a tenth of the variation in moral behaviour.[^s1] Single-ingredient fixes, such as moral identity or moral emotion, predicted behaviour only modestly, and James Rest's neo-Kohlbergian school proposed four components (sensitivity, judgment, motivation and implementation) that must all work.[^s1] Darnell and colleagues argued that Aristotle's phronesis, which presupposes well-habituated dispositions and deliberates towards the mean in a particular case, is the integrating faculty this job needs.[^s1]

## The four functions

- **Constitutive function (moral sensitivity).** Noticing the ethically salient features of a situation and which virtues are relevant to it.[^s1]
- **Integrative function (moral adjudication).** Weighing virtues that pull in different directions, such as honesty and compassion when telling a hard truth would wound someone, and deciding how to blend them or which gives way.[^s1] The authors treat this as the core of deliberation and stress that it is highly situation-specific.[^s1]
- **Blueprint function.** A working sense of what makes a life go well, which gives a person's moral identity its motivational pull. It need not be a sophisticated philosophy; an ordinary, well-brought-up person can have one.[^s1]
- **Emotional regulation function.** Bringing emotions into line with an accurate reading of the situation, which the authors describe as infusing emotion with reason rather than suppressing it.[^s1]

Set beside Rest's model, sensitivity, judgment and motivation line up roughly with the first three functions; the main difference is the fourth, since the neo-Kohlbergian "implementation" component stresses ego-strength and social skill and contains no specific account of emotion.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The model gives a vocabulary for diagnosing where a particular decision is likely to fail. Someone can see a problem clearly yet freeze when two good values conflict, or reason well but act from unchecked anger.[^s1][^s3] Its authors also present it as a corrective to the common wisdom model, which they credit for focusing on practical wisdom but criticise for leaving out emotion, for thin moral aspirations, and for a perspectival metacognition that does not by itself motivate action.[^s3] They conclude that the APM has more power to explain morally motivated action, while the common model may suit research on moral cognition, and they describe the two as potentially complementary.[^s3]

## How to use it

The model is a research framework, not a self-help method, but it suggests a simple review around a hard decision. Ask what in the situation matters morally; name the values that conflict and decide, with reasons, how to weigh them; check the choice against the kind of life you are trying to lead; and notice whether your feelings fit the facts.[^s1] This is the shape of many judgment calls in medicine, nursing, teaching and business, fields where phronesis has been revived as a professional ideal.[^s3] Developmental findings suggest the functions mature at different rates: in a cross-sectional Jubilee Centre study of 492 adolescents and young adults, older participants showed more empathic concern and perspective-taking, while moral-identity measures did not differ by age.[^s4]

## Common misreadings

The APM does not assume Aristotle's ethics is simply true, and it drops his views on women and non-Greeks.[^s1][^s3] Nor does it require a strong "unity of the virtues": real people's virtues are treated as held probabilistically, not as a complete package.[^s1][^s3] It also targets overt moral performance, not cleverness or self-description.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

A proof-of-concept study approximated the four functions with existing measures (virtue-identification tasks, choices and justifications in dilemmas, moral-identity scales, and empathy and perspective-taking scales) in separate samples of UK adults and adolescents. A model with a single higher-order "phronesis" factor fitted both samples, and that factor was positively related to prosocial behaviour.[^s2] The authors call this interim: it needs replication, tests against existing wisdom measures, longitudinal designs and purpose-built instruments.[^s2][^s3] They also concede that no current model explains how practical wisdom develops or how best to teach it.[^s3] Critics have pressed further. Villacís and Vaccarezza argue that moral-identity scales capture only part of the blueprint, the most debated function.[^s6] Sanderse argues that the model departs from Aristotle, for whom practical wisdom culminates in a reasoned choice (prohairesis) rather than in action itself, and that its social, interpretive and political dimensions are underplayed.[^s5]

## Connections

The model is **rooted in Aristotelian virtue ethics** and was **introduced by Kristján Kristjánsson** and his Jubilee Centre colleagues, who built it as an **alternative to the neo-Kohlbergian four-component model** of moral functioning.[^s1] Because its aim is to close the gap between knowing and doing, it **addresses weakness of will**, the classical name for acting against one's better judgment.[^s1] Its empirical viability was **demonstrated by the multifunction phronesis assessment study**.[^s2] It **contrasts with the common wisdom model** and is **studied in the psychology of wisdom**, where its authors place it within research on contextual integrative thinking.[^s3] It is **challenged by** Villacís and Vaccarezza's **review of the blueprint function**.[^s6] Its constitutive function overlaps with **moral awareness**, its emotional function is measured through **empathy** and **perspective-taking**, adjudication has been assessed with the **Situated Wise Reasoning Scale**, and the blueprint points to **eudaimonia**.[^s1][^s4] It models **practical wisdom** as in the ***Nicomachean Ethics***, presupposes **moral habituation**, and bears on **teaching for wisdom** and **wisdom in the professions**.[^s1][^s3]
