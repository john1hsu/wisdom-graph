# Solomon's paradox

Solomon's paradox is the finding that people tend to think more wisely about someone else's troubles than about their own. Asked about a friend's relationship crisis, they are quicker to concede the limits of what they know, to look for a compromise, to weigh how the other party sees things and to expect that events may take several turns; asked about the same crisis in their own life, they do all of this less.[^s1][^s6] The psychologists Igor Grossmann and Ethan Kross gave the bias its name in 2014.[^s5]

## Origin

The label points to King Solomon of the Hebrew Bible, famous for wise judgment, whose own decisions, as Grossmann and Kross tell it, contributed to the decline of his kingdom.[^s1] Later researchers retell the story in the same way: Solomon asked for wisdom on becoming king, yet his discernment did not extend to managing his own life.[^s3] The name is a modern coinage that uses the king as an illustration, not a historical claim about him.[^s5]

The original paper reported three experiments with 693 participants in total.[^s6] Wise reasoning was scored on four elements: recognising the limits of one's knowledge, searching for compromise, considering other perspectives, and recognising that a situation can change and unfold in several ways.[^s1] The first two studies gave University of Michigan students a scenario about a partner's infidelity; the third gave crowd-sourced adults aged 20–40 and 60–80 a scenario about betrayal by a friend or relative, framed either as happening to them or to someone close to them.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The finding challenges the idea that wisdom is a fixed possession. The same people reasoned measurably better in the role of adviser than in the role of the one caught up in the problem.[^s1] In the third study, older adults were no wiser than younger adults about their own conflicts and showed the same gap, so the authors suggested that the saying "with age comes wisdom" may hold mainly for other people's dilemmas.[^s1][^s5] One practical lesson follows: being good at advising others is weak evidence that you will handle your own crisis well, and the moments when wisdom matters most, a betrayal or a falling-out with someone close, are exactly where the gap appeared.[^s1]

## Evidence

The original studies found the self-other gap in all three experiments, and the gap was removed or reduced when people reflected on their own dilemma from a distanced, third-person vantage point (fully in the second study, only marginally in the third).[^s1] The scenarios were hypothetical and limited to infidelity and betrayal, and the authors noted that everyday situations still needed testing.[^s1]

An online study of 399 adults found the asymmetry again, but with a small effect, smaller than in the original work.[^s3] Thinking about one's own conflict went with lower positive mood and lower self-transcendence, the sense of looking beyond one's own interests, and both statistically mediated the gap.[^s3] The authors cautioned that the design was cross-sectional, that participants rated strategies rather than using them, and that people simply know more about their own conflicts than a friend's, which may confound the comparison.[^s3]

A 2023 meta-analysis pooled six studies and 20 effect sizes and estimated a small advantage for reasoning about others (d ≈ 0.32), with high heterogeneity.[^s2] Effects were larger with observer-rated measures than with self-report scales, and larger in smaller samples, a pattern that can signal inflated estimates; publication bias was not tested, the samples came mainly from the United States and China, and some figures in the paper are reported inconsistently.[^s2] The effect looks real but modest and rests on a small body of work focused on relationship conflicts, so its status here is *mixed*.[^s1][^s2]

## How to spot it

Watch for the signs that mirror the four elements of wise reasoning.[^s1] You are giving a friend balanced, patient advice about a situation you are handling impulsively yourself. You are certain about the other person's motives and cannot describe their view in terms they would accept. You can picture only one ending. You treat any middle ground as losing.[^s1][^s3]

## How to fix it

The best-tested remedy is self-distancing: think about your own problem as an observer would, for example by referring to yourself by name or in the third person.[^s1] In the original second study this erased the self-other gap.[^s1] A month-long and a week-long diary study (555 adults in total) asked people to write each day about their most significant experience in the third person; their wise reasoning about interpersonal challenges rose compared with first-person writers, though the effects were small to moderate, the samples were North American, there was no no-diary control, and other kinds of decision were not tested.[^s4]

Simpler habits follow the same logic. Ask what you would tell a friend in your exact position, then check whether you are taking that advice.[^s1] Before acting, write down what you don't know, one fair statement of the other person's view, and two ways the situation could develop.[^s1][^s4] Mood and self-transcendence may play a part, so a pause that lets the first sting fade and turns attention to everyone's interests is a reasonable step, though that link is still tentative.[^s3] When a conflict brings persistent distress, talking with a trusted person or a professional is wiser than relying on any self-help technique.

## Connections

Solomon's paradox **undermines wise reasoning**, the set of capacities on which it was measured, and is a form of **egocentric bias**, since taking an egocentric view crowds out the perspective-taking and long-range thinking that wise reasoning needs.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by the "Exploring Solomon's paradox" study** and **supported by** a 2023 **meta-analysis**.[^s1][^s2] It is partly, and tentatively, **explained by self-transcendence**.[^s3] It was **introduced by Igor Grossmann** and **Ethan Kross**.[^s5] **Self-distancing** is the practice that counters it; **seeking counsel** and **giving advice** turn on the same insight that outsiders often see more clearly; **perspective-taking** and **intellectual humility** are two of the capacities it weakens; and **interpersonal conflict** is the life situation in which it has been studied.[^s1][^s4]
