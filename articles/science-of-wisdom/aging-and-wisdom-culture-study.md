# Aging and wisdom across cultures study

Is the link between growing older and reasoning more wisely a fact about human development, or a fact about one culture? In 2012 Igor Grossmann, Richard Nisbett, Shinobu Kitayama and colleagues put the question to the test by comparing Americans and Japanese aged 25 to 75. The answer they reported was that culture shapes not only how wise people are but *when* in life wise reasoning tends to appear.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The study grew out of an earlier American finding that older adults reasoned more wisely about social conflicts than younger adults, despite declining fluid intelligence. The authors wanted to know whether that age pattern would hold in a society that socialises people differently around conflict. They predicted that Japan's emphasis on interdependence and avoiding open conflict might teach these reasoning habits earlier, while Americans, who meet more frequent and varied conflicts, might keep learning them across the lifespan.[^s1]

**Design.** The team drew age-stratified random samples of 225 adults from Washtenaw County, Michigan, and 186 from the Tokyo area, including working-class participants, split into younger (25–40), middle-aged (41–59) and older (60–75) groups. Over two sessions, participants read newspaper-style stories about conflicts between groups (over ethnic tensions, resources and politics) and real advice-column letters about conflicts among siblings, friends and spouses, and said how each situation would unfold and why.[^s1]

**Measure.** Coders who did not know participants' ages rated the transcripts on six aspects of wise reasoning: taking others' perspectives, recognising that situations change, considering several possible outcomes, recognising the limits of one's own knowledge, searching for compromise, and anticipating resolution of the conflict. Coders in each country also scored translated answers from the other country to check that the scheme travelled, agreeing about 80% of the time.[^s1]

## What it found

Among Americans, wise reasoning rose clearly with age, strongly for intergroup conflicts (r = .53) and modestly for interpersonal ones (r = .24). Among Japanese, age was unrelated to either. For intergroup conflicts, younger and middle-aged Japanese outscored Americans of the same age, while older Americans outscored older Japanese. For interpersonal conflicts, Japanese scored higher at every age, but the gap narrowed sharply among older adults.[^s1] Fluid intelligence declined with age in both countries, and controlling for education, occupational prestige and knowledge-based intelligence did not change the age results.[^s1]

One detail sharpens what "wise" meant here. Americans talked about resolving the conflict more often, yet Japanese scored higher on most of the other aspects. The authors argued that pressing for resolution is not a universal mark of wisdom, and that it is the quality of the reasoning behind any resolution that counts.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The study warns against two tempting shortcuts: assuming that seniority guarantees good judgment, and assuming that older adults' judgment fades with their memory. Grossmann hoped the findings would counter ageist stereotypes in both Western and East Asian societies, and argued that cultural researchers rarely consider how development over a lifetime produces, or erases, differences between cultures.[^s2] Work with the same American sample found that wise reasoning about conflicts, rather than intelligence, accounted for part of older adults' greater well-being, which is one reason the timing of wisdom's growth is worth understanding.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The results are best treated as a well-designed but single comparison, so the study's evidence status is mixed. The authors themselves called it a first step: it covered only two cultures, the data are cross-sectional, so age differences could reflect generations rather than individual development, and the proposed cause, interdependent socialisation, was inferred rather than measured or manipulated.[^s1] Answers to different stories by the same person correlated only weakly, which is consistent with later diary research showing that a person's wise reasoning varies substantially from one situation to the next and is higher in social than in non-social situations.[^s1][^s4]

Other measures tell a different story. An earlier study of 136 middle-aged and older Americans and Japanese, using a broader set of analytical and emotional wisdom measures, found that older adults scored higher than middle-aged adults in both cultures, alongside culture-specific differences on particular dimensions.[^s3] Whether wisdom rises with age in a given culture therefore seems to depend on which facets of wisdom are measured as well as where.[^s1][^s3]

## What it means in practice

- Don't read age, or its absence, as a proxy for judgment; look at how someone actually reasons through a conflict: whether they take other perspectives, allow for change and admit what they don't know.[^s1]
- Expect wise habits to be teachable early: in the Japanese sample, many younger adults already reasoned this way, which suggests upbringing and social norms, not just years, can build them.[^s1][^s2]
- In a cross-cultural team or family, be cautious about treating a push for quick resolution as the wiser stance; patience with an unresolved conflict can reflect careful reasoning rather than avoidance.[^s1]
- Judge your own wisdom by situation, not as a fixed trait; the same person can reason well about a friend's dispute and poorly about their own.[^s4]

## Connections

The study is a test of **wisdom and age** and is filed under it, since it shows that the age pattern depends on culture.[^s1] It was **introduced by** Igor Grossmann with colleagues, and is **studied in** lifespan developmental psychology[^s1] and cultural psychology, the two fields whose questions it joins.[^s2] It is **supported by** the earlier American study of reasoning about social conflicts, whose age gains it replicated in the US sample,[^s1] and **challenged by** Takahashi and Overton's comparison, which found age advantages in both countries on different measures.[^s3] It relates to **wise reasoning**, the capacity it measures; to **perspective-taking** and **intellectual humility**, two of its coded components; to **interpersonal conflict** and **ageing**, the situations it studies;[^s1] and to **wisdom and well-being**, through the companion finding that wise reasoning helps explain older adults' well-being.[^s5]
