# Major life decisions

A major life decision is one of the few choices that bend the shape of a life: leaving a job or a field, moving to another city or country, beginning or ending a relationship, deciding whether to have a child. Such choices share three features that make them hard. The stakes are high, the outcomes are uncertain and often slow to reveal themselves, and the choice may change your preferences, so that the person who decides is not quite the person who lives with the result.[^s2] Wisdom here is less about finding a formula than about deciding well while knowing how much cannot be known.[^s3]

## Origin

People have always agonised over such choices, but two lines of modern work sharpened the question. In philosophy, L. A. Paul argued that some choices, with having a first child as her central case, are *transformative*: they give you an experience you cannot grasp in advance, and they can change what you care about.[^s2] If so, the textbook method of imagining each outcome and weighing how good it would feel cannot really be carried out, because the values it needs are unavailable until after you commit.[^s2] Decision theorists have since debated how much of this problem can be repaired.[^s3] In economics, Steven Levitt ran a large online field experiment in which thousands of people stuck on a decision let a coin toss nudge them, and then reported back months later on what they had done and how they felt.[^s1]

## Why it matters

These are the choices people most often look back on, and they are where ordinary forecasting errors cost most. Across studies of more than 19,000 adults, people of every age recognised how much they had changed over the past decade but expected to change little over the next one, a pattern called the end of history illusion.[^s4] In one study, people said they would pay considerably more to see their current favourite band in ten years than others would pay now to see the band they had loved ten years before, as if today's tastes were permanent.[^s4] A big decision made for a self who is assumed never to change rests on a shaky premise.[^s4]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Separate what can be learned from what can only be lived.** Gather the knowable facts (money, logistics, what others in that life report) while accepting that the felt experience may stay opaque until you are in it.[^s2]
- **Be honest about the real reasons.** Paul warns against telling yourself afterwards that you simply knew how it would turn out; she suggests resting the choice more on values that do not depend on predicting your future feelings, such as moral commitments or the kind of life you want to try.[^s2]
- **Count desire as evidence, carefully.** Villiger argues that wanting a transformative outcome can still be a rational basis for choosing it, provided your past experience and the testimony of others suggest such wanted changes tend to turn out well, and its other merits are at least as good as the alternatives.[^s3]
- **Remember that not choosing is a choice.** Postponement keeps the status quo, which carries its own costs.[^s2][^s1]
- **Commit once you have decided.** In experiments with photographs and posters, people who could not swap their choice came to like it more than people who kept the option to switch, yet most participants preferred the option to switch.[^s5] Endless second-guessing may cost satisfaction.[^s5]

## Pitfalls

The most common error is treating the familiar option as safe by default. In Levitt's experiment, people facing important decisions who were nudged by the coin towards a change, such as quitting a job or ending a relationship, were more likely to make it and reported being happier six months later than those nudged to stay; he concluded that people may be overly cautious about life-changing choices.[^s1] A second error is assuming that only mistakes of action will haunt you. Gilovich and Medvec's influential account held that recent regrets centre on things done and long-term regrets on chances missed; a large public replication found the short-term half of that pattern but not a clear long-term tilt towards inaction regrets, so the popular slogan that you will only regret what you did not do outruns the evidence.[^s6] A third is projecting today's priorities onto a future self who will probably want different things.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is suggestive rather than settled. Levitt's participants chose to visit a coin-flipping website, many were young, educated men drawn by his popular writing, survey follow-up was incomplete, and happiness was self-reported; the result is one study, not a law.[^s1] The changeability findings came from small choices in a lab and may not scale to marriages or careers.[^s5] The philosophical debate about transformative choice remains open.[^s3] None of this is personal advice for a specific case, and if a decision is causing persistent distress or sleeplessness, talking it through with a counsellor or other professional is a sensible step.

## Connections

Major life decisions sit under the broader heading of **decisions under uncertainty**. The difficulty of deciding is **explained by transformative experience**, Paul's account of choices whose value cannot be known in advance,[^s2] and the topic is **studied in decision theory**, where her challenge is debated.[^s3] The tendency to over-favour the status quo is **demonstrated by** Levitt's **coin-toss study**.[^s1] Related practices in this graph include **deliberation** and Franklin's **moral algebra** for weighing options and **seeking counsel** for borrowing others' experience; **self-distancing** and **wise reasoning** help you see your own case as an outsider would. The **end of history illusion** explains why future selves are hard to picture,[^s4] **regret** research explains why the road not taken can loom large,[^s6] and **intellectual courage** names the willingness to act when certainty is not on offer.
