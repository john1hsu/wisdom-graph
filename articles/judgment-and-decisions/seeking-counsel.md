steps:
  - Name the decision and decide whether it is important and uncertain enough to deserve outside counsel.
  - Write down your own current view and how confident you are, before you ask anyone.
  - Choose two or three advisers who know this kind of situation, including at least one who has lived through it and one likely to disagree with you.
  - Ask each one separately and personally, describe the problem without revealing your preferred answer, and ask what they would do and why.
  - Ask anyone who has already been through the same choice how it actually turned out and felt for them.
  - Compare their answers with your own and move your view meaningfully toward the advice where they know more or agree with each other, noting where and why you still differ.
  - Make the decision yourself, take responsibility for it, and later tell your advisers what you decided and how it went.
example:
  situation: A team lead has a job offer from a fast-growing start-up and must answer by Friday. They are excited and have already half-decided to accept.
  before: "I'll ask my best friend, who always backs me, mention the salary first, and take the yes as confirmation."
  after: "I'll write down my own answer first, then ask a former colleague who joined a start-up two years ago how it really went, and a mentor who tends to be cautious. I won't tell them which way I'm leaning, and I'll actually shift my view if they both raise the same worry."
  why: Asking advisers who know the terrain, without steering them, gives the decision a second independent view and the lived experience of someone who has already been there, which people tend to undervalue.

# Seeking counsel

Seeking counsel means deliberately asking other people for their view before an important decision, and then letting what they say actually shape the choice.[^s1][^s2] It differs from collecting reassurance: the wise version involves choosing advisers who know the terrain, asking in a way that does not steer them, and giving their advice genuine weight even when it cuts against your own first answer.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The idea is old and appears in several traditions. In his discussion of deliberation, Aristotle notes that on important questions people call in others to help them deliberate, because they distrust their own ability to decide alone.[^s2] He places this within deliberation about matters whose outcome is uncertain, rather than things that are settled.[^s2] The Hebrew book of Proverbs contrasts plans that fail for lack of counsel with plans that succeed through many advisers (Proverbs 15:22, a variant of 11:14); translators' commentary reads "counsel" here simply as advice or suggestions, and "plans" as ordinary, good-faith schemes.[^s6]

The modern study of advice began in decision research. In the "judge–advisor" set-up, a person makes an estimate, receives someone else's estimate, and then gives a final answer, which lets researchers measure exactly how far people move toward advice.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

A second, independent opinion usually improves accuracy, because averaging separate judgments cancels out some of each person's errors; on that logic, giving advice roughly equal weight to your own view is a sensible benchmark when the adviser is about as competent as you.[^s3] Yet people consistently fall short of it. Ilan Yaniv and Eli Kleinberger called this *egocentric discounting*: we weight our own opinion more than others', partly because we can see our own reasons but not the adviser's.[^s1] They also found that people form impressions of advisers quickly and lose trust in them faster than they gain it.[^s1]

Counsel matters most where our own imagination is weakest. In experiments by Daniel Gilbert and colleagues, students predicted how much they would enjoy a speed date or a peer evaluation more accurately when told only how a previous participant had felt than when given details about the event itself, yet both they and independent judges expected the opposite.[^s4] Those who have already lived through a choice can be better guides to how it will feel than our own forecasts.[^s4]

Fear of looking incompetent keeps many people from asking. In experiments by Alison Wood Brooks, Francesca Gino and Maurice Schweitzer, observers rated people who sought advice as more competent than those who did not, especially when the task was hard, when the observer was the one being asked, and when the adviser had relevant expertise.[^s5]

## How to apply it

The steps above follow the evidence. Writing down your own view first makes it possible to notice whether you moved at all.[^s1] Asking advisers separately and without revealing your preference keeps their judgments independent, which is where the averaging benefit comes from.[^s3] Asking someone who has been through the same choice draws on the surrogate's experience that Gilbert's studies found so useful.[^s4] Asking for help on a genuinely hard problem, and asking a relevant expert directly, is the version least likely to cost you credibility.[^s5] At work this suits hiring, project and career decisions; in personal life it suits choices about a move, a relationship or a large purchase, the kind of important and uncertain questions Aristotle had in mind.[^s2][^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Shopping for agreement.** Asking only people who will back you, or framing the question so the answer is obvious, defeats the purpose, since independent judgment is what makes advice useful.[^s3]
- **Asking, then discounting.** On average people move only about two-fifths of the way toward advice, even when it is described neutrally.[^s3]
- **Overreacting to one bad call.** Because trust in advisers falls faster than it rises, a single miss can lead people to dismiss an adviser who is usually right.[^s1]
- **Ignoring expertise.** Seeking advice from someone without relevant expertise earns less of the competence boost than asking an expert.[^s5]
- **Asking about the wrong things.** Aristotle places counsel in deliberation about important matters whose outcome is uncertain, not settled questions or trivial ones.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The core finding of egocentric discounting is well established, so the evidence for the problem is strong; the evidence that a given person will decide better by asking is more conditional, hence *mixed*.[^s1][^s3] A 2023 meta-analysis of 129 datasets from 53 articles (17,296 participants) estimated that people shift about 39% toward advice, rising to 48% when it is described as high quality and to about 55% for uncertain estimates; it also detected publication bias.[^s3] Almost all of those studies used anonymous, computerised number-estimation tasks, few allowed people to choose whether to seek advice, and few involved several advisers, so how well they generalise to real personal decisions is uncertain.[^s3] The surrogation studies used small student samples and brief events.[^s4] The competence finding rests mainly on one paper's experiments.[^s5]

## Connections

Seeking counsel is a part of **deliberation**, the weighing of uncertain, important choices that Aristotle described, and it is **applied in major life decisions**.[^s2] It **addresses egocentric discounting**, the tendency to underweight others' views,[^s1] and is **explained by the wisdom of crowds**, the error-cancelling effect of combining independent judgments.[^s3] Its value for predicting how a choice will feel is **demonstrated by the neighbourly-advice study**.[^s4] It is **rooted in Hebrew wisdom literature**, whose proverbs praise many advisers.[^s6] It **requires intellectual humility**, the willingness to distrust one's own judgment that Aristotle names.[^s2] It is related to **self-distancing** and **perspective-taking**, which bring an outsider's view inside your own head, and to **Socratic ignorance**, the recognition of what one does not know.[^s1][^s4]
