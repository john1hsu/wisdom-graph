steps:
  - Check that the goal is one you have actually decided on and still endorse; if you are lukewarm about it, deliberate first, because a plan cannot supply commitment you do not have.
  - Identify the critical moment, either the opportunity to act (a time, a place, a recurring event) or the obstacle that usually derails you (a temptation, a distraction, a feeling).
  - Write one plan per cue in the form "If [specific situation], then I will [specific action]", making the cue concrete enough that you will recognise it and the action small enough to start at once.
  - For your usual obstacle, add a shielding plan, such as "If I feel the urge to check messages, then I will note it and keep writing until the timer ends."
  - Say or rehearse the plan a few times so the cue and the response are firmly linked, and remove anything in the setting that competes with it.
  - Afterwards, review whether the cue appeared and the plan fired; revise the plan if circumstances have genuinely changed, not merely because keeping it felt hard.

# Implementation intentions

An implementation intention is a small, specific plan that links a future situation to a response: when a named cue arises, you will do a named thing. The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer distinguished it from a goal intention ("I intend to finish the report"), which commits you to an outcome but leaves open when, where and how you will pursue it. An implementation intention is subordinate to the goal and takes an if-then form, which he wrote as "When situation x arises, I will perform response y!"[^s1] It is one of the best-studied answers to an old problem in the wisdom literature: knowing what is best and still not doing it.[^s2]

## Origin

Gollwitzer, a researcher on goal pursuit, set out the idea in a 1999 *American Psychologist* review. He reported that simple if-then plans substantially raised completion rates for tasks that people found hard to get started on, such as writing a report over a holiday break, while adding little for easy tasks. Similar plans helped people carry out unpleasant health behaviours such as regular self-examinations and exercise.[^s1] A 2006 meta-analysis with Paschal Sheeran pooled 94 independent tests and put the average effect on goal attainment at a medium-to-large d = 0.65.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Practical wisdom is not only judging well but acting on the judgment. Aristotle's *akrasia*, or weakness of will, names the gap between the two. Psychologists study the same gap as the distance between holding a goal intention and actually attaining the goal.[^s2] Implementation intentions target that gap directly. Gollwitzer and Sheeran found that they help with four recurring problems: getting started, staying on track when distractions or temptations arise, disengaging from a failing course, and not exhausting yourself for later goals.[^s2] The proposed mechanism is that choosing a cue in advance makes it easier to notice, and linking it to a response lets the response start with little deliberate effort. Gollwitzer called this "strategic automaticity": you hand control of the moment to the situation you planned for, instead of relying on willpower when it is weakest.[^s1]

This may explain why, in early studies, plans helped even people whose self-control was impaired, such as people in drug withdrawal, and why they helped frontal-lobe patients with the weakest conscious control most.[^s1] At work, a planning prompt can be as small as a line on a form. In a field experiment with 3,272 employees, a mailer asking people to write down the date and time they would get a free flu shot raised vaccination by about 4 percentage points over a 33 per cent baseline. A date-only plan had a smaller, non-significant effect, which suggests that specificity matters.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Start from a goal you have genuinely decided on, because plans add little when the underlying goal is weak or abandoned.[^s1] Then choose the critical cue: an opportunity to act, or the obstacle that usually trips you up. A 2025 meta-analysis of 642 tests found that plans worked better when they used a true contingent if-then format, when people were highly motivated, and when they rehearsed the plan.[^s3] Plans that tell you to ignore a distraction when it arises often outperform plans that only spell out the task, so write one for your usual temptation as well as one for starting.[^s1] Keep each response small and immediate, rehearse it, and review afterwards whether the plan fired, as you would in an after-action review or an evening review.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Planning instead of deciding.** An if-then plan multiplies commitment that already exists. It does not create it, and plans for goals people do not hold do little.[^s1]
- **Vague cues.** "When I have time" is not a cue you will notice. The flu study's time-specific plans outperformed date-only ones.[^s4]
- **Mistaking firmness for rigidity.** Gollwitzer notes that plans make you less flexible when the cue appears, which is usually the point.[^s1] The philosopher Richard Holton offers a useful line here. Strength of will means not reopening a sound resolution merely because temptation has arrived. Clinging to a trivial one against good reasons is stubbornness, not virtue.[^s6]
- **Too many plans at once.** Effects depend on a clear link between one cue and one response, and on rehearsing it.[^s3]

## Evidence

The core effect has been tested hundreds of times across cognitive, emotional and behavioural outcomes, with effects ranging from about d = 0.27 to 0.66.[^s3] That breadth is real, but the size is probably smaller in everyday conditions than the headline figures suggest. Later reviews report smaller effects for physical activity, and the combined practice of mental contrasting with implementation intentions shows clear signs of publication bias, with an adjusted effect of g = 0.24.[^s5] Field effects, as in the vaccination study, are modest per person even when they are valuable at scale.[^s4] The fair conclusion is that if-then planning reliably helps, by a small to moderate amount, people who already want to act. It is not a cure for ambivalence. The status here is therefore *mixed*.[^s3][^s5]

## Connections

- It **addresses** weakness of will, because the 2006 meta-analysis found that if-then plans raise goal attainment beyond what a goal intention alone achieves.[^s2] It also addresses **procrastination**, the problem of getting started, in Gollwitzer's original studies.[^s1]
- It **develops** self-control in the broad sense of regulating thoughts, feelings and actions.[^s3]
- It was **introduced by** Peter Gollwitzer and is **explained by** strategic automaticity.[^s1]
- It is **demonstrated by** the Gollwitzer and Sheeran meta-analysis and by the flu-vaccination planning-prompt field experiment.[^s2][^s4]
- It **complements** mental contrasting, which strengthens commitment by setting a desired future against present obstacles before the if-then plan is made.[^s5]
- It follows good deliberation: deliberation settles what to do, and the plan settles when and how, so the resolution is not reopened at the moment temptation arrives.[^s1][^s6] The evening review and after-action review are natural places to check whether a plan fired and whether it still deserves keeping.[^s3]
