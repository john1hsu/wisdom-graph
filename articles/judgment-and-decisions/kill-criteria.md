steps:
  - When you commit to a goal or project, write down two to four signals that would show it is failing, each paired with a date by which you will check it.
  - Make each signal concrete and, where you can, numeric, such as a minimum level of sales, sign-ups, savings or progress, so that someone else could tell whether it has been met.
  - Set a budget for the effort as well as the money, counting hours, weekends and favours, because time spent tends to escape the books.
  - Share the criteria with someone who had no stake in starting the project and ask them to hold you to them, or to make the stop-or-go call themselves.
  - On each review date, compare results with the criteria and ask whether, starting from scratch today, you would begin this.
  - When a criterion is tripped, treat stopping as the default; continue only if you can write down new information that justifies it, not the cost already sunk.
  - When you stop, record what you learned, give credit for the progress made, and name the next goal the freed time and money will go to.
example:
  situation: A café owner started a weekday lunch-delivery service three months ago, buying a used van and training two drivers. Orders are well below what she hoped and the service loses money every week.
  before: "We've bought the van and trained the drivers, so stopping now would waste all that. Let's push on until the summer and see if word of mouth picks up."
  after: "When we launched I wrote that we'd stop if we weren't at forty orders a day by the end of month three. We're at eighteen. I'll end deliveries next week, sell the van, and put the two most-ordered dishes on the counter menu."
  why: The first response weighs what has been spent; the second applies a test set before any results came in, when judgment was not yet coloured by investment and pride, and pairs the exit with a next step.
selfCheck:
  - Before I started, did I write down what result, by what date, would tell me to stop?
  - Is each criterion concrete enough that someone else could tell whether it has been met?
  - Now that a criterion has been tripped, is my reason for carrying on new information, or what I have already put in?
  - Am I counting my time and effort against the budget, not only the money?
  - Who without a stake in the original decision could check this call?

# Kill criteria

Kill criteria are the conditions, decided before the results come in, under which you will stop a goal or project. The decision researcher and former poker player Annie Duke compares them to the "unlesses" experienced players carry to the table: they will keep playing unless they lose a set amount, the game gets tougher, or they become tired or emotional.[^s4] The practice moves the hardest part of quitting, deciding what counts as failure, to the moment when judgment is least clouded by money spent, effort made and pride at stake.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Researchers studying escalation of commitment, the tendency to keep backing a losing course of action, tested the idea long before it had a catchy name. In a 1992 experiment, Itamar Simonson and Barry Staw asked business students to play a manager deciding whether to keep funding a failing product; those who first wrote down the minimum sales and profit below which they would count their choice a mistake later put about $1.2 million less of a $10 million budget back into it than a baseline group.[^s1] Simonson and Staw recommended that whoever starts a project spell out the targets which, if missed, will lead to a change of course.[^s1] Duke's 2023 advice for leaders uses the term *kill criteria* for the same move and adds regular reviews of a goal's costs and benefits.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Goals focus effort, but having a goal can itself keep people going past the point where it still makes sense, and cultures that prize grit make quitting feel like failure.[^s4] Once a project is under way, bad news is easy to explain away. Simonson and Staw argue that targets set in advance make negative evidence harder to read as ambiguous, make withdrawal more legitimate and prompt people to think about backup plans.[^s1] Chip Heath's work points to a second mechanism: people keep mental budgets for their investments and resist spending past them, so a clear budget acts as a natural stopping point, whereas spending that is hard to track, especially time and effort, slips past it and fuels escalation.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Set the criteria at the same time as the goal, and set the review dates with them.[^s4] Good criteria name an observable state and a date: a minimum level of adoption, revenue or progress by the end of a given month.[^s1] Budget the time and effort as well as the money, since investments that are hard to book against a budget are the ones people keep making.[^s3] Ask someone outside the original decision to hold you to the rule, or to make the stop-or-go call: in a study of new-product decisions, handing the decision to a fresh manager was about as effective as an analyst-supplied stopping rule, and both worked better than risk analysis alone.[^s2] When a team runs a premortem, imagining how a project might fail, the failure modes it lists are a natural source of kill criteria.[^s4] And when you do stop, give credit for the lessons and milestones reached, so that people are not punished for quitting at the right time.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

**Vague criteria.** In the new-product study, participants who wrote their own stopping rules often wrote rules too ambiguous to apply, and none of them withdrew from the failing product.[^s2] "If it isn't working" is not a criterion.

**Treating the rule as a suggestion.** Even among participants who accepted a precise rule supplied by an analyst, about half kept the product when the rule said to withdraw.[^s2] A tripped criterion should shift the burden of proof onto continuing.

**Counting only money.** Time, weekends and goodwill are rarely posted to the budget, which is why "just a bit more effort" so often goes unchecked.[^s3]

**Quitting too readily.** Budgets cut both ways: Heath found people also refused profitable further investments once sunk costs were high, and suggested premature de-escalation may be as common in practice as escalation.[^s3] Kill criteria should mark when a goal is failing, not when it is merely hard; a rule set on a poor forecast can be wrong, and some participants who followed their own rules still kept products that should have been dropped.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is encouraging but partial. In Simonson and Staw's experiment, setting minimum targets was one of the three most effective techniques, alongside accountability for the decision process and reducing threat to ego, while listing pros and cons had no effect and accountability for outcomes increased escalation.[^s1] In Boulding and colleagues' studies, precommitment to a supplied stopping rule raised withdrawal from a failing product from none in the base condition to 43 percent, yet still fell short of the 66 percent of a control group who saw no history of the project, so the bias was reduced, not removed.[^s2] These were short role-play experiments with small groups of participants, such as business students playing managers, so the effects in real organisations and personal lives are unknown.[^s1][^s2] Duke's advice for leaders is practitioner guidance rather than a tested programme.[^s4]

## Connections

Kill criteria **develop** the skill of **knowing when to quit**, which Duke presents as a core skill for leaders and poker players alike;[^s4] she has helped **popularise** the term.[^s4] The practice **addresses escalation of commitment**, as Simonson and Staw's **de-escalation strategies study** **demonstrated**,[^s1] and it is **supported by** Boulding and colleagues' **new-product stopping-rules study**, with the caveats above.[^s2] Heath's work on budgets **explains** part of how it works through **mental accounting**.[^s3] It is **applied** by people **leading others** who must decide which projects and initiatives to wind down.[^s4] It sits close to **satisficing**, which likewise fixes a standard before searching; to **implementation intentions**, another if-then plan made in advance; to **seeking counsel** and the **after-action review**, which bring outside eyes and lessons to the decision; and to **regret** and **major life decisions**, where stopping is hardest.
