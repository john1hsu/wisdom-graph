# Ethical lenses framework

The ethical lenses framework is a short, structured way to think through a decision with moral stakes. Developed at the Markkula Center for Applied Ethics at Santa Clara University, it moves through five stages: recognise the ethical issue, get the facts, evaluate the options, choose and test a course of action, then act and reflect on the outcome.[^s1][^s2] Its distinctive middle step looks at each option through six "lenses", each drawn from a major strand of moral thought, so that no single consideration quietly decides the matter.[^s1] The Center presents it as an introductory tool for deliberation, not a procedure that produces answers.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The framework grew out of the Center's essay *Thinking Ethically* by the philosopher Manuel Velasquez and colleagues, which updated earlier pieces by Velasquez and Claire Andre. That essay set out five classic approaches and traced them to their sources: the utilitarian approach to Bentham and Mill, the rights approach to Kant, fairness or justice to Aristotle, the common good to Plato, Aristotle, Cicero and Rawls, and the virtue approach to the tradition that asks what character we should develop.[^s3] Later revisions by a wider group of Center scholars added a sixth lens, care ethics, which stresses relationships and the particular circumstances of the people involved, and set the lenses inside a five-stage decision process.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Many poor decisions are never recognised as ethical at all. The framework puts recognition first: before weighing anything, ask whether the choice could hurt someone, benefit people unevenly, or involve a choice between goods or between bads, and whether it is about more than legality or efficiency.[^s1] That second question directly targets the habit of letting a moral choice pass as a technical or business one. The Center also suggests thinking ahead about the dilemmas a given role will predictably bring, such as who owns an idea for an entrepreneur or favouritism for a public official, so they are recognised when they arrive.[^s2] Research supports treating recognition as its own capacity: Bebeau, Rest and Yamoor's dental ethical sensitivity test found that students' scores for recognising hidden ethical issues were distinct from their scores for moral reasoning.[^s6]

The lenses then guard against a second failure, judging by one value alone. An option that maximises overall benefit can still trample someone's rights; one that is fair on paper can ignore a relationship of trust. Seeing where the lenses disagree is part of the point.[^s1]

## How to use it

- **Recognise the issue.** Ask the opening question plainly: "Could this decision be damaging to someone?"[^s2]
- **Get the facts.** Separate what is known from what is not, list who has a stake and whose concerns weigh most, consult the relevant people, and look for creative options.[^s1]
- **Evaluate through the lenses.** For each option, ask which best respects everyone's rights, treats people fairly, produces the most good and least harm, serves the whole community, makes you the person you want to be, and attends to the relationships and feelings of those involved.[^s1]
- **Choose and test.** Pick the option that holds up best overall, then imagine explaining it to someone you respect or to a public audience, and ask how it can be carried out with the most care.[^s1]
- **Act and reflect.** Afterwards, ask how it turned out, what you learned and what follow-up is needed.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a calculator.** The approaches identify considerations; they do not settle them, and people must still deliberate.[^s3]
- **Lens-shopping.** People disagree about what each lens contains, such as which rights exist or what counts as a harm, and the lenses can point in different directions. Picking the one that flatters a preferred option defeats the exercise.[^s1]
- **Mistaking other guides for ethics.** The framework warns that feelings, law, religion, cultural norms and science each inform ethical choices but cannot stand in for them: a practice can be legal, customary or technically possible and still be wrong.[^s1]
- **Skipping the facts.** The essay notes that checking the facts is the first step and is often overlooked.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The framework itself has not been tested as a specific intervention, so its status here is **practitioner** advice built on long-established normative theories.[^s1][^s3] The broader evidence on ethics instruction is encouraging but uneven. A meta-analysis of 66 studies with more than 10,000 participants found sizeable benefits from ethics instruction in the sciences, with effectiveness improving over the preceding decade.[^s4] A companion meta-analysis of business ethics instruction found professional, focused, workshop-based programmes especially effective, but reported considerable problems with many of the criteria used to judge such programmes.[^s5] Neither tests this framework directly, and gains on a course measure are not the same as wiser decisions under real pressure.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

The framework **develops moral awareness** through its recognition stage and **develops deliberation** through its structured weighing of options,[^s1] and it **addresses ethical fading** by asking whether an issue is about more than legality or efficiency.[^s1] It is **applied in** professional life, where the Center urges anticipating each role's predictable dilemmas.[^s2] It was **introduced by** Manuel Velasquez and colleagues and is **influenced by** virtue ethics and consequentialism, among other classic approaches,[^s3] and by **care ethics**.[^s1] It is weakly **supported by** meta-analytic evidence that ethics instruction can work.[^s4] It sits under **deliberation** and applied ethics, and is related to **moral case deliberation** (a group method for similar questions), **moral algebra**, **seeking counsel** and the **Golden Rule**, which echo its publicity and stakeholder tests, to **perspective-taking**, which the care lens relies on, and to the **after-action review**, which mirrors its final stage of reflection.[^s1]
