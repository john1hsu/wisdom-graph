steps:
  - Name the decision and choose a horizon year far enough ahead (often 25–40 years) that most people in the room will no longer be in charge.
  - Ask one person, or a separate group, to imagine they actually live in that year and to speak as its residents, staff or customers, rather than simply forecasting.
  - Have the future side describe what the place is like and what they wish today's decision-makers had done, then list their priorities from that standpoint.
  - Have the present side list its priorities from today's needs, without trying to anticipate the future group.
  - Bring both sides together to negotiate one shared plan, and record where the future side changed it and where it was overruled and why.
  - Rotate the role in later sessions so that everyone has spoken for the future at least once, and end by writing down reasons and advice for your successors.
example:
  situation: A department head is drafting next year's budget and is tempted to cut the apprenticeship programme, whose payoff arrives only after the current team has moved on.
  before: "Cut it. Nobody in this meeting will be here when those apprentices are senior, and we need the money for this year's targets."
  after: "Before deciding, ask two colleagues to speak as the department's staff in 2045: what will they need from us, and what would they say about this cut? Then weigh their case against this year's pressures, and write down why we chose what we chose."
  why: The first response counts only the people present. The second gives the people who will inherit the decision a voice in the room and leaves a record they can judge us by, without pretending that their interests automatically win.
selfCheck:
  - Who will live with this decision after I have moved on, and has anyone in the room spoken for them?
  - If I were a member of that future group, what would I ask today's decision-makers to protect?
  - Am I treating my guess about future people's wishes as a certainty, or as one voice to weigh?
  - Have I written down my reasons so that my successors can see and judge them?

# Imaginary future generations

The imaginary-future-generations method asks some people in a meeting to step into the shoes of people who will live decades from now and to argue for them. It is the signature technique of Future Design, a research and policy movement that studies how to design social systems so that today's decision-makers leave sustainable environments and societies to those who come after.[^s4] Rather than assuming that people's time horizons are fixed, Future Design tries to change how they think by giving the future a seat at the table.[^s4]

## Origin

The economist Tatsuyoshi Saijo and colleagues began Future Design in Japan in 2012.[^s4] Saijo names as an inspiration the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy, whose leaders, he writes, were said to consider the effect of major decisions seven generations ahead.[^s4] The method was first tested in a laboratory game in which successive three-person "generations" choose between taking more money now, which shrinks what every later generation receives, and a sustainable option that keeps the pot stable.[^s1] When one member of each group was asked to negotiate as an imaginary future person, 60% chose the sustainable option, compared with 28% when no one spoke for the future.[^s1] Saijo also proposes a trait he calls "futurability": the sense of being happier when one gives up a present benefit to enrich future people.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Philosophers of intergenerational justice point out what makes the future so easy to neglect: there is no reciprocity between non-contemporaries, and power runs one way, since the living can shape future people's lives while future people can do nothing to the living.[^s6] Future people are never in the meeting, so even well-meaning groups tend to give them less than they give people who are present. Imaginary future generations is a way of correcting for that absence in the moment of decision rather than relying on good intentions afterwards.[^s1] It turns stewardship, the virtue of holding something in trust for those who come later, into a concrete group habit.

## How to apply it

The best-documented application is in the town of Yahaba, in northern Japan. In 2015–16 about twenty residents met for five sessions to draft a vision for the town in 2060; two groups were told to think as residents of 2060 and two as residents of today, and the four then paired off to negotiate a shared top-ten list of measures.[^s2] The future groups emphasised local resources and slow, hard problems, while the present groups focused on unmet current needs; in the end six or seven of each pair's ten measures came from the future groups' proposals.[^s2] Explanation alone did not move people: the groups clashed over free childcare and medical care, which the future groups opposed because of the cost to later residents, and one future group accepted it only with restrictions.[^s2] A related step, writing down reasons and advice for the next generation before deciding, also increased sustainable choices in a separate laboratory study of 312 students.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Mistaking the role for a forecast.** Participants are asked to imagine actually living in the future, not to predict it; in Yahaba, people who played the role described it as enjoyable and eye-opening.[^s2]
- **Treating the imagined voice as the real one.** We cannot identify future individuals or know their preferences in any detail, so an imaginary representative is offering an informed guess to be weighed, not a mandate.[^s6]
- **Expecting agreement without negotiation.** Present and future priorities really do conflict, and the method works by surfacing that conflict and bargaining over it, not by making it disappear.[^s2]
- **Running it once.** Hara and colleagues found that perspectives shifted across a sequence of sessions, with participants alternating between present and future roles.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is promising but limited and context-dependent, so its status here is *mixed*. In a three-session follow-up in Yahaba with 26 randomly selected residents, agreement that "the people of today are responsible" for the town's problems rose after people had played the future role, and those who could hold both the present and the future view in mind gave more weight to leaving room for later decisions.[^s3] But that study had no control group, a fixed session order, self-reported outcomes and only a handful of participants in the key subgroup.[^s3] In a field experiment in Bangladesh, an imaginary future person raised sustainable choices in rural Bogra but not in urban Dhaka.[^s4] Most studies so far come from Saijo's network and many used students; the movement's own review acknowledges that the mechanism is still unclear.[^s4]

## Connections

The practice **addresses intergenerational discounting**, the tendency to give future recipients less than present ones, as the laboratory negotiation study shows; it is **demonstrated by** that **negotiating-with-the-future study**.[^s1] It may help **develop stewardship**: playing the future role was followed by a stronger sense of present responsibility, though the evidence is from one small case study.[^s3] It was **introduced by Tatsuyoshi Saijo**, and its context-dependence is **challenged by** the **Bangladesh study** in which it had no effect in the city.[^s4] It has been **applied in community decision-making**, most fully in Yahaba's long-term planning.[^s2] It is a structured form of **perspective-taking** and **deliberation**, extended across time; like **self-distancing**, it gains clarity by stepping outside one's own vantage point, and like **seeking counsel**, it brings a missing voice into a decision.[^s2][^s6]
