steps:
  - Use the method only for an important choice that is up to you and genuinely two-sided, not for a matter of taste or a change you have already committed to.
  - Frame one specific option (for example, "accept the transfer") and divide a page into two columns headed Pro and Con.
  - Over three or four days, add a short note to either column whenever a reason occurs to you, instead of trying to list everything in one sitting.
  - Bring all the reasons into view and give each a rough weight, such as 1 to 3.
  - Strike out pairs of reasons of equal weight on opposite sides, and let one strong reason cancel two or three weaker ones that together equal it.
  - Ask whether any single remaining reason is decisive on its own, or whether a reason you never wrote down is the one actually driving you.
  - Wait a day or two; if no important new reason appears, decide by the balance and act on it.

  situation: A project manager is offered a two-year posting to the company's office in another city and has to reply within a week.
  before: "It sounds exciting and I hate saying no to leadership. I'll accept in tomorrow's one-to-one and sort out the details later."
  after: "I'll keep a Pro and Con page on my phone for the next four days, weigh the reasons on Friday, and see what's left after cancelling the equal ones. If the only big thing on the Con side is my partner's job, that's a conversation to have before I answer, not a line to cancel out."

# Moral algebra

Moral algebra is Benjamin Franklin's name for a simple way of thinking through a hard choice. You write the reasons for and against in two columns over several days, estimate how much each one weighs, cancel reasons of equal weight on opposite sides, and decide by whatever balance remains.[^s1] Franklin did not claim it made judgment exact. He claimed that it helped him judge better and made him less likely to take a rash step.[^s1]

## Origin

In 1772 the chemist and minister Joseph Priestley was offered a post as librarian and companion to Lord Shelburne. The salary and patronage were attractive, but Priestley was settled and productive in Leeds, so he asked Franklin for advice.[^s1] Franklin declined to tell him what to do and instead described a method, which he called "prudential Algebra" in that letter.[^s1] The difficulty with hard decisions, he explained, is that the reasons for and against are not all present to the mind at the same time. Different ones come up at different moments, so whichever happens to be uppermost tends to win.[^s1] His remedy was to collect the reasons on paper over three or four days, weigh them, and strike them out against each other until the balance became clear.[^s1] Priestley took several months to decide, and the editors of Franklin's papers note that Franklin later recommended the same method to his relative Jonathan Williams Jr.[^s1] Franklin's later description of it as "Moral Algebra" gave the method its usual name.[^s4] The psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer and the philosopher Thomas Sturm treat it as the classic model of decision by weighing and adding all the reasons.[^s4]

A famous relative of the method is Charles Darwin's 1838 note headed "This is the Question". In it he set the advantages of marrying, such as children, companionship and a home, against those of staying single, such as freedom and time for his work. He concluded that he should marry, and later that year he proposed to Emma Wedgwood.[^s2] Darwin's list has no weights, and the note ends by suggesting he trust to chance, a reminder that a list organises a decision without removing its uncertainty.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Moral algebra gives deliberation a concrete form. It answers a specific failure: whatever reason is most vivid at the moment of choosing gets too much weight, so the decision follows the latest mood rather than the whole picture.[^s1]

Research on prediction suggests why such crude bookkeeping can help. Robyn Dawes reviewed studies in which an equal-weighted sum of the relevant factors, chosen by people, beat the holistic judgments of the average expert in every case he tabulated.[^s3] In one example, a simple sum of test scores, grades and college quality predicted graduate students' later success better than the admissions committee's own ratings did.[^s3] People, he concluded, are good at picking out what matters but poor at combining many factors in their heads.[^s3] His studies tested statistical prediction rather than personal lists, but they support moral algebra's core idea: write the reasons down and let simple arithmetic combine them.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Follow the steps above. Save the method for decisions that are important, uncertain and yours to make, such as a job move, a major purchase or a change of direction for a team.[^s1] Keep the page open for several days, and treat the weights as honest rough estimates, as Franklin did, not as precise numbers.[^s1] At work, the same logic suits recurring judgments such as hiring or selection: agree on the few criteria that matter, score each one, and add the scores, instead of relying on an overall impression.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Counting instead of weighing.** The longer column does not win. Franklin's method cancels reasons by their weight, so one heavy reason can outweigh several light ones.[^s1]
- **Letting the negatives swamp the list.** In online studies with more than 1,200 participants, Aaron Snyder and Zakary Tormala found that a few negatives raise feelings of conflict more than an equal number of positives do. A handful of cons can make a mostly good option feel torn.[^s6]
- **Forcing a full ledger where one reason should decide.** Gigerenzer and Sturm argue that under high uncertainty and with little past experience to go on, simple rules that rely on the single best reason can match or beat weighing everything.[^s4] Sometimes one reason really is decisive, and the list's job is to show you which one it is.[^s4]
- **Using it on a decision already made.** William Miller and Gary Rose review studies in which listing pros and cons lowered commitment among people who were still ambivalent about a change. In people who had already decided, the same exercise tended to strengthen commitment.[^s5] They recommend the exercise mainly when you want to stay neutral between options.[^s5]
- **Mistaking the page for certainty.** Darwin's list settled his question, yet he still ended by leaving the outcome to chance.[^s2] Any list is only as good as the reasons on it.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

No controlled trial has tested Franklin's procedure as he described it, so its standing is **mixed**: indirect support from simple sums of well-chosen factors,[^s3] qualified by the heuristics research and the decisional-balance findings above.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Moral algebra **develops deliberation**, giving its weighing a working procedure.[^s1] It **addresses impetuosity**, acting on passion without deliberating, which Franklin had in view when he said it made a rash step less likely.[^s1] It was **introduced by Benjamin Franklin** and is **applied in major life decisions** such as Priestley's career move.[^s1] It is **supported by Dawes's study of improper linear models**,[^s3] **contrasts with fast and frugal heuristics** that decide on one good reason,[^s4] and is **challenged by Miller and Rose's review of decisional balance**.[^s5] **Seeking counsel** is a natural companion step, since an adviser may add reasons you have missed. Unlike **rumination**, which circles a problem without settling it, the method has a built-in end: when no new reasons appear, you decide.[^s1]
