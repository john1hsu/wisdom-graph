# Decisions under uncertainty

A decision under uncertainty is one where you cannot list every way things might turn out, or cannot put trustworthy odds on the outcomes you can list. It differs from a decision under risk, such as a dice game or an insurance table, where the chances are known or can be estimated from many similar cases.[^s1][^s2] Most choices that call for wisdom, such as taking a new job or backing a project in a forming market, are of the first kind.[^s2][^s4] Wisdom here is not a method for removing uncertainty; it is the skill of acting well while being honest about how much cannot be known.[^s3]

## Origin

The modern distinction goes back to the economist Frank Knight, who in 1921 proposed keeping the word *risk* for measurable uncertainty and *uncertainty* for the unmeasurable kind.[^s1] For Knight the difference lay in grouping: many similar cases reveal a spread of outcomes, but a unique situation offers no such group, so judgment must stand in for calculation.[^s1] Daniel Ellsberg's later urn puzzle sharpened the point: in an urn whose mix of colours is partly unknown, many people prefer bets on the known proportion, a pattern that no single set of precise probabilities can produce and that decision theorists now call ambiguity aversion.[^s5] Wisdom researchers reached a parallel conclusion from another direction. The Berlin wisdom paradigm made "recognition and management of uncertainty" one of its five criteria of wise judgment, grounded in the limits of human information processing, our partial access to reality and the unknowability of the future.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Treating an uncertain situation as if it were merely risky is a common and costly error. Courtney, Kirkland and Viguerie argued that managers who force a single forecast understate uncertainty and miss threats and opportunities, while those who abandon analysis swing to pure gut feel; both errors treat uncertainty as all or nothing.[^s6] Mousavi and Gigerenzer add that tools built for risk, such as optimisation over estimated probabilities, can perform worse than simpler rules when the environment is uncertain, because they fit noise in limited past data.[^s2] A recent account in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* goes further and defines wisdom itself as the capacities for intractable problems, including radically uncertain, novel or goal-ambiguous ones.[^s4]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Name the kind of uncertainty.** Ask whether the future is reasonably clear, splits into a few distinct scenarios, spans a continuous range, or is truly ambiguous, and match the tools to the level instead of defaulting to one forecast.[^s6]
- **Hold your knowledge loosely.** Intellectual humility, awareness of what you do and do not know, and scenario flexibility, imagining different ways a situation could unfold, are the metacognitive strategies that wisdom researchers single out for this terrain.[^s4][^s3]
- **Prefer robust and reversible moves.** No-regrets moves pay off in every plausible scenario, and small options such as pilots or limited partnerships capture upside while capping the loss; big bets suit only situations where you can live with the downside.[^s6]
- **Use simple rules where data are thin.** Under uncertainty, heuristics that ignore some information, such as splitting resources evenly or relying on the single best cue, can match or beat complex models, provided the rule fits the environment.[^s2]
- **Borrow other eyes.** Seeking out other perspectives and deferring to people who know more about a domain help compensate for the narrowness of any one view.[^s4]
- **Accept that some options will stay incomparable.** When evidence is scarce, it can be rational to suspend judgment for a while, or to admit that two options cannot be ranked precisely, rather than inventing false precision.[^s5]

## Pitfalls

The first pitfall is false precision: dressing a guess up as a probability, then trusting the number because it looks scientific.[^s1][^s2] The second is paralysis, waiting for a certainty that will never arrive; Courtney and colleagues note that even in the murkiest situations people usually know something useful, and that truly ambiguous situations tend to become more tractable over time if you watch for signals.[^s6] The third is overconfidence in one's own narrative, which wisdom researchers treat as the opposite of the humble, multi-perspective reasoning that uncertain problems require.[^s4][^s3] A fourth is reading the heuristics findings as licence to trust any hunch; the claim is only that particular simple rules suit particular environments.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The core distinction between risk and uncertainty is conceptual and widely accepted, though philosophers still debate how best to model ambiguity, and several imprecise-probability decision rules struggle to fit the choices people actually make in Ellsberg's urn.[^s5] The finding that simple heuristics can outperform complex models comes from forecasting and classification studies and holds under specifiable conditions, such as small samples, many options and high unpredictability, so it is a conditional result rather than a general law.[^s2] Wisdom research treats the recognition of uncertainty as a core feature of wise judgment, but this rests on expert ratings and theory more than on experiments showing that training this habit improves real outcomes.[^s3][^s4] Long-running uncertainty about health, money or safety can also be stressful; when worry about the unknown becomes persistent or overwhelming, talking with a doctor, counsellor or other professional is a sensible step, and this article is not advice for any particular case.

## Connections

Decisions under uncertainty **contrast with decisions under risk**, the distinction Frank Knight drew in 1921, and the idea of unmeasurable uncertainty was **introduced by Frank Knight**.[^s1] The topic is **studied in decision theory**, where the Ellsberg paradox and imprecise probabilities model choices with unknown odds,[^s5] and **studied in the psychology of wisdom**, where the Berlin wisdom paradigm counts managing uncertainty as a criterion of wise judgment.[^s3] It sits under **practical wisdom**, the capacity to act well when rules run out. Related entries include **intellectual humility** and **perspective-taking**, the metacognitive habits that uncertain problems demand,[^s4] **wise reasoning**, which acknowledges uncertainty and one's own fallibility,[^s4] **fast-and-frugal heuristics** and **satisficing**, simple strategies suited to thin information,[^s2] and **seeking counsel** and **deliberation**, which widen and slow judgment.
