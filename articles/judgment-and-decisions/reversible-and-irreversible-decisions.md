# Reversible and irreversible decisions

Some choices are like walking through a door you can step back out of; others are like a door that locks behind you. The principle of matching deliberation to reversibility says that most of the care in deciding should go to the second kind. Choices that are cheap to undo can be made quickly, tried and corrected, while the few that are costly or impossible to reverse deserve slower thought, more information and other people's views.[^s5] Research adds an important twist: the option to change your mind is not free, because people tend to like a choice less while it remains open.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The idea has two roots. In economics, Kenneth Arrow and Anthony Fisher showed in 1974 that when the costs of a step are uncertain and its effects cannot be undone, as with environmental damage, that uncertainty reduces the expected net benefit of taking it, so efficient policy leans towards restraint.[^s4] In management, the same intuition was popularised through the image of doors you can and cannot walk back through. Amazon's 2016 shareholder letter, for example, argues that "many decisions are reversible, two-way doors," that these call for a light, fast process, and that most decisions can be taken with roughly 70 per cent of the information one would like, because waiting for near-certainty is usually too slow.[^s5]

## Why it matters

A common failure of judgment is uniform caution: treating every choice as momentous, or none of them. Spending weeks on a decision that could be revised next month wastes time and blocks learning, since trying something is often the fastest way to find out whether it works. Treating a hard-to-undo commitment as routine does the opposite damage, and its consequences last.[^s5] Under real uncertainty, irreversibility is what turns a mistake from a lesson into a lasting cost, which is why it deserves extra weight.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Before deciding, estimate what it would cost, in money, time, trust or relationships, to undo the choice if it proves wrong, and let the answer set the effort: a quick choice and a review date for the reversible, slower deliberation, more evidence and counsel for the irreversible.[^s5][^s4] Where you can, turn one-way doors into two-way ones by piloting, trialling or committing in stages. But once a reversible choice has been made and given a fair test, consider closing the door deliberately. In Gilbert and Ebert's experiments, students who could still swap a photograph they had chosen ended up liking it less than students whose choice was final, yet most people predicted no difference and two-thirds preferred to keep the option open.[^s1] Setting a date after which you stop reconsidering lets a choice settle.[^s3]

## Common misreadings

The principle is not "reversible decisions don't matter". Many small reversible choices add up, and something that is reversible in principle may be costly to reverse in practice, such as a public promise or a team's trust.[^s5] Nor is it "keep every option open": the reversibility research suggests that open-ended revisability can feed second-guessing, regret and lower satisfaction.[^s2][^s3] And irreversibility is not a reason to avoid deciding, since waiting for near-certainty has costs of its own.[^s5]

## Evidence

The core principle, calibrating effort to reversibility, is practitioner wisdom backed by economic reasoning rather than a tested intervention.[^s4][^s5] The related finding that reversible choices reduce satisfaction comes from Gilbert and Ebert's 2002 studies, which explained it through a "psychological immune system" that helps people make peace with outcomes once they are fixed but stays switched off while change is possible.[^s1] Bullens and colleagues found that reversible decisions kept choice-related thoughts more accessible, used up working memory and increased regret.[^s2] Their 2016 review describes the satisfaction effect as consistent, with possible mechanisms including counterfactual thinking and a lack of closure, but also notes limits: most studies were brief lab tasks with short revision windows, much of the work comes from a few research groups, and moderators matter (one study found maximizers were happier with reversible choices and satisficers with final ones).[^s3] Treat the effect as well supported in the lab but not yet established for long, real-life commitments.[^s3]

## Connections

This principle is a guide for [[decisions-under-uncertainty]], and its economic basis lies in decision-theoretic work on uncertainty and irreversibility.[^s4] It sharpens [[major-life-decision]] and [[deliberation]] by saying where slow thought is worth its cost, and it fits [[leading-others]], where leaders must decide which calls to delegate and make fast and which to escalate.[^s5] The finding that keeping options open lowers satisfaction is demonstrated by Gilbert and Ebert's study and explained by the psychological immune system,[^s1] and supported by Bullens and colleagues' evidence linking reversibility to regret.[^s2] That links it to [[regret]] and to [[satisficing]], since maximizers and satisficers seem to respond to reversibility differently.[^s3] [[kill-criteria]] and [[knowing-when-to-quit]] are the other side of the same coin: deciding in advance when a reversible commitment should in fact be reversed.[^s3]
