steps:
  - Before you look at any options, write down the few things the choice must achieve and the minimum each must reach; this is your "good enough" line.
  - Decide in advance how much search the choice deserves, such as a number of options, a deadline or a budget of hours, scaled to how much is at stake and how reversible it is.
  - Look at options one at a time and take the first that clears every line, rather than collecting the whole field to rank.
  - If nothing clears the line within your search budget, lower one standard deliberately; if many options clear it at once, raise one, and say which and why.
  - Once you have chosen, stop comparing; do not keep checking the options you passed up or what others chose.
  - Keep maximizing-style search for the rare choices where a better option is worth the extra effort and the decision is hard to undo.
example:
  situation: A team lead has to replace the team's project-tracking tool within a month, and dozens of products are available.
  before: "I'll trial every serious tool on the market, build a forty-row comparison sheet and keep reading reviews until I'm sure we have the best one."
  after: "We need four things: our existing workflows, a single sign-on, an export option and a price under our budget. I'll trial tools from the shortlist IT already approved, adopt the first one that meets all four, and stop shopping once it's in."
  why: Fixing the standard before searching turns an endless comparison into a bounded task, and stopping after the choice spares the team the second-guessing that research links to looking for the best.

# Satisficing

Satisficing means choosing an option that is good enough rather than insisting on the best. You set an aspiration level, the minimum an acceptable option must reach, and you consider options until one meets or exceeds it.[^s3] Its opposite in psychology is maximizing: seeking, and needing to be sure of, the best possible option.[^s1] Satisficing is not carelessness or low standards. A satisficer can set a high bar; what changes is that the bar is fixed before the search, and the search ends when it is cleared.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The idea comes from the economist and cognitive scientist Herbert Simon. In the mid-1950s he proposed replacing the "global rationality" of economic theory, which pictures a chooser who weighs every option and picks the optimum, with a kind of rationality suited to real people with limited time, information and computing power.[^s3] He later coined the term "bounded rationality" for this view, and described people as satisficing because they lack the means to maximize.[^s3] Simon at first treated satisficing as a rough approximation of ideal choice; later he treated the search for efficient, good-enough solutions as an approach in its own right.[^s3] Economists and psychologists have since developed it, for example in theories in which people adjust their aspiration level up or down depending on which options turn out to be available.[^s3]

In 2002 the psychologist Barry Schwartz and colleagues turned the contrast into a question about people rather than procedures. They asked whether some people habitually maximize and others satisfice, and what that does to their lives.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most choices offer more options than anyone can examine, so the goal of finding the best can never be fully met, and trying anyway has costs.[^s3] Across seven samples of 1,747 people, Schwartz's team found that scores on their Maximization Scale went with less happiness, optimism, self-esteem and life satisfaction, and with more regret and depression.[^s1] In follow-up studies, people who scored high on maximizing compared themselves with others more, were more affected by others doing better, and were less happy with their purchases.[^s1]

A study of graduating students looking for work shows the trade-off sharply. Those who scored high on maximizing landed jobs with starting salaries about 20% higher, yet felt less satisfied with the jobs they took and more negative throughout the search.[^s2] The researchers linked this to maximizers' heavier reliance on outside information and their fixation on the options they had passed up.[^s2] Satisficing is the wise response to this trade-off: it spends effort where effort pays, and it leaves room to be content with a sound choice.[^s1][^s2]

## How to apply it

Follow the steps above. The key move is the order: decide your standards first, then search.[^s3] If you look first, every new option tempts you to redefine what you want, and the search never ends.[^s1] Scale the effort to the stakes. A sandwich, a hotel room or a routine supplier deserves little search. A job, a home or a key hire may deserve more, because the job study suggests that extra search can bring real gains, even when it costs some satisfaction.[^s2] At work, writing the acceptance criteria before reviewing candidates, vendors or designs makes the stopping point visible to everyone and keeps a group from drifting into endless comparison.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "good enough" as "anything will do."** Satisficing needs a clearly stated standard. Without one it is just taking the first thing that comes along.[^s3]
- **Setting the bar after the search.** Standards that shift with every new option turn satisficing back into maximizing.[^s1]
- **Satisficing on everything.** Some decisions do repay a careful search, as the job-market salary gap shows.[^s2]
- **Reopening a settled choice.** Comparing your choice with options you passed up, or with what others chose, is the habit most tied to maximizers' regret and lower satisfaction.[^s1][^s2]
- **Confusing maximizing with perfectionism.** In Schwartz's data the two were correlated, but perfectionism went with *more* happiness while maximizing went with less, so they are not the same trait.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The core finding is real but **mixed**. Most of the evidence is correlational and self-reported, so it shows that maximizers tend to be less satisfied, not that switching strategy will make anyone happier.[^s1][^s2] The measurement has also been criticised. Diab, Gillespie and Highhouse found that the original Maximization Scale was internally inconsistent and seemed to capture indecision, avoidance and emotional instability as well as maximizing.[^s4] With a tighter scale focused on seeking the best option, maximizing was still linked to more regret, but not to lower life satisfaction, indecisiveness or neuroticism.[^s4] A study of 300 Chilean students using a later instrument found the same split: difficulty in deciding was strongly tied to regret and lower life satisfaction, while searching widely among alternatives went with modestly *higher* life satisfaction.[^s5] The best-supported lesson is narrower than the popular one. What seems to hurt is agonising over choices and dwelling on the options you passed up, not having high standards.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Satisficing was **introduced by Herbert Simon** as part of his account of **bounded rationality**, and it is **studied in decision theory**.[^s3] It **contrasts with maximizing**, the habit of seeking the best.[^s1] It is **supported by Schwartz and colleagues' 2002 studies** and by **Iyengar, Wells and Schwartz's job-search study**, which also show its main **application in major life decisions** such as choosing a job.[^s1][^s2] It is **challenged by Diab, Gillespie and Highhouse's measurement study**, which suggests the "unhappy maximizer" partly reflects how maximizing was measured.[^s4] Satisficing also bears on **regret**: the best evidence ties regret to difficulty deciding and to dwelling on passed-up options.[^s4][^s5] It sits alongside **fast and frugal heuristics**, other simple strategies for choosing under limits, and complements **moral algebra** and **deliberation**: fixing your standards first tells you when the weighing can stop.[^s3]
