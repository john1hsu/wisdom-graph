# Ancient Egyptian wisdom literature

Ancient Egyptian wisdom literature is a body of texts that Egyptologists group into two main kinds: *instructions* (Egyptian *sebayt*, "teachings"), collections of maxims and longer reflections on everyday conduct, and *laments* or discourses that describe a world in which moral order has broken down.[^s2] The instructions are the better known. Each is presented as the teaching of a famous sage, and their advice concerns life in society: how to behave towards superiors, equals and inferiors, how to speak, how to keep one's temper and how to earn a good name.[^s2][^s3] Much of it is, in effect, a manual on hearing and giving counsel.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

The instructions were written by and for scribes and officials, and copied in schools to train them; the main surviving examples include the teachings attributed to Kagemni, Ptahhotep, Merikare, Any and Amenemope.[^s3] The best-known, the *Teaching of Ptahhotep*, presents itself as the words of a vizier to King Isesi of the Old Kingdom, who asks permission to instruct his son.[^s1] Scholars date its actual composition later, to the Middle Kingdom (Twelfth Dynasty), on the evidence of its language and of the oldest manuscripts, chief among them Papyrus Prisse.[^s1] Some of the sages to whom instructions were credited, Ptahhotep and Hordedef among them, may be legendary; the famous name lent the teaching its authority.[^s2] The genre lasted well over a thousand years, through New Kingdom works such as *Amenemope*, which mention the gods far more often, to Demotic instructions such as *Ankhsheshonqy* in Ptolemaic and Roman times.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The instructions are among the oldest surviving collections of advice for working life, and their central worries still sound current: how to disagree with a superior or an equal, when to speak in a meeting, and how to hear out someone whose request must be refused.[^s1][^s3] They also influenced later traditions. A Hebrew author apparently used *Amenemope* as a model and a source for a section of the Book of Proverbs, which refers to its "thirty sayings".[^s5] For readers today, the instructions offer a distinctive picture of wisdom as social and verbal self-command: knowing when to listen, when to stay silent and what to say.[^s3][^s4]

## Key teachings

- **Listen first, and let people finish.** Ptahhotep tells an official to hear a petitioner out patiently, since a good hearing soothes the heart even when the request cannot be granted (section 20, Papyrus Prisse 9.3–7).[^s1] The Egyptian verb for "hear", *sdm*, also meant "obey" and sometimes "judge", and inscriptions praised officials as patient, good listeners.[^s4]
- **The hearer becomes the one who is heard.** The epilogue of *Ptahhotep* says that listening benefits the listener, that the heart decides whether someone becomes a hearer, and that the fool who cannot hear mistakes knowledge for ignorance (sections 42–45).[^s1][^s4]
- **Don't be proud of your knowledge.** An early maxim of *Ptahhotep* begins: *Do not be proud on account of your knowledge.* Speak with the ignorant as with the wise, it continues, because fine words are more sought after than greenstone yet are found even among women at the grindstone (section 4, Prisse 5.8–10).[^s1]
- **Speak only when it helps.** In a superior's council, Ptahhotep advises speaking only when you have found a solution; the student should listen, and speak only after becoming a craftsman.[^s3]
- **Silence is self-command, not weakness.** *Amenemope*'s ideal is the "truly silent man", whose restraint contrasts with the "heated man" undone by his own anger.[^s3] Michael Fox reads silence, good timing, restraint, fluency and truthfulness as the five canons of Egyptian rhetoric, aimed at success and at social harmony.[^s3]
- **Report faithfully.** A messenger between officials should carry a message exactly as given, not stir up conflict between them (section 11).[^s1]

## Common misreadings

**"The world's oldest book, written around 2400 BCE."** Popular accounts often date *Ptahhotep* to the vizier it names, but the text itself is most likely a Middle Kingdom composition set in an earlier, idealised age.[^s1][^s2] **"Silence means meekness."** In the instructions silence is a deliberate strategy: it lets a hot-headed opponent expose himself and builds a reputation for steadiness.[^s3] **"The voice of all Egyptians."** Every surviving text comes from the scribal elite, so the instructions may reflect the values of officials rather than of society as a whole, and some advice, such as bowing to a superior who will not be swayed (section 5), reflects a steeply hierarchical court.[^s1][^s2] **"Proverbs simply copied Egypt."** The parallels between *Amenemope* and Proverbs are real, but the Hebrew writer adapted the Egyptian work as a model and source rather than translating it.[^s5]

## In practice today

A manager who hears out a complaint fully before explaining what cannot be done is following Ptahhotep's advice on petitioners; a colleague who waits to speak until they have a workable proposal is following his advice on councils; and the "silent man" models answering a provocative email after the heat has passed.[^s1][^s3] Written for young officials in a very different society, the teachings are best read as one ancient voice on listening and restraint, not as rules.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

Egyptian wisdom literature is part of the wider **ancient Near Eastern wisdom literature** and **influenced Hebrew wisdom literature**, most clearly through the ***Instruction of Amenemope*** and Proverbs.[^s5] It **teaches giving advice**, through maxims on when and how to speak in council and how to receive petitioners, and so bears on **seeking counsel** and **leading others**.[^s1][^s3] It **teaches intellectual humility** in Ptahhotep's warning against pride in knowledge[^s1] and **self-control** through the ideal of the silent man, the opposite of the angry "heated man" (compare **anger**).[^s3] Its contrast between the hearer and the fool echoes **folly**,[^s4] and its concern with careful, truthful speech, tied to the order of **maat**, invites comparison with the Buddhist teaching of **right speech** and with **Confucianism**'s concern for fitting conduct, though each tradition grounds speech in its own terms.[^s3] It is **studied in Egyptology**, where scholars debate how far its ideals reached beyond the scribal class.[^s2]
