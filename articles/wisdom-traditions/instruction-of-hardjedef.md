# Instruction of Hardjedef

The *Instruction of Hardjedef* (also spelled Hordjedef or Djedefhor) is the earliest known example of the Egyptian instruction, the genre of teachings in which a father passes practical wisdom to his son.[^s1] It is credited to Prince Hardjedef, a son of King Khufu, builder of the Great Pyramid, and is addressed to his son Au-ib-re.[^s2][^s3] Only the beginning survives, and what remains is advice about two things a capable adult should not leave to chance: founding a household and preparing for one's own death.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The text names Hardjedef as its author in a single framing sentence, which already uses the father-to-son setting that later instructions expanded into longer stories.[^s1] Miriam Lichtheim, whose translation is standard in English, treats the attribution as a literary fiction: she classes Hardjedef, Kagemni and Ptahhotep alike as pseudepigrapha and dates Hardjedef, the most archaic in language of the three, to the Fifth Dynasty rather than the Fourth.[^s1] The Munich museum that holds one copy keeps the traditional attribution to the Fourth Dynasty and calls the teaching the oldest literary work in Egypt.[^s2] Either way, the surviving text was pieced together from nine New Kingdom ostraca (inscribed flakes of limestone and pottery) and a Late Period wooden tablet, copied more than a thousand years after its supposed author lived, largely as school exercises for scribes.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

The instruction shows how early Egyptian wisdom joined everyday prudence with the awareness of death. Its opening asks the reader to examine and correct himself before others have to.[^s1] It then advises: when you prosper, found a household, take a wife who is mistress of her own heart, and build a house for your son.[^s1] Next it turns to the tomb: make your place in the necropolis worthy, endow a funerary priest and a well-watered field to support him, and value that provision even above your heir, because the priest's service will profit you more than your son's.[^s1][^s2] Its reason is compressed into one line of Lichtheim's translation: "The house of death is for life."[^s1] The teaching treats mortality not as a reason for gloom but as something to plan for in good time, while one still has the means.[^s1][^s2]

Hardjedef's name also outlived the text. By the Ramesside period he was counted among the great authors of the past, and a later papyrus asks whether there is anyone like him.[^s2][^s3] He appears as a character in the tales of *Khufu and the Magicians*, which shows he was remembered as a figure of learning.[^s3]

## Common misreadings

- **That it is Hardjedef's own words.** Egyptologists generally read the attribution as a way of lending the teaching a famous name; the prince himself may have written nothing.[^s1]
- **That it is a full book of maxims.** Only the opening survives; we do not know what else it taught.[^s1][^s3]
- **That its funerary advice is only about religion.** The provisions it describes, a field, a priest and a tomb, were part of how Old Kingdom society organised property and obligation, and the advice is as much about managing those as about belief.[^s2]

## A later dissent

Egyptian wisdom did not simply repeat this view. The Middle Kingdom *Song from the Tomb of King Intef*, one of the harpers' songs, says the singer has heard the sayings of Imhotep and Hardedef, still recited in full, and then asks where their places are now; the tombs of such men have crumbled as if they had never been.[^s1] Its answer is to enjoy life and follow one's heart while it lasts, since no one takes his goods along.[^s1] Lichtheim notes that this song went so far as to doubt the usefulness of tombs, a theme later writers kept returning to.[^s1] Read together, the two texts stage an old disagreement about how to live in view of death: provide carefully for what comes after, or take the present seriously because careful provision may not last. Many readers find something of value in both.

## Applying it today

The surviving lines translate readily into modern terms: put your affairs in order while things are going well rather than in a crisis; arrange for the people and causes that should continue after you; and do not assume that heirs or successors will remember to look after what you have not arranged yourself.[^s1][^s2] This is general reflection, not legal or financial advice; for wills and estates, consult a qualified professional.

## Connections

The *Instruction of Hardjedef* **introduces** **ancient Egyptian wisdom literature**: it is the genre's earliest known example and sets its father-to-son frame.[^s1] It **contrasts with** the **harpers' songs**, which name Hardedef among the sages and then question the lasting value of tombs.[^s1] It is **studied in** **Egyptology**, where scholars such as Brunner-Traut and Posener published its ostraca.[^s1] It sits beside the later *Instruction of Amenemope* and the **Book of Proverbs** in the wider wisdom literature of the region, and relates to **death awareness** and **memento mori**, because it treats awareness of death as a reason to act well now.[^s1][^s2] It also relates to the **sage**: Egyptians remembered Hardjedef as a classic author and model of wisdom.[^s3]
