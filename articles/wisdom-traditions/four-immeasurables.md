# The four immeasurables

The four immeasurables are a set of attitudes that Buddhist teaching asks a person to extend to all beings without limit: loving-kindness or goodwill (*mettā*), compassion (*karuṇā*), sympathetic joy in others' good fortune (*muditā*) and equanimity (*upekkhā*).[^s1] The Pali texts call them *appamaññā*, "boundless", because they are not meant to stop at family, friends or one's own group, and *brahma-vihāra*, "divine abodes", because they are meant to become the mind's usual dwelling place rather than an occasional visit.[^s1] They matter for wisdom because they treat kindness not as a single warm feeling but as four distinct responses, each suited to a different situation and each with its own way of going wrong.

## Origin

The four appear throughout the early Pali discourses as meditations in which goodwill, compassion, joy and equanimity are radiated in every direction.[^s1] Their classic systematic treatment is in chapter IX of the *Visuddhimagga*, the fifth-century Theravāda manual by Buddhaghosa, which defines each quality by its characteristic, function and proximate cause, and names the condition in which it fails.[^s2] Scholars still debate their place on the path. Eviatar Shulman argues from the Pali sources that they link ethics and meditative concentration, can sometimes stand in for the standard meditative absorptions, and in some texts lead to liberation directly; he rejects readings that treat them as borrowed from Brahmanical religion or as merely preparatory to insight.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Ordinary kindness tends to be partial and brittle: generous to people we like, cool to strangers, and easily curdled into envy, pity or burnout. The framework's answer is balance. The Theravāda monk and scholar Nyanaponika Thera describes how the four protect one another: goodwill keeps compassion from becoming partial, compassion keeps goodwill and joy from complacency, sympathetic joy keeps compassion from sliding into melancholy, and equanimity steadies all three.[^s1] He calls equanimity their "crown and culmination", not a replacement for caring but the even balance that lets care last.[^s1]

## Key teachings

- **Each quality has a function.** Buddhaghosa gives goodwill the function of preferring others' welfare, compassion that of not bearing their suffering, sympathetic joy that of being unenvious, and equanimity that of seeing beings as equal.[^s2]
- **Each has a far enemy and a near enemy.** The far enemy is the plain opposite: ill will, cruelty, aversion, and the pull of greed and resentment. The near enemy is a look-alike that shares its outward features: greed, or possessive affection, for goodwill, worldly grief for compassion, worldly merriment for joy, and the "equanimity of unknowing", a dull indifference, for equanimity.[^s2]
- **The circle widens gradually.** Practice usually begins with oneself as a point of reference, moves through a respected person, loved ones and neutral people to those one finds hostile, and ends by "breaking down the barriers" between them.[^s1]

## Common misreadings

Equanimity is the quality most often misread, as detachment or not caring; the tradition counts exactly that as its near enemy.[^s2] Schools also differ. Theravāda keeps the order goodwill, compassion, joy, equanimity and does not tie the four to the bodhisattva ideal, whereas many Tibetan presentations put equanimity, understood as impartiality toward near and far, first, and use the four as a foundation for *bodhicitta*, the resolve to awaken for the sake of all beings.[^s3] Most modern research on "loving-kindness" tests only the first one or two qualities, so it should not be taken as a test of the whole framework.[^s5]

## Evidence

The framework is a teaching judged by long practice rather than by experiment, but its modern meditations have been tested. A meta-analysis of 24 studies found that loving-kindness meditation raised daily positive emotions by a medium amount against wait-list controls, though it rarely beat active alternatives such as mindfulness, about two in five studies carried a high risk of bias, and none focused on sympathetic joy or equanimity.[^s5] A review of 22 randomised trials of kindness-based meditation found benefits for depression, compassion and self-compassion against passive controls, weaker or inconclusive results against active ones, and low to moderate study quality; it also noted that the practice can at first be hard for some people.[^s6] Anyone who finds that meditation brings up persistent distress is better served by a qualified teacher or a mental-health professional than by pushing on alone.

## Connections

The four are rooted in [[buddhism]] and sit within Buddhist ethics.[^s1] They develop [[compassion]] and [[equanimity]], which are themselves the second and fourth of the set.[^s1] Sympathetic joy counters envy and goodwill counters ill will, since Buddhaghosa defines them by exactly those functions and opposites.[^s2] Equanimity is commonly confused with indifference, its near enemy.[^s2] The modern evidence for loving-kindness practice is summarised in two meta-analyses.[^s5][^s6] Loving-kindness meditation, self-compassion and empathy are closely related topics.
