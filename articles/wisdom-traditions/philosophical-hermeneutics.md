# Philosophical hermeneutics

Hermeneutics is the study of interpretation. It began as an aid to theology, law and other disciplines, and in philosophy became a movement that studies interpretation itself.[^s2] **Philosophical hermeneutics** is the name for the version developed by Hans-Georg Gadamer (1900–2002), which extends the art of interpretation from texts to all human understanding: of other people, of the past, of art and of ourselves.[^s3] It explains how a finite person, who can never see from nowhere, can still come to understand better: through openness, conversation and judgment rather than method alone.

## Origin

Modern hermeneutics runs from Friedrich Schleiermacher's general art of interpretation, through Wilhelm Dilthey's defence of the human sciences, to Martin Heidegger, who recast phenomenology as the self-interpretation of a being already immersed in the world.[^s2] Gadamer studied closely with Heidegger at Marburg and wrote his doctorate and habilitation on Plato.[^s1] His *Truth and Method* (1960) gave philosophical hermeneutics its classic statement, though it reached English readers only in 1975.[^s1] Gadamer drew on Plato for his picture of understanding as dialogue and on Aristotle's account of *phronesis*, practical wisdom, for his picture of understanding as situated judgment that no set of rules can replace.[^s1][^s3] His conduct under National Socialism remains disputed among scholars.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Much advice about judging well assumes we can set our biases aside. Hermeneutics argues that this is impossible: the assumptions we bring are what let us see anything at all, so the task is to put them at risk rather than pretend to have none.[^s2][^s3] The tradition thus offers a philosophical account of three capacities often linked with wisdom: knowing the limits of one's knowledge, taking another's view seriously as possibly true, and applying general knowledge to a particular case.[^s3]

It has also shaped practice. Dutch clinical ethicists ground **moral case deliberation**, in which professionals reason together about a hard case from their own work, in a dialogical ethics that cites *Truth and Method*; they hold that moral judgments arise from dialogue among open-minded people in practice, not from applying theory.[^s4] A recent account of that work draws on Gadamer's Socratic art of questioning, his openness and his "fusion of horizons" to describe the ethicist's role as building spaces for shared moral learning.[^s5]

## Key teachings

- **Pre-judgments are where understanding starts.** Gadamer uses *Vorurteil*, prejudice, in its neutral sense of a judgment made beforehand, and he criticises the Enlightenment's "prejudice against prejudice".[^s2] Our assumptions open us to a subject; understanding means testing and revising them, not starting from a blank slate.[^s1]
- **The hermeneutic circle.** We grasp the parts through a sense of the whole and revise our sense of the whole through the parts, so understanding moves in a circle of anticipation and correction.[^s2]
- **We are shaped by history we cannot fully see.** Gadamer's "historically effected consciousness" means our understanding is formed by traditions whose influence we can never make entirely explicit, so self-knowledge is always partial.[^s2]
- **Understanding is a fusion of horizons.** A horizon is the range of what can be seen from a standpoint. When we truly understand a text or a person, neither horizon simply absorbs the other; both are widened.[^s3]
- **Conversation is the model.** Good dialogue follows the subject matter rather than the wish to win, offers reasons, shows good will to understand and starts from recognising that one does not know.[^s3] For Gadamer, real listening assumes that the other person may have the better view.[^s3]
- **Understanding is application, as in phronesis.** For Gadamer, interpreting and applying are one act, not a theory followed later by its use. Like Aristotle's practically wise person, the interpreter turns shared knowledge into the right response to a new situation.[^s3]
- **The aim is formation.** Interpretation succeeds less by proof or repeatability than by whether it educates, leaving the interpreter more cultivated (*Bildung*).[^s2]

At work or at home, these teachings suggest a few habits: before a disagreement, notice the pre-judgments you are bringing;[^s2] ask what question the situation is really posing before reaching for an answer;[^s1] listen as though the other person might be right;[^s3] and count a conversation as successful if your own understanding has changed, not only theirs.[^s5]

## Common misreadings

- **"Prejudice is fine, so anything goes."** Gadamer's defenders argue that understanding stays answerable to its subject matter; pre-judgments are to be tested, not indulged.[^s1]
- **"Respecting tradition means deferring to it."** Making a tradition one's own means critically changing it.[^s3] Still, Jürgen Habermas objected that Gadamer's rehabilitation of tradition and authority gives too little ground for criticising ideology and power; Gadamer replied that critics are also situated in traditions.[^s1][^s2]
- **"Fusion means agreement."** The fusion of horizons is a dynamic widening in which both sides change, not a merger into consensus or the victory of one side.[^s3] Jacques Derrida questioned whether understanding and agreement deserve that priority at all; their 1981 Paris encounter is still debated.[^s1][^s2]
- **"Hermeneutics is a method."** Gadamer's title contrasts truth with method; he denies that a technique can guarantee understanding.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

This is a philosophical tradition, judged by argument and by its fruitfulness in fields from theology and law to education, psychology and ethics, not by experiment.[^s3] It has not itself been tested as an intervention, and practices built on it, such as moral case deliberation, have mostly been studied qualitatively.[^s4] Critics also ask whether it describes ethical understanding without telling us how to act.[^s1]

## Connections

Philosophical hermeneutics was **introduced by** **Hans-Georg Gadamer** and **introduced in** **Truth and Method**,[^s1] and it is **influenced by** **Martin Heidegger**'s hermeneutics of facticity,[^s2] by **Aristotle**'s account of phronesis[^s3] and by **Plato**'s dialogues.[^s1] It **teaches** **practical wisdom** as the model of understanding and **intellectual humility**, since good dialogue starts from knowing that one does not know.[^s3] It underpins **moral case deliberation**, which its developers ground in a dialogical, hermeneutic ethics.[^s5] It is **challenged by** **Jürgen Habermas**'s critique of tradition and by **Jacques Derrida**'s doubts about the priority of understanding.[^s2] It sits within the wider movement of **phenomenology** that Heidegger reshaped,[^s2] and it shares with **Socratic philosophy** and **Socratic ignorance** the view that admitting what one does not know is where understanding begins.[^s3]
