# Confucianism

Confucianism is the way of life propagated by Confucius in the sixth and fifth centuries BCE. The name is a Western coinage with no exact Chinese counterpart; the tradition has been at once a worldview, a social ethic, a political ideology, a scholarly tradition and a way of life, and is sometimes treated as a philosophy and sometimes as a religion, though never as an organised church.[^s3] Philosophers usually read its ethics as a form of virtue ethics, centred on the exemplary person who follows the Way (*dao*).[^s1]

## Origin

Kong Qiu, known in English as Confucius, was born in 551 BCE in the small state of Lu, as Zhou royal power was collapsing and warlord families ruled. He argued for restoring the Zhou rites (*li*) to bring back order, travelled for about fifteen years seeking a ruler who would employ him, and ended his life teaching in Lu.[^s2] He saw himself less as a founder than as a transmitter who revived the old to reach the new.[^s3] His sayings survive in the *Analects*, about five hundred short passages in twenty books. Tradition held it was compiled soon after his death in 479 BCE, but most modern scholars think it grew over two to three centuries and record an evolving image of Confucius rather than his exact words.[^s2]

The tradition then divided and grew. Mencius argued that human nature is good and that an unfit ruler could be deposed; Xunzi argued that it is bad and must be reshaped by ritual and teaching.[^s3][^s1] Under the Han emperor Wudi it became state orthodoxy, its classics the basis of later civil service examinations, and it spread through Chinese literate culture to Korea, Japan and Vietnam.[^s3] Neo-Confucians such as Zhu Xi (1130–1200) and Wang Yangming (1472–1529) later rebuilt it under Buddhist and Daoist influence; Wang taught the unity of knowledge and action.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Confucianism treats wisdom as something built through practice in ordinary roles: as a child, friend, colleague or official. Its picture of learning is unusually balanced. In the *Analects*, study without reflection leaves you confused and reflection without study is dangerous (2.15), and real knowledge means knowing what you know and what you do not (2.17).[^s2] Confucius is said to have refused four things: acting on guesses, demanding certainty, stubbornness and insisting on himself (9.4).[^s2] On error the text is blunt: "To err and not change – that, we may say, is to err" (15.30).[^s2] These are habits of wise reasoning, framed as character.

## Key teachings

- **Humaneness (*ren*).** Once a word for aristocratic birth, *ren* became a moral excellence anyone could attain.[^s1] Asked about it, Confucius says it means cherishing people, and that wisdom means knowing people (12.22).[^s2]
- **Reciprocity (*shu*).** Asked for one saying to practise all one's life, he names reciprocity: do not do to others what you do not want done to you (15.24; also 12.2).[^s2] A disciple summed up his whole Way as loyalty and reciprocity (4.15), though Eno notes that passage may be a later addition.[^s2]
- **Ritual propriety (*li*).** Restraining oneself and returning to ritual makes one humane (12.1).[^s2] Ritual gives people regular ways to act out respect, which strengthens the disposition itself.[^s1]
- **The exemplary person (*junzi*).** The *junzi* seeks harmony with others without trying to be the same as them (13.23), and stays untroubled when unrecognised (1.1).[^s2]
- **Family and graded care.** Filial piety is the first step toward humaneness,[^s3] and everyone is owed concern, but those closest to us are owed more, a view scholars call care with distinctions.[^s1]
- **Lifelong self-cultivation.** Confucius claimed to excel only in love of learning,[^s3] and his short autobiography runs from a commitment to learning at fifteen to acting freely without overstepping at seventy (2.4).[^s2]
- **Governing by example.** Leading people by virtue and ritual, so they feel shame, works better than ruling by law and punishment alone (2.3).[^s1][^s3]

## Common misreadings

- **"Confucius say."** Many sayings sold under his name are not in the *Analects*, and even the *Analects* is a layered record, so a quotation should be traced to a numbered passage before it is credited to him.[^s2]
- **Blind obedience.** The texts advise gently remonstrating with parents (4.18), Xunzi allows disobeying a father when obedience would bring disgrace, and Mencius held that bad rulers could be removed.[^s1][^s3]
- **Empty formality.** Confucius accepted a cheaper ceremonial cap but refused a bow that expressed the wrong attitude (9.3); the point of ritual is the respect it carries.[^s1]
- **Uniformity.** The *junzi* aims at harmony, not sameness (13.23).[^s2]
- **One fixed doctrine.** Mencius and Xunzi disagree about human nature, and Neo-Confucians disagree with both.[^s1] Modern scholars also debate its historic exclusion of women from office and education, and whether its hierarchy and filial roles can be reformed for democratic societies.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

As a tradition judged by argument and long practice, Confucianism is normative here. Psychologists in China have started measuring a "*junzi* personality". Across four studies with 1,169 Chinese participants, Ge and Hou (2021) found it linked to better mental health, partly through self-control and authenticity, including six months later.[^s4] The measure is self-reported and the experiments manipulated self-control and authenticity, not *junzi* character, so this weakly supports a modern construct and does not test the tradition itself.[^s4]

At work, the teachings translate into hiring for character as well as talent (12.22), welcoming disagreement in pursuit of harmony (13.23) and owning mistakes quickly (15.30).[^s2]

## Connections

Confucianism was **introduced by Confucius**, who saw himself as a transmitter of older Zhou ways.[^s3] It **contrasts with Daoism**: the *Daodejing* tells readers to discard *ren* and righteousness, and the *Zhuangzi* doubts our pretensions to moral knowledge.[^s1] It was **challenged by Mohism**, whose founder Mozi called ritual wasteful and argued for impartial care against graded love.[^s1] A modern *junzi* personality study lends it weak **support**.[^s4] It is a branch of **Chinese philosophy** and a form of **virtue ethics**,[^s1] developed by **Mencius** and **Xunzi**.[^s3] Its negative form of the **Golden Rule**, and its stress on **filial piety** and learning link it to **intellectual humility**, **compassion**, **moral habituation** and the **evening review**.[^s2]
