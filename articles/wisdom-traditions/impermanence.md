# Impermanence

Impermanence (Pāli *anicca*, Sanskrit *anitya*) is the Buddhist teaching that everything conditioned arises, changes and passes away. It is one of the three characteristics of sentient existence, alongside suffering and non-self, and the Buddhist diagnosis is that ignorance of these characteristics leads people to grasp the shifting elements of body and mind as "I" and "mine".[^s1] Seeing impermanence clearly is presented not as a gloomy fact to endure but as part of the key to release from suffering.[^s1]

## Origin

The teaching goes back to the earliest Buddhist discourses. In the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* (Saṃyutta Nikāya 22.59), traditionally the Buddha's second discourse, he takes each of the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness) in turn and asks his first five disciples: "Is form permanent or impermanent?"[^s2] They answer that it is impermanent, that what is impermanent is unsatisfactory, and that it is not fitting to regard it as one's self; seeing this, the disciple grows weary of the aggregates, becomes dispassionate and is released.[^s2] Mark Siderits reconstructs this as an argument from impermanence: a self would have to be permanent, none of the aggregates is permanent, so none of them is the self.[^s1]

Later schools sharpened the idea in different ways. The doctrine of momentariness, which treats phenomena as strings of momentary events, grew out of the principle of impermanence in the Abhidharma literature.[^s3] Siderits stresses that this is a later development: the Buddha's "impermanent" did not mean lasting only a moment.[^s1] The schools also disagreed with one another. The Sarvāstivāda held that all physical and mental phenomena are momentary, while the post-canonical Theravāda held that only mental events are, with material ones lasting around sixteen or seventeen mind-moments.[^s3] They split too on whether past and future phenomena exist: the Sarvāstivāda said yes, the Theravāda and Sautrāntika said no.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The practical claim is that much suffering comes from treating what changes as if it should stay put: a relationship, a role, health, reputation, even a mood. Recognising impermanence loosens that grip.[^s1] It also cuts both ways. Hard times pass too, and good times are worth noticing precisely because they will not last.[^s6]

Other traditions reached kindred insights by their own routes. Marcus Aurelius repeatedly reminds himself that everything around him is changing and will soon be gone, that change is no evil, and that "The universe is transformation: life is opinion" (*Meditations* 4.3; see also 4.36, 4.43).[^s4] In Greek philosophy, Heraclitus is remembered for the slogan *panta rhei*, "everything flows", and his fragment on stepping into rivers fed by ever-different waters.[^s5] Johanna Seibt notes that the radical-flux reading of Heraclitus may owe much to early commentators such as Plato and Aristotle, and that his own view tied change to measure and balance, so the popular slogan is best treated as a summary of his reputation rather than a verified quotation.[^s5] The Stoic and Heraclitean versions rest on views of nature and reason, not on the Buddhist analysis of the self, so they are parallels rather than the same doctrine.[^s1][^s4][^s5]

## How to apply it

- **Name what you are holding as fixed.** When a change upsets you, ask which part of the situation you had quietly assumed would never change.[^s1]
- **Look at the parts.** Following the discourse's method, notice that a feeling, a thought or a bodily sensation arises and fades; watch one do so instead of arguing with it.[^s2]
- **Picture the stressor from further down the road.** Ask how this setback will look in a year or several, focusing on the ways it will have changed or passed.[^s6]
- **Let endings sharpen attention.** When something good is ending (a team, a season, a stage of family life), use that fact to appreciate it and act on it now.[^s4][^s6]

At work this means treating structures, roles and successes as provisional; in relationships, letting people change rather than holding them to who they were.[^s1][^s6]

## Common misreadings

- **"Nothing matters."** Siderits notes that the realisation that everything is impermanent can seem to drain life of meaning, but the Buddhist middle path rejects both eternalism, the view that persons are eternal, and its opposite extreme; it is meant to end suffering, not to justify indifference.[^s1]
- **"Everything lasts only an instant."** Momentariness is one later scholastic development, disputed in its details across schools, not the original teaching.[^s1][^s3]
- **Detachment as coldness.** The discourse describes dispassion toward the aggregates, not a withdrawal of care from other people.[^s2]

## Evidence

As a religious and philosophical teaching, impermanence is judged by argument and long practice, so its status here is normative.[^s1] A modern, secular version has been tested. In six studies, Bruehlman-Senecal and Ayduk found that imagining a stressor from a distant-future perspective reduced distress, and that the most consistent reason was greater focus on the event's impermanence; directly prompting an impermanence focus produced similar effects.[^s6] The benefits showed up mainly for people facing bad outcomes: among students who did well on an exam, a stronger impermanence focus went with more negative feeling, a reminder that good things pass too.[^s6] Distancing did not change later exam performance.[^s6] These are student and online samples and short-term outcomes. Reflection on impermanence is not a treatment: anyone facing persistent distress, or a loss that becomes overwhelming, should seek professional support, and in a crisis contact local emergency or crisis services.

## Connections

Impermanence is **rooted in Buddhism**, where it is the first of the **three marks of existence** with **dukkha** and **non-self**,[^s1] and was **introduced by the Buddha** in discourses such as the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*.[^s2] It **contrasts with eternalism**, one of the two extremes the Buddhist middle path avoids.[^s1] Marcus Aurelius's reflections suggest it can help **develop equanimity**, since change is no evil to be resisted.[^s4] Its secular form is **supported by the "This too shall pass" study** and is **applied in adversity**, where focusing on the passing nature of a stressor eased distress.[^s6] It has a Greek parallel in **Heraclitus**'s philosophy of flux.[^s5] It sits beside **memento mori**, **mono no aware** and **wabi-sabi**, which draw on finitude in other traditions, and beside **equanimity**, **acceptance**, the **view from above**, **grief and loss**, **savoring** and **hedonic adaptation**.
