# Daoism

Daoism (also spelled Taoism) is an indigenous Chinese tradition, both philosophical and religious, that has shaped Chinese life for more than two thousand years. Its classics are the *Laozi* (*Daodejing*) and the *Zhuangzi*; around them grew a ritual religion centred on the Dao.[^s4] Next to the duty-centred ethics of Confucianism, it offers a yielding, unforced way of meeting the world.[^s4] Hansen describes it as a naturalist philosophy of practice whose central question is how to find and follow a *dao*, a way or path.[^s1]

## Origin

Tradition makes Laozi a Zhou court archivist, older than Confucius, who wrote a short book at a frontier pass as he left for the west. The story comes from Sima Qian's *Shiji* of about 100 BCE, by which time Laozi was already a legend, and some modern scholars think he never existed.[^s2] Most now read the *Daodejing* as a composite that grew from collections of sayings, written down from the late fifth century BCE and largely stable by the mid-third.[^s2] Bamboo slips found at Guodian in 1993, from a tomb of about 300 BCE, hold roughly 2,000 of its characters and are the oldest copies known.[^s2]

The *Zhuangzi*, from the Warring States period, is the earliest surviving Chinese text to offer a philosophy for private life rather than for rulers, using parables to show how values depend on one's standpoint.[^s4] Neither author named a school: the labels *daojia* ("Daoist school") and *daojiao* ("Daoist teaching") were coined under the Han.[^s1] After the Han, the "dark learning" (*xuanxue*) of Wang Bi and Guo Xiang revived Lao-Zhuang thought and eased Buddhism's entry into China.[^s1] Rivalry and borrowing between the two traditions later helped produce Chan (Zen) Buddhism.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Much wisdom advice is about doing more. Daoism argues that much trouble comes from forcing, grasping and over-managing. Its texts warn that difficult problems begin as easy ones, so the wise act while things are small (ch. 63), and that long journeys start with a single step (ch. 64).[^s3] It also pairs knowing others with the deeper task of knowing oneself (ch. 33).[^s3] The *Zhuangzi* adds an intellectual humility: our lives are limited while the ways of knowing are not, so no single standpoint has the last word.[^s1]

## Key teachings

- **The Dao.** The *Daodejing* opens by saying that a way that can be spoken of is not the constant Way, and later calls "Dao" only a provisional name (chs. 1, 25).[^s3] Readers take it as the source of all things, a ground of non-being, or a natural pattern to navigate.[^s2][^s1]
- **Virtue or power (*de*).** *De* is what one gains from the Dao; interpreters dispute whether it is an endowment of vital energy or one's authentic nature.[^s2] The highest *de* is unassuming and does not advertise itself (ch. 38).[^s3][^s4]
- **Non-forcing action (*wu wei*).** The Dao does nothing for the sake of doing, yet nothing is left undone (ch. 37).[^s3] *Wu wei* means acting in harmony with things without strain, not doing nothing.[^s4][^s2]
- **Naturalness (*ziran*) and the uncarved block (*pu*).** *Ziran* is spontaneity, things being self-so; *pu* symbolises an original simplicity that artificial shaping and clever knowledge damage.[^s4][^s2]
- **Softness and water.** "The softest thing in the world dashes against and overcomes the hardest" (ch. 43).[^s3] Water benefits everything while settling in the low places people avoid (chs. 8, 78), and the living are supple while the dead are stiff (ch. 76).[^s3]
- **Reversal and return.** Opposites define each other (ch. 2); the crooked becomes straight and the empty full (ch. 22); things return to their root in stillness (ch. 16).[^s3]
- **Doubt about imposed morality.** Benevolence and righteousness come into fashion only when the great Way is lost (ch. 18).[^s3] The Guodian text of chapter 19 differs here: it says to cut off artificiality where the received text says benevolence.[^s2]

## Common misreadings

- **"*Wu wei* means passivity."** It means unforced, well-timed action, not inaction.[^s4][^s2]
- **"Daoism rejects all ethics."** Neo-Confucians called it amoral; Hansen argues that it resists any one authority's claim to the right way.[^s1] The *Laozi* also criticises war, heavy taxes and cruel punishment.[^s2]
- **"Philosophical Daoism is pure, religious Daoism a later corruption."** Scholars no longer accept that split: thinkers, ascetics, alchemists and priests influenced one another throughout, and the division is at most a convenience.[^s4] Practices aimed at longevity were important to the religion, though the *Zhuangzi* scorned them.[^s4]
- **One fixed reading.** Interpreters disagree about whether the *Laozi* is mystical, political or a life philosophy, whether it is coherent or a layered anthology, and whether it is relativist.[^s2]
- **Famous lines are exact quotations.** With several early versions and many loose renderings, check a "Lao Tzu said" line against a scholarly translation.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

As a tradition judged by argument and long practice, Daoism is normative here. Psychologists in China built a "Taoist cognitive psychotherapy" around eight principles, including knowing when enough is enough, the soft overcoming the hard and letting nature take its course.[^s5] Chang and colleagues report an earlier Chinese trial in which 143 patients with generalised anxiety did better with it than with medication alone; their own culturally adapted pilot with five Chinese American immigrants found clear gains only in the three without depression, with no control group.[^s5] This is preliminary and tests a modern therapy, not the tradition; persistent anxiety or low mood is a reason to see a qualified clinician.[^s5]

At work, the teachings point to fixing small problems early (ch. 63), not over-managing, and leading without display.[^s3][^s2]

## Connections

Daoism was **introduced in** the *Daodejing* and traditionally **introduced by** Laozi, whose historicity is doubtful.[^s2] It **contrasts with** Confucianism: it treats Confucian virtues as symptoms of a lost Way and rejects any privileged authority on the right path.[^s1][^s3] A pilot of **Taoist cognitive therapy** lends it weak **support**.[^s5] It belongs to **Chinese philosophy**, shares ground with **Buddhism** through Chan,[^s4] and informs the **sage** who acts through *wu wei*, the ideals of **simplicity** and **contentment** (knowing what is enough), **humble leadership**, **intellectual humility**, **know thyself** and **equanimity**.[^s3][^s1]
