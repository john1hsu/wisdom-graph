# Book of Proverbs

The Book of Proverbs (Hebrew *Mishlei*) is a book of the Hebrew Bible, also part of the Christian Old Testament, that collects advice on how to live well: long parental instructions, poems in which Wisdom speaks in her own voice, and hundreds of short sayings, most of them a single line in two balanced halves.[^s2] Its opening verses say what it is for: to know wisdom and discipline, to give prudence to the inexperienced, and to let the wise grow in learning and wise counsel.[^s3] Its motto, repeated at the close of the first collection, is that "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7; compare 9:10).[^s3][^s1]

## Origin

The title names Solomon, the tradition's patron of wisdom, but scholars read the heading as an attribution that neither proves nor necessarily implies authorship.[^s2] The book is an anthology of distinct collections: an introduction of instructions and wisdom poems (chapters 1–9); a first "Solomonic" collection of about 375 sayings (10:1–22:16); the "words of the wise" (22:17–24:34); a second Solomonic collection said to have been copied by King Hezekiah's scribes (chapters 25–29); and appendices with the sayings of Agur and of King Lemuel's mother, ending in the acrostic poem on the capable wife (chapters 30–31).[^s2] Dating is disputed. Some encyclopedia writers place the final edition after the Babylonian exile while stressing that much of its material is older; others think the late monarchy just as likely. It is probably the oldest surviving example of Hebrew wisdom literature.[^s2]

Proverbs belongs to a wider ancient Near Eastern genre. Its section 22:17–24:22 has a demonstrable literary link with the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope*, perhaps reworked from memory rather than copied.[^s2] The Egyptologist and biblical scholar Bernd Schipper argues that the Hebrew writer adopted the very scribal techniques of Egyptian schools, producing a wisdom teaching in Egyptian style.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Proverbs treats wisdom as something learned, practised and handed on. In a recent reading of the opening verses, Matthew O'Kelly argues that the introduction sets out three aims: understanding oriented to the fear of God, the cultivation of moral virtue, and teaching the next generation to do the same.[^s1] Its setting, a parent instructing a child and urging them to listen, makes wisdom a matter of formation over time rather than of cleverness in the moment.[^s1][^s3]

Many of its sayings speak to everyday judgment. It warns that the fool's way seems right in his own eyes, while the wise person listens to counsel; that plans fail without advice and succeed with many advisers; that a gentle answer turns away anger; that a person who rules their temper is better than one who captures a city; that pride comes before a fall; and that no one should boast about tomorrow, because they do not know what a day will bring (12:15, 15:22, 15:1, 16:32, 16:18, 27:1).[^s3]

## Key teachings

- **Wisdom begins in reverence and teachability.** The motto ties knowledge to the fear of the LORD, and the book sets wise listeners against fools who despise instruction (1:7).[^s3][^s1]
- **Seek counsel and stay humble.** Several sayings link wisdom with humility and with seeking advice, and folly with being sure of one's own view (11:2, 12:15, 15:22).[^s3]
- **Govern speech and temper.** Soft answers, few words and slowness to anger recur as marks of the wise (15:1, 16:32).[^s3]
- **Sayings need judgment.** Proverbs 26:4–5 tells the reader both not to answer a fool by his folly and to answer him, for different reasons.[^s3] One teacher reads the pair as a deliberate prompt to judge when each applies, showing that wisdom does not mean doing the same thing in superficially similar cases.[^s4]

## Common misreadings

- **"Proverbs are promises."** The book describes an order in which actions tend to bring fitting consequences, but its sayings are generalisations about how life usually goes, not guarantees for every case.[^s2][^s4]
- **"Solomon wrote it."** The book itself names other authors, such as Agur and Lemuel's mother, and scholars treat the Solomonic heading as traditional rather than historical.[^s2]
- **"It is purely Israelite" or "it is merely copied."** Its Egyptian connections are real, but scholars describe adaptation and rewriting, not plain borrowing.[^s2][^s5]
- **Quoting single verses as universal rules.** Many popular quotations are lifted from paired or contrasting sayings; 26:4–5 shows that a single verse can mislead when read alone.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Proverbs sits within **Hebrew wisdom literature** and is probably its oldest surviving book, so the graph records the tradition as **introduced in** it.[^s2] It is **part of** the **Hebrew Bible** and is read in **Judaism** and Christianity alike.[^s2] It is **influenced by** the *Instruction of Amenemope* and, more broadly, by **ancient Egyptian wisdom literature** and its scribal methods.[^s2][^s5] It is **studied in** **biblical studies**, where scholars still debate how its introduction frames the whole.[^s1] It relates to **seeking counsel**, **humility**, **hubris**, **anger** and **right speech** through its sayings on advice, pride, temper and words (12:15, 16:18, 15:1);[^s3] to **friendship** through its image of iron sharpening iron (27:17);[^s3] to **folly**, its constant counter-figure;[^s3] and to **wise reasoning**, because its clashing sayings ask the reader to weigh context before acting.[^s4]
