# Bhagavad Gita

The *Bhagavad Gita* ("Song of the Lord") is a Sanskrit dialogue embedded in the sixth book of the *Mahabharata*, one of India's two great epics.[^s3] On the eve of a war between two branches of the same family, the warrior Arjuna loses his nerve, and his charioteer Krishna, whom the epic presents as an avatar of the god Vishnu, answers him in a sustained philosophical argument.[^s3][^s1] At heart it is about acting well when every option costs something: how to decide, keep judgment steady and relate to outcomes you cannot control.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Britannica dates the composition to perhaps the first or second century CE and places it in chapters 23 to 40 of Book VI of the epic.[^s3] The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy (IEP) notes that the *Gita* is generally treated as an interpolation into the wider epic, which took shape around the start of the Common Era.[^s1] It draws on the Upanishads and on the Samkhya school's distinction between spirit and matter, and it reconciles Vedic ritual action with Upanishadic knowledge through devotion (*bhakti*).[^s3]

The setting is a moral crisis. Arjuna lays down his bow and gives three reasons not to fight: the war's terrible consequences (1.34–36), what it would make of him as a person (1.38–39), and the duties it would violate (1.41). The IEP reads these as early versions of consequentialist, virtue-based and duty-based arguments.[^s1] The rest of the work is Krishna's reply.

## Why it matters

The *Gita* became central to medieval Vedanta, and the philosophers Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja and Madhva all wrote commentaries on it.[^s1] In the nineteenth century British writers called it a Hindu counterpart to the New Testament, the New England Transcendentalists Emerson and Thoreau prized it, and Gandhi wrote a commentary on it.[^s3] Many Hindus still turn to it for spiritual guidance.[^s3] It also offers an early, worked-out account of acting under pressure without being ruled by hope or fear.[^s1][^s5]

## Key teachings

**Act without clinging to results.** Krishna's best-known teaching (*karma yoga*, roughly 2.46–67) is that Arjuna should do what his situation requires without attachment to its fruits.[^s1] Telang's 1882 Oxford translation renders 2.47 as "Your business is with action alone", not with its fruit, and warns equally against sliding into inaction.[^s2] The next verse calls the resulting evenness of mind, in success and failure alike, yoga itself.[^s2]

**Steady wisdom.** Arjuna then asks what a person of steady mind is like (2.54). Krishna describes someone who has given up cravings, is content in the self and draws in the senses as a tortoise draws in its limbs (2.55–58).[^s2] He also traces a chain: dwelling on objects of desire breeds attachment, attachment breeds desire, desire breeds anger, and anger clouds discrimination and memory until judgment is ruined (2.62–63).[^s2] Steadiness and equanimity come back later in the text (6.9, 18.26).[^s1]

**Your own duty, and the common good.** The *Gita* holds that it is better to do one's own duty (*svadharma*) imperfectly than another's well (3.35).[^s2][^s1] In the text this duty is tied to Arjuna's role as a warrior.[^s3] Krishna also speaks of acting for the welfare and holding-together of the world (*lokasaṃgraha*, 3.20–24).[^s1]

**Three paths.** Alongside action, the text teaches paths of knowledge (*jñāna*) and devotion (*bhakti*). Readers disagree about how the three fit together, and about how to read the late call to devotion that promises freedom from evil (18.66).[^s1]

## Common misreadings

*"Don't care about results."* Gandhi, who called renunciation of fruit the central teaching, insisted that it means not hankering after results, not indifference to them. In his words, "renunciation means absence of hankering after fruit".[^s4] The same verse that rejects fruit as a motive also warns against becoming attached to inaction (2.47).[^s2]

*"It is simply a call to war," or "it is simply about inner peace."* The text's literal setting is a battlefield, and Gandhi conceded that its letter is compatible with fighting. Even so, he read the battle as an image of the duel between right and wrong inside each person, and concluded from his own practice that full renunciation requires nonviolence.[^s4] Classical commentators also differ. The IEP reports that Śaṅkara read the work as favouring renunciation of action, while Rāmānuja held that ethical action itself cannot be abandoned.[^s1]

*"One fixed meaning."* The IEP argues that treating the *Gita* chiefly as a religious scripture owes much to the colonial era, and that it is better read as philosophy. Modern scholars variously read it as consequentialist, duty-based, virtue-based or as a distinct ethics of its own.[^s1] Translations also differ sharply. The IEP renders 2.50 as yoga being "skill in action", while Telang has devotion in actions being wisdom.[^s1][^s2] Before trusting a "Gita quote" seen online, check it against a scholarly translation and verse number.

## Evidence and limits

Psychiatrists Dilip Jeste and Ipsit Vahia used qualitative and text-analysis methods to code the *Gita*'s picture of a wise person. They found ten components, including knowledge of life, emotional regulation, control over desires, decisiveness, compassion and insight or humility.[^s5] These overlap substantially with modern scientific models, but the *Gita* puts far more weight on mastering desire, renouncing material pleasure and devotion to God.[^s5] This is a textual comparison, not a test of whether living by the *Gita* makes people wiser. As a philosophical and religious text, its teachings are weighed by argument, interpretation and long practice rather than by experiment.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

- **Broader:** classical Indian thought, with Buddhism linked as a related Indian tradition.[^s3]
- **Part of:** the sixth book of the *Mahabharata*.[^s3]
- **Influenced by:** the Upanishads and Samkhya dualism.[^s3]
- **Studied in:** the psychology of wisdom, through Jeste and Vahia's comparison with modern models.[^s5]
- **Related:** its evenness in success and failure echoes equanimity and the Stoic dichotomy of control; *karma yoga* is its path of action; and practical wisdom names knowing what one's situation requires.[^s1][^s2]
