# Buddhism

Buddhism is the family of traditions that trace themselves to Siddhārtha Gautama, called the Buddha ("awakened one"). At its centre is a diagnosis and a cure: life is pervaded by suffering or unsatisfactoriness (*dukkha*), that suffering has causes, it can cease, and there is a path to its cessation.[^s1] Its sources present these Four Noble Truths less as a creed to be believed than as realities to be understood and acted on.[^s2] It treats wisdom (*paññā*, Sanskrit *prajñā*) as seeing things as they actually are.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Gautama was born into the Śākya clan near today's India–Nepal border. Older estimates put his life at about 560–480 BCE; many scholars now think he died around 405 BCE.[^s1] Traditional accounts say he left household life after confronting ageing, sickness and death, tried meditation teachers and severe asceticism, found both insufficient, and reached awakening through insight and meditation before teaching for about forty-five years.[^s1][^s2]

He was one of the *śramaṇas*, wandering seekers who were dissatisfied with Vedic ritual and sacrifice and did not accept the Vedas as final authority. They shared with some Upaniṣads ideas of rebirth, karma and liberation through overcoming ignorance; the Buddha's distinctive claim was that the crucial ignorance is the conceit of an "I" and "mine".[^s1] His teaching survives in the Pāli *Nikāyas* and the Chinese *Āgamas*, which were transmitted orally for centuries, written in languages he probably did not speak, and shaped by later sectarian disputes, so scholars disagree about how much of the Buddha's own view can be recovered.[^s1] Theravāda, prevalent in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, preserves the Pāli canon; Mahāyāna sūtras in Sanskrit, Chinese and Tibetan came later.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The tradition offers a carefully argued account of how our own reactions add to our pain. In the first discourse attributed to the Buddha, the second truth locates the origin of suffering in craving that drives further becoming, and each truth comes with a task: suffering is to be comprehended, its origin abandoned, its cessation realised and the path developed.[^s3] It also joins wisdom to warmth: the four "immeasurable" attitudes of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy and equanimity are cultivated across schools, and equanimity there means meeting situations without prejudice so that care can extend to everyone equally, not indifference.[^s4]

## Key teachings

- **The Middle Way.** The first discourse rejects both sensual indulgence and self-torment and identifies the middle way with the Noble Eightfold Path, from right view to right concentration.[^s3]
- **The Eightfold Path in three trainings.** Its factors are grouped into wisdom (right view, right intention), ethical conduct (right speech, action, livelihood) and concentration (right effort, mindfulness, concentration); "right" (*sammā*) is closer to "complete" or "perfect".[^s2]
- **Three marks of existence.** Conditioned things are impermanent, unsatisfactory and not-self.[^s1] On one reading the Buddha rejects a permanent substance-self while accepting an evolving process-self in everyday life; scholars still debate how far the denial goes.[^s1][^s2]
- **Dependent origination.** Phenomena arise in dependence on conditions, and the traditional twelve-link chain runs from ignorance to ageing and death; modern scholarship treats the twelve-link list as a later compilation of shorter early formulas.[^s1][^s2]
- **Nirvana.** Literally a "blowing out" of the fires of craving, aversion and delusion; on what follows death the Buddha left the question undetermined.[^s2]
- **Ethics and ideals.** Lay followers keep five precepts against killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying and intoxication. Theravāda practitioners mostly aim to become *arhats*, while Mahāyāna practitioners take the bodhisattva vow to become Buddhas for the sake of all beings and cultivate six perfections, from generosity to wisdom.[^s4]

Schools disagree on much of this. Some hold that liberation needs both meditation and analysis, while the Chan tradition stresses meditation;[^s1] Mahāyāna texts criticise earlier disciples for seeking nirvana only for themselves, a charge that may be unfair to actual Theravāda practice;[^s4] and scholars argue over whether Buddhist ethics is best read as a virtue ethics, a form of consequentialism, or something that fits no Western category.[^s4]

## Common misreadings

- **Pessimism.** *Dukkha* covers frustration and alienation arising from impermanence, not only pain, and the diagnosis comes with a claim that suffering can end.[^s1] Letting go of craving is not meant to abandon love or compassion.[^s2]
- **"Buddhism is just mindfulness."** Modern mindfulness-based interventions deliberately present mindfulness as non-proprietary. Within the tradition, "right" mindfulness is one factor of a path that depends on right view, right effort and ethical discernment.[^s5] Lindahl argues that secular programmes should be judged empirically on their own terms, not by fidelity to Buddhist doctrine.[^s5]
- **A charter of free inquiry.** The *Kālāma Sutta* is often quoted as telling people to accept only what they verify for themselves. A close reading suggests its criteria are more ethical than epistemological, draw partly on authority, tradition and public opinion, and end in the Kālāmas taking refuge in the Buddha.[^s6] The tradition's caution about clinging to views is expressed instead in the raft simile, which discourages dogmatic attachment even to the Buddha's own doctrines.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

As a tradition, Buddhism is judged by argument, interpretation and long practice, so its status here is normative. Clinical programmes borrowed from it are studied empirically, but the tradition's goal, the end of suffering, has no agreed measure, so mindfulness trials do not test Buddhist claims directly.[^s5] Its teachings on suffering are philosophical and spiritual, not treatment: anyone in persistent distress should seek professional or crisis support.

## Connections

Buddhism was **introduced by the Buddha**, Siddhārtha Gautama, and grew from the **śramaṇa movement** of wandering ascetics, whose shared ideas it reworked; it **contrasts with Vedic ritualism**, whose sacrifices and scriptural authority the śramaṇas rejected.[^s1] It is often **confused with mindfulness-based interventions**, which adapted one of its practices outside its ethical and soteriological frame.[^s5] It belongs to **Indian philosophy**.[^s1] Its teaching on **impermanence**[^s1] and its cultivation of **compassion** and **equanimity**[^s4] relate it to those nodes, and its recollection of death and its teaching on stilling hatred are drawn on in the articles on **memento mori** and **forgiveness**.
