# Epicureanism

Epicureanism is the school of ancient philosophy that grew from the teaching of Epicurus. It holds that pleasure is the only thing good in itself, but it defines the highest pleasure not as excitement or luxury but as tranquillity, *ataraxia*, together with freedom from bodily pain.[^s1][^s2] Epicurus saw philosophy as a therapy for life, as medicine is for the body.[^s1]

## Origin

Epicurus was born in 341 BCE and grew up on Samos. He studied with followers of Democritus and of Plato, taught in Mytilene and Lampsacus, and around 306 BCE settled in Athens, where he bought the property that gave the school its nickname, the Garden.[^s1][^s2] The Garden was both a school and a community whose members tried to live by its teachings, and Epicurean communities spread across the Hellenistic world after his death in about 270 BCE.[^s2] Its later voices include Philodemus, whose writings were recovered from the papyri of Herculaneum, and the Roman poet Lucretius, whose *De rerum natura* follows the founder's doctrine closely.[^s1] The school declined with the rise of Christianity and was partly revived in the Renaissance and early modern period.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Epicureanism offers a sober account of what a good life needs, and asks people to notice which desires and fears are doing the steering. Epicurus held that it is never too early or too late to care for the soul.[^s3] His claims are practical: most of what we need is easy to get, much of what we fear is not worth fearing, and good friends are the surest protection we have.[^s4] The questions arise whenever someone weighs a promotion against their evenings or faces illness and death.

## Key teachings

- **Pleasure as calm, not thrill.** Epicurus distinguished "moving" pleasures, felt while a need is being met, from "static" pleasures, the settled state of being free of want, and ranked the static kind highest.[^s2] Pleasure reaches its limit once everything that causes pain is removed (*Principal Doctrines* 3).[^s4]
- **Choosing among pleasures.** Some pleasures bring greater pains later and some pains are worth accepting; plain food pleases as much as rich food once hunger is gone.[^s3]
- **Limit your desires.** Desires are natural and necessary, natural but unnecessary, or empty, and natural wealth is limited and easy to obtain while wealth built on empty opinion has no end (*PD* 15).[^s4][^s1] The remedy is to reduce desires rather than feed them.[^s2]
- **Prudence first.** Prudence, or practical wisdom, is the greatest good and the source of the other virtues, and one cannot live pleasantly without also living wisely, honourably and justly (*PD* 5).[^s3][^s4] On this view the virtues are valued as the means to a tranquil life.[^s2]
- **Death is nothing to us.** Good and evil require sensation, and death is the end of sensation, so it cannot harm us (*PD* 2).[^s4][^s3] Fear of it rests on a mistake.[^s1]
- **The gods are not to be feared.** The gods are blessed and untroubled and do not meddle in human affairs; fear of divine punishment feeds superstition, which the study of nature dispels (*PD* 1, 11–12).[^s1][^s4]
- **Friendship.** Of everything wisdom provides for a happy life, the greatest by far is friendship (*PD* 27).[^s4] Epicurus even held that the wise person would die for a friend.[^s1]
- **Justice as agreement, and a quiet life.** Justice is a compact neither to harm nor be harmed (*PD* 33).[^s4] Once reasonable security is won, the most untroubled life comes from withdrawal from the crowd (*PD* 14), and the wise avoid political competition so as to "live hidden".[^s4][^s5]

Later Epicureans compressed this into the *tetrapharmakos*, or four-part remedy: the gods are not to be feared, death is not to be feared, the good is easy to obtain and the bad easy to endure. It is preserved by Philodemus rather than in a text by Epicurus himself.[^s5] Epicureans also disagreed among themselves: Cicero's *De finibus* (1.65–70) reports rival accounts of whether friends are loved only for the security they bring or come to be loved for their own sake.[^s6]

## Common misreadings

- **"Epicurean" as gourmet indulgence.** Epicurus explicitly denied that by pleasure he meant the pleasures of the prodigal or of sensuality; he meant freedom from pain in the body and trouble in the soul.[^s3] His own life was moderately ascetic, and the "eat, drink and be merry" stereotype fits the rival Cyrenaics better.[^s2]
- **Selfishness without loyalty.** The ethics is egoistic in structure, yet it expects great loyalty to friends; scholars debate how the two fit.[^s1][^s6]
- **Atheism.** Epicurus did not deny the gods; he denied that they punish or reward.[^s1]
- **Hiding from all duty.** The advice to avoid politics is about escaping the anxieties of ambition and competition, within a theory in which justice and security matter a great deal.[^s4][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

As a philosophical tradition, Epicureanism is judged by argument and long practice, so its status here is normative. Cicero attacked its hedonism, and scholars still ask whether egoism can support the self-sacrifice it expects between friends.[^s1][^s6] Its calm also depends on its physics: the claim that death is nothing to us rests on the view that the mind is made of atoms that disperse at death.[^s2] Its argument against fearing death is a philosophical consolation, not a treatment; anyone facing persistent fear, grief or distress about illness or dying should seek professional or crisis support.

## Connections

Epicureanism was **introduced by Epicurus**[^s1] and **influenced by Democritus**, whose atomism Epicurus learned from Democritus's followers.[^s2] It **contrasts with Cyrenaic hedonism**, whose pursuit of ever-new gratifications Epicurus rejected,[^s1] and is often **confused with intemperance**, the indulgence the *Letter to Menoeceus* explicitly disowns.[^s3] Its ethics is **introduced in the Letter to Menoeceus**[^s3] and in the **Principal Doctrines**,[^s4] which also apply it to **facing mortality** through the argument that death is nothing to us.[^s4] It belongs to **Hellenistic philosophy** and is a form of **ethical hedonism**.[^s2] It teaches the **classification of desires** and grounds **contentment** and **simplicity** in the limit of natural wealth,[^s4] and it puts **friendship** at the centre of **eudaimonia**.[^s4] See also **Stoicism** and **hedonic and eudaimonic well-being**.
