# Seven Sages of Greece

The Seven Sages were a group of archaic Greek statesmen, lawgivers and thinkers whom later Greeks held up as models of practical wisdom. The usual names are Thales of Miletus, Solon of Athens, Bias of Priene, Pittacus of Mytilene, Chilon of Sparta, Cleobulus of Lindus and Periander of Corinth, though the membership was never settled.[^s6] What they share is less a doctrine than a style: short, memorable sayings about self-knowledge, moderation, timing and the limits of human fortune, offered by people who also governed cities and gave advice.[^s5][^s6]

## Origin

The sages lived in the late seventh and early sixth centuries BCE. Ancient tradition said the title "sage" was first given to Thales when Damasias was archon at Athens, in 582/1 BCE, and then to all seven.[^s5] Stories about them seem to have circulated orally in the sixth and early fifth centuries, before writers fixed them in literature.[^s6] Plato's *Protagoras* contains the best-known early list of seven: it includes Myson rather than Periander and says the sages dedicated "Know thyself" and "Nothing overmuch" at Apollo's temple at Delphi as the first-fruits of their wisdom.[^s5] Later writers kept changing the roster. Diogenes Laertius notes that there was no agreement on who made up the seven, and that the scholar Hermippus counted seventeen candidates from which different authors chose.[^s1][^s6]

A favourite legend tied the group together. A tripod was found in a fisherman's catch, Delphi ruled that it should go to the wisest, and each sage in turn passed it on to another, until it was dedicated to the god.[^s1] The story turns modesty itself into a mark of wisdom.

## Why it matters

The tradition is one of the earliest Western attempts to say what a wise person is like. Its answer is practical: the sage is someone whose judgment helps a city, a ruler or a friend at a decisive moment, and whose wisdom fits in a sentence that can be remembered when it is needed.[^s5][^s6] Several of its sayings still frame how people think about wisdom. Asked what is difficult, Thales is said to have answered "to know oneself", and asked what is easy, "to give advice to another".[^s1] That asymmetry, clear-sighted about others and blind about ourselves, is the core of what psychologists now call Solomon's paradox.[^s7] Solon's lessons serve the same purpose in a different setting: a run of success is a reason to look harder at one's own excess and at how much depends on luck, not a reason to stop checking.[^s3][^s4]

## Key teachings

- **Know yourself.** Ancient sources credited this maxim to Thales, to Chilon, to the sages as a group, or even to Phemonoë, a legendary figure of Delphi, which shows how freely sayings moved between names.[^s1][^s5]
- **Nothing in excess.** "Nothing too much" was attached to Solon among others, and stood for measure in desire, speech and power.[^s2][^s5]
- **Excess breeds hubris, and hubris brings ruin.** In his poem on good order (fragment 4), Solon warns that the leaders of the people, lured by wealth, act unjustly, cannot keep excess in check, and that much grief will follow from their great hubris.[^s4] Diogenes reports a related saying of his: wealth breeds satiety, and satiety outrage.[^s2]
- **Look to the end.** In Herodotus, Solon tells the wealthy king Croesus that human life is largely chance, that the god raises many up only to ruin them, and that no one should be called fortunate before death, only lucky.[^s3]
- **Guard your words and trust character.** Solon is credited with calling speech the mirror of action and secrecy the seal of speech, and with advising people to trust good character more than an oath.[^s2]

## Common misreadings

- **A real school or meeting.** The sages did not found a school, and the scene of all seven gathering at Delphi is hard to credit, though their dates just allow it.[^s5] One scholar even argued that Plato invented the group; the historian Johannes Engels rejects this, pointing to earlier oral and literary traces.[^s6]
- **Securely attributed sayings.** Most maxims reached us through much later collectors, and the same saying is often given to different sages.[^s1][^s6] "Call no man happy until he is dead" is a later compression of Herodotus's scene, not a quotation.[^s3]
- **Pure contemplation.** These were remembered mostly as people of action: Solon gave Athens laws, and even Thales' reputation as a sage seems to have rested on practical advice and his prediction of an eclipse rather than on theory.[^s2][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

As a tradition of moral advice, the sages' teaching is judged by argument and long use, so its status here is normative. Little is known reliably about the historical men, and the lists, anecdotes and sayings reflect the authors who transmitted them.[^s5][^s6] One saying has modern support: in three experiments with 693 adults, people reasoned more wisely about another person's relationship conflict than about their own, and the gap disappeared when they took a distanced view of themselves.[^s7] That confirms an asymmetry the sages noticed; it does not test the tradition as a whole.[^s7]

## Connections

Its teachings were **taught by** sages such as **Thales**, the first to be called a sage and the source of many of its sayings, and **Solon**, whose surviving poems state its warnings about excess in his own words.[^s1][^s4][^s5] The tradition's maxim on self-knowledge is **supported by** the Grossmann and Kross experiments on Solomon's paradox, though only weakly, as one saying among many.[^s7] It is the traditional source of **know thyself** and of the **Delphic maxims** more broadly, and its "nothing overmuch" is related to the later **doctrine of the mean**.[^s5] Solon's warnings about wealth, excess and the ruin of the powerful make it an early teacher about **hubris**.[^s3][^s4] Solon's role as adviser to Croesus links it to **seeking counsel**, and Plato already has Socrates invoke the sages and their Delphic maxims, tying the tradition to **Socratic philosophy**.[^s3][^s5]
