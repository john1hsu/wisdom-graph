# Judaism

Judaism is the monotheistic religion that began with the ancient Hebrews, centred on belief in one transcendent God and on a covenant that, according to the Torah, God made with Abraham and renewed with Isaac, Jacob and Moses; in the early 21st century there were nearly 15 million Jews worldwide.[^s1] Its scriptures are the Hebrew Bible, especially the Torah (the five books traditionally attributed to Moses), and it distinguishes this *Written Torah* from an *Oral Torah* that was later written down and compiled.[^s1] As a wisdom tradition it is less a set of maxims than a way of living in which study, debate and the training of character are themselves religious duties.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Israelite religion was centred on worship in Jerusalem from about 1000 BCE; the Second Temple was destroyed by Rome in 70 CE, and the diaspora that followed reshaped the tradition.[^s1] Rabbinic Judaism grew up to replace Temple-based practice: Johanan ben Zakkai's programme put the study of Scripture, prayer and piety in place of sacrifice and pilgrimage, which made Judaism practicable anywhere.[^s2] Around 200 CE Judah the Prince compiled the Mishnah, which records disputed as well as accepted views, and teachers of the following centuries built on it to produce the Palestinian and Babylonian Talmuds.[^s2] Later streams include the mystical Kabbalah (12th–13th centuries) and Hasidism (18th century), and in the 19th century Reform and Conservative Judaism emerged as modifications of Orthodox practice.[^s1]

## Why it matters

For anyone trying to live wisely, Judaism offers an unusually developed model of wisdom as a *communal and argumentative* practice. In rabbinic academies legal decisions were reached through debate, and the Mishnah's habit of preserving minority opinions keeps alternatives on the record rather than erasing them.[^s2] Its ethics also treats character as something formed deliberately, by habit, law and self-examination, rather than something one simply has.[^s4][^s5] These ideas apply well beyond religious life: in a team meeting, a family disagreement or a mentoring relationship, the questions of whom you learn from and how you disagree are often where wisdom shows or fails.[^s3][^s6]

## Key teachings

**Wisdom as learning from everyone.** *Pirkei Avot* ("Sayings of the Fathers"), a tractate of the Mishnah on the conduct of a life under Torah, collects the ethical sayings of early sages.[^s2] In it Ben Zoma asks "Who is wise? He who learns from every man", and goes on to redefine the other goods people chase: the mighty person is one who governs his own urges, the rich person one who is content with his portion, and the honoured person one who honours others.[^s3] Each definition moves the measure of success from outward status to inner discipline and regard for others.[^s3]

**Disagreement for the sake of truth, conducted with humility.** The Babylonian Talmud (*Eruvin* 13b) tells of a long dispute between the schools of Hillel and Shammai, in which a heavenly voice declares that both views are "the words of the living God" yet the law follows Hillel.[^s6] The reason the Talmud gives is character, not cleverness: Hillel's students were agreeable and patient, bore insults calmly, taught Shammai's views alongside their own and even cited them first.[^s6] The lesson often drawn is that how one disagrees matters as much as the argument itself.[^s6]

**The middle way in character.** The medieval philosopher and legal authority Maimonides, drawing on Plato and Aristotle, treated the soul as capable of health and sickness, and virtue as a settled disposition at the mean between two vices, formed by habituation.[^s4] In his code he writes that the right way is the mean in each character trait, and he allows a deliberate swing to the opposite extreme as therapy for a bad habit.[^s4] He also departs from Aristotle: the pious person may go beyond the mean, anger is to be avoided as an extremely bad trait, and meekness is ranked as the highest virtue.[^s4]

**Musar: working on one's character.** In 19th-century Lithuania, Israel Salanter founded the Musar movement, which called for regular self-analysis and the emotionally intense study of ethical texts so that people could confront their own impulses; it brought ethical training into the curriculum of many yeshivas.[^s5]

## Common misreadings

Judaism is often reduced to "the religion of the Old Testament", but rabbinic Judaism reads Scripture through centuries of Mishnah, Talmud and commentary, and it was this study-centred form that let the tradition survive without a Temple.[^s2] It is also not monolithic. Maimonides' rationalism, in which moral virtue serves intellectual perfection, sits beside mystical streams such as Kabbalah and Hasidism, and scholars still disagree about which of Maimonides' own views represents his settled position.[^s1][^s4] Even the Musar movement was opposed by rabbis who feared its stress on ethics would displace Torah study or breed sectarianism.[^s5] In the original, each of Ben Zoma's definitions is grounded in a biblical verse, context that is easily lost when the sayings circulate as free-standing maxims.[^s3] This article describes these teachings; it does not adjudicate between Jewish movements or between Judaism and other traditions.

## Connections

Judaism **teaches** **contentment** and **self-control** through Ben Zoma's redefinitions of riches and strength in *Pirkei Avot*.[^s3] Through the Hillel–Shammai story it **teaches** a form of **intellectual humility**: taking the opposing view seriously enough to teach it first.[^s6] Maimonides' ethics is **influenced by** **Aristotelian virtue ethics**, and through it the tradition **teaches** a version of the **doctrine of the mean** that both adopts and revises Aristotle, linking to **moral habituation**, **anger** and **humility**.[^s4] It **teaches** the practice of **Musar**.[^s5] It belongs to the **Abrahamic religions** alongside **Christianity**, and its biblical layer is described in **Hebrew wisdom literature** and the **Book of Proverbs**.[^s1] Related nodes include **tzedakah**, the **Golden Rule**, **Maimonides**, ***Pirkei Avot*** and the **Talmud**.[^s2][^s4]
