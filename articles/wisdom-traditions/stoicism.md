# Stoicism

Stoicism is a school of Greek and Roman philosophy that treats wisdom as a way of life. It holds that only virtue is truly good, that health, wealth and reputation are "indifferent" to happiness even when it is reasonable to prefer them, and that the goal is to live in agreement with nature, including our nature as reasoning and social beings.[^s1] Modern scholars describe it as a eudaimonist virtue ethics: virtue alone is enough for a flourishing life.[^s2]

## Origin

Zeno of Citium founded the school in Athens around 300 BCE. It is named after the *Stoa Poikilē*, the painted porch in the Agora where its first teachers lectured.[^s1] Zeno read the Socratic dialogues closely and studied with the Cynic Crates, and the Stoics revived Socrates' view that reason governs action.[^s1] Historians usually divide the tradition into three phases: the early Stoa of Zeno, Cleanthes and Chrysippus, the school's great systematiser; a middle period including Panaetius and Posidonius; and the Roman Stoics, among them Seneca, Musonius Rufus, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius.[^s1][^s2] The labels for these periods are themselves debated.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Stoicism offers a worked-out account of how judgment shapes experience. Epictetus begins his *Handbook* by dividing things into those that are up to us, such as our judgments, desires and actions, and those that are not, such as our body, property and reputation (*Enchiridion* 1).[^s3] He argues that people are disturbed less by events than by their opinions about them; death, for instance, frightens us because we judge it terrible (*Enchiridion* 5).[^s3] This is a practical claim about where to put effort and attention, and it has travelled far: the modern revival of Stoicism draws on Viktor Frankl's logotherapy and early cognitive behavioural therapy,[^s2] and Albert Ellis credited the Stoics with discovering the central premise of his rational emotive behaviour therapy.[^s5]

## Key teachings

- **Virtue is the only good.** The virtues, including prudence, justice, courage and moderation, are forms of knowledge, and whoever has one has them all.[^s1]
- **Preferred and dispreferred indifferents.** Health and wealth are normally to be chosen and sickness and poverty avoided, but neither makes a life happy or unhappy; what matters is how one uses them.[^s1][^s2]
- **What is up to us.** Freedom comes from wanting what is in our power and accepting what is not; Epictetus advises wishing events to happen as they actually do (*Enchiridion* 8).[^s3]
- **Passions as mistaken judgments.** Destructive passions are excessive impulses resting on false judgments of value. The wise person is free of them but still has "good feelings" (*eupatheiai*) directed at real goods, and involuntary first reactions are not yet passions.[^s1][^s2]
- **The sage and progress.** The sage, who alone has true knowledge, is an ideal; even the school's leaders did not claim to have reached it, so most people are at best making moral progress.[^s1]
- **Nature, providence and cosmopolitanism.** The Stoics were causal determinists who identified fate with rational, providential order, and they held that all human beings belong to one universal city under a common law.[^s1] Concern for others widens outward from oneself through *oikeiōsis*, the natural affinity that grounds justice.[^s1][^s2]

Stoics did not agree on everything. Chrysippus rejected an early view that all indifferents are equal, Posidonius found parts of Chrysippus's account of the passions inadequate, and Panaetius departed from orthodox physics.[^s1] Seneca was willing to borrow from Epicurus when he thought Epicurus was right.[^s2]

## Common misreadings

- **"Stoic" as a stiff upper lip.** The lowercase word for someone who endures without showing feeling differs from the philosophy, and *apatheia* means freedom from disturbing passions, not suppressing all emotion.[^s2] A pre-registered study across samples in New Zealand, Norway and the United States found that this popular "Stoic ideology", especially wanting to feel fewer emotions and to express fewer, was linked to lower hedonic and eudaimonic well-being.[^s4]
- **Withdrawal from the world.** The Stoics criticised the Epicurean retreat from public life and expected the wise to take up civic roles;[^s2] their cosmopolitanism made justice central rather than optional.[^s1]
- **Passive resignation.** Accepting what is not up to us goes with full responsibility for what is: our judgments, choices and actions.[^s3]
- **A self-help toolkit only.** The ethics sat within a system that joined logic and physics; ancient Stoics compared logic to bones and sinews and ethics to the flesh of one living whole.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

As a philosophical tradition, Stoicism is judged by argument and long practice, so its status here is normative. Its link to cognitive therapy is historical and conceptual: Ellis cited Epictetus directly, and Aaron Beck referred to Stoic authors in early work, but this lineage is not itself evidence that Stoic exercises work.[^s5] Direct tests are thin. A Royal Holloway report on a free four-week online Stoic course run during the 2020 lockdown described average gains in self-reported resilience and life satisfaction, but it reported no control group, so the changes cannot be confidently credited to the course.[^s6] The clearest peer-reviewed finding cuts the other way for the popular caricature: emotional suppression labelled as "stoicism" is associated with worse well-being.[^s4] People in serious or persistent distress should seek professional or crisis support rather than rely on philosophy alone.

## Connections

Stoicism was **introduced by Zeno of Citium**[^s1] and **influenced by Socratic philosophy**, as well as by the Cynics.[^s1] It **contrasts with Epicureanism**, its main Hellenistic rival over pleasure, tranquillity and public life.[^s2] It is often **confused with emotional suppression**, a popular misreading that research links to lower well-being.[^s4] It belongs to **Hellenistic philosophy** and is a form of **virtue ethics** aimed at **eudaimonia**.[^s2] Its teachings include the **dichotomy of control**[^s3] and the **cardinal virtues**,[^s1] and practices such as **memento mori**, **negative visualization** and the **view from above** are rooted in it.
