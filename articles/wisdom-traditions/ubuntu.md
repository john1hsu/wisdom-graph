# Ubuntu

Ubuntu is an ethical tradition of sub-Saharan, and especially southern, Africa. The word comes from Nguni languages such as Zulu and Xhosa and means "humanness"; to have ubuntu is to live a genuinely human life.[^s3] The same idea is called *hunhu* in Shona and *botho* in Sotho-Tswana, and all these words share the Bantu root *-ntu*, "human".[^s1] Its best-known expression is the Nguni maxim *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*, usually rendered "a person is a person through other persons".[^s2][^s1]

## Origin

Ubuntu began as an oral, lived ethic, carried in proverbs, customs and the counsel of elders rather than in founding texts.[^s1] Its written history is recent. Christian Gade traced the word in print back to at least 1846, and found that until about 1950 writers used it only for a quality of a person, roughly "humaneness". From the 1960s onwards some authors began to call it a philosophy, an ethic or "African humanism", and only in the 1990s did the *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* maxim become the standard way of defining it.[^s4] South Africa's 1993 interim constitution, which spoke of a need for ubuntu but not for victimisation, set off a wave of writing about what the word means.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Many theories of wisdom start from the individual; ubuntu starts from the relationship. On Metz's reading, it agrees with Aristotle that a good life is the realisation of our nature, but holds that this self-realisation happens only through relating well to others, not partly through solitary goods such as contemplation.[^s3] It is a counterweight to treating wisdom as a private achievement. It has also shaped public life: South Africa's Constitutional Court invoked ubuntu when it struck down the death penalty, and Metz argues that an ubuntu ethic was largely behind the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's mostly non-retributive approach to apartheid-era crimes.[^s3]

## Key teachings

- **Personhood is achieved, not given.** In this tradition one becomes a person, in the moral sense, by honouring relationships with others; humanity in this sense is relational and partly shaped by other people, rather than something an isolated individual simply has.[^s2][^s1]
- **Identity and solidarity.** Metz analyses the communal relationship that ubuntu prizes into two parts: *identity*, thinking of oneself as part of a "we" and sharing projects, and *solidarity*, helping others and caring about how their lives go. Together they amount to a broad kind of friendship or love.[^s3][^s2]
- **Humaneness in practice.** Mangena lists humaneness, gentleness, hospitality and generosity as core qualities, illustrated by the custom of giving strangers food, shelter and water and by Shona proverbs about sharing.[^s1] Metz adds kindness, compassion, forgiveness and tolerance to the associated virtues.[^s3]
- **Wisdom lives in community and elders.** Knowledge is treated as experiential and handed down through elders' stories and proverbs, and respect for elders follows from their role as custodians of moral wisdom.[^s1]
- **Seek agreement, repair harm.** Metz argues that ubuntu favours consensus-seeking decisions, in which people look for broad agreement rather than settling matters by majority vote, and favours repairing relationships over punishment, though not to the point of forbidding the use of force to protect the innocent.[^s2]

Two practical questions follow. Before a hard decision at work, have the people it affects been heard, and will they still be able to work together afterwards?[^s2] After a conflict, is the goal to win, or to restore a relationship in which both sides can keep contributing?[^s3]

## Common misreadings

- **"Ubuntu means the group always wins."** Scholars disagree here. Mangena defends the priority of the community over the individual, while Onyebuchi Eze sees person and community as shaping each other with neither on top.[^s1] Metz grounds human rights and dignity in ubuntu, which rules out sacrificing one person simply because it would benefit many.[^s2]
- **"Ubuntu is African humanism."** Mangena argues that humaneness, a moral quality of a person, should not be conflated with humanism as a worldview, and Gade notes that early texts using the label rarely said what it meant.[^s1][^s4]
- **"Ubuntu requires forgiveness."** Tutu credited ubuntu with making confession, forgiveness and reconciliation possible.[^s1] Keolebogile Mbebe argues that the commission's use of it pressured victims to forgive, and that ubuntu rests rather on accountability, restitution and repairing relationships.[^s6] No one is obliged to forgive; where harm has left lasting distress, professional support is worth seeking.
- **"Ubuntu is an unchanged ancient doctrine."** Its written, philosophical form is largely a twentieth-century development, and Eze warns that presenting it as a unanimous pre-colonial tradition romanticises the past.[^s4][^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Ubuntu is a moral and philosophical tradition, judged by argument and lived practice rather than by experiment. Critics raise three main objections: that it is too vague to guide action, that it is collectivist and neglects individual freedom, and that it grew out of small-scale traditional societies and may not fit a modern, plural one.[^s2] Bernard Matolino and Wenceslaus Kwindingwi go further, arguing that its promotion in post-apartheid public life has been an elite identity project and that the conditions it presupposes no longer hold.[^s5] Metz replies by restating ubuntu as a precise principle about honouring relationships of identity and solidarity.[^s2] The debate is open.

## Connections

Ubuntu belongs to **African philosophy**.[^s1] It **contrasts with Aristotelian virtue ethics**: both see the good life as realising human nature, but ubuntu places that realisation wholly in relationships.[^s3] It is often **confused with humanism**, a label scholars say blurs a moral quality into a worldview.[^s1][^s4] It has been **applied in reconciliation** after political conflict, most famously in South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission,[^s1][^s3] though its link to **forgiveness** there is disputed.[^s6] It is **challenged by "The end of ubuntu"**, Matolino and Kwindingwi's critique of its revival.[^s5] It is related to **compassion** and **empathy**, which Mangena names among its central qualities,[^s1] to **virtue ethics**, since it describes the good life as self-realisation marked by virtues such as kindness, compassion and tolerance,[^s3] to **hedonic and eudaimonic well-being**, since it offers an objective rather than pleasure-based account of the good life,[^s3] and to **seeking counsel**, given the role of elders as keepers of moral wisdom.[^s1]
