# Socratic philosophy

Socratic philosophy is the tradition of inquiry that grew from the conversations of Socrates in fifth-century Athens and from the many followers who wrote about him and founded schools in his name. Its core is a way of life more than a doctrine: question what you and others take for granted, admit what you do not know, and put the health of your soul, meaning your character and understanding, ahead of money, status and comfort.[^s2][^s3] Almost every ancient school claimed Socrates as its founder or model; the Epicureans were the notable exception.[^s1]

## Origin

Socrates was born in 469 BCE and was executed in 399 BCE, after an Athenian jury convicted him of irreverence toward the city's gods and of corrupting the young.[^s1] He wrote nothing, so everything we know comes from others. Plato shows a questioner who disclaims knowledge, Xenophon a practical adviser who prizes self-mastery, Aristophanes' *Clouds* a comic sophist, and Aristotle, born after his death, a philosopher who asked what-is-it questions about ethics.[^s1][^s2] Reconstructing the historical figure from these clashing portraits is known as the Socratic problem, and scholars still disagree about how far Plato's Socrates speaks for Socrates or for Plato.[^s1][^s3]

His followers took his example in different directions. Plato founded the Academy; Antisthenes taught that virtue is enough for happiness and was claimed as a forerunner by Diogenes and the Cynics; Aristippus of Cyrene and Euclides of Megara led circles of their own; later, Zeno the Stoic said Socrates inspired him, and the sceptic Arcesilaus adopted his method of questioning and refuting.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The tradition joins two things people often keep apart: humility about what one knows and firmness about how to act. Socrates told his jury that his mission was to urge Athenians to care first about the improvement of the soul rather than money or reputation, and he called himself a gadfly stinging a sluggish city awake (*Apology* 29d–30e).[^s4] He lived that out in public: at the trial of the generals after Arginusae he alone among the presiding councillors refused to put an illegal motion to the vote, and under the Thirty he refused an order to help arrest Leon of Salamis.[^s1] For anyone who has to decide, lead or advise without certainty, the lesson is that admitting the limits of your knowledge does not excuse you from acting on your best judgment of what is right.[^s1][^s2]

## Key teachings

- **The examined life.** Socrates told his jurors that "the life which is unexamined is not worth living" (*Apology* 38a).[^s4] The point is to test one's motives and convictions.[^s2]
- **Questioning as a method.** In the elenchus, Socrates asks for a definition (What is courage? What is piety?) and then shows, from the other person's own answers, that it does not hold together; many dialogues end in *aporia*, an honest impasse.[^s3] Scholars disagree about whether this can establish truths or only expose inconsistency.[^s2]
- **Human wisdom is knowing its limits.** Socrates set his own simple ignorance against the *double ignorance* of people who do not know but think they do; this is the teaching known as Socratic ignorance.[^s2]
- **Virtue is a kind of knowledge, and the virtues form a unity.** In the *Protagoras*, courage is knowledge of what is and is not to be feared, and Socrates argues that the virtues are, in some sense, one.[^s3]
- **No one does wrong willingly.** Wrongdoing comes from ignorance of the good, since everyone desires what they believe is good; on this view, knowing what is best cannot be overpowered by pleasure or passion, which denies weakness of will.[^s2][^s3]
- **Happiness and the soul.** The Socratic dialogues may be the first to make *eudaimonia*, living well, the aim of ethics. Doing injustice damages the soul, so it is better to suffer wrong than to do it.[^s2][^s3]

## Common misreadings

- **"I know that I know nothing."** This popular line is a paraphrase rather than a sentence of the *Apology* (compare 21d),[^s4] and Socrates held firm convictions, such as never to harm anyone wrongfully.[^s2]
- **Socrates as a sophist.** Aristophanes lumped him with the paid teachers of rhetoric, and Plato's Socrates says that caricature prejudiced his jury.[^s1] He denied being anyone's teacher and said no one could show he took pay (*Apology* 33a–b).[^s4]
- **Question-asking as a trap.** The aim was to help people examine themselves; in the *Theaetetus* Socrates compares himself to a midwife who helps others give birth to ideas and test them.[^s2]
- **One settled doctrine.** His followers drew different lessons from him, and scholars dispute whether doctrines such as intellectualism were his own.[^s2][^s3]

## Living it today

Before acting on a key word in a decision (fair, urgent, success), ask what it means, test your own answer as hard as a colleague's, and admit uncertainty when it is real. In a disagreement, the less wise move is to win; the Socratic move is to keep asking until both people see which of their shared beliefs conflict.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

As a philosophical tradition, Socratic philosophy is judged by argument and long practice, so its status here is normative. Its intellectualism is disputed, since it seems to deny that people ever knowingly act against their better judgment.[^s3] Its method has a measurable modern descendant. In cognitive therapy for depression, a study of 55 patients found that sessions in which therapists used more Socratic questioning were followed by larger symptom drops, even after controlling for the therapeutic alliance; the design was correlational and small, so it shows association, not proof that questioning causes improvement.[^s5] Philosophy is no substitute for care: people in persistent distress should seek professional or crisis support.

## Connections

Socratic philosophy was **introduced by Socrates**, whom we meet only through others' writings.[^s1] It is often **confused with sophistry**, the confusion that Aristophanes popularised and that helped convict Socrates.[^s1] It **contrasts with Epicureanism**, the one major school that did not claim him and that criticised his irony and his neglect of natural science.[^s2] It belongs to **ancient Greek philosophy** and is an early, eudaimonist form of **virtue ethics**.[^s3] Teachings and practices rooted in it include **Socratic ignorance**, **know thyself** and **self-reflection**, and it shaped **Stoicism**, whose founder said Socrates inspired him.[^s2] Its denial of **weakness of will** remains one of its most debated claims.[^s3]
