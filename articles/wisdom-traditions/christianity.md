# Christianity

Christianity is the religion based on the teachings of Jesus of Nazareth, with the Bible, especially the New Testament, as its scripture. It began in the first century CE and is now followed by more than two billion people on every continent, mainly in the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Protestant branches.[^s1] Alongside its claims about God and salvation, which this article describes rather than evaluates, it carries a large body of practical wisdom about desire, love, forgiveness and the use of possessions, worked out by theologians, monks and ordinary communities over two thousand years.[^s2][^s5]

## Origin

The movement began among Jews who believed Jesus was the messiah, soon became mostly non-Jewish, and was shaped by Paul and other missionaries. Rome persecuted it until Constantine I supported it, and from the fifteenth century missionaries carried it across much of the world.[^s1] Its wisdom teaching grew out of Jewish wisdom literature. Proverbs 8 and the Wisdom of Solomon picture Wisdom as present with God before creation, and early Christian hymns applied those traits to Christ (Colossians 1:15–20).[^s6] In the sayings tradition behind Matthew and Luke, Jesus speaks as Wisdom's envoy; Matthew goes further and presents him as Wisdom herself, adapting an invitation from Sirach (Matthew 11:28–30).[^s6]

## Why it matters

Much of the tradition's wisdom concerns what people love and want, not only what they know. Jesus warns his hearers to guard against greed because "one's life does not consist of possessions" (Luke 12:15), and the parable that follows mocks a rich man who builds bigger barns and dies that night (12:16–21).[^s3] The same chapter ties attention to possessions: the heart follows wherever one's treasure is (12:34).[^s3] This makes the tradition a resource for anyone weighing how much is enough, what to worry about, and whom their choices serve.[^s3][^s4]

## Key teachings

- **The dual commandment.** Love God and love your neighbour (Matthew 22:37–39) sums up the ethic; the second table of the Ten Commandments, including the ban on coveting, is held to bind everyone.[^s2]
- **A deeper law.** In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus extends the commandments inward, treating anger as kin to murder and lust as kin to adultery.[^s2]
- **The reversal of wisdom.** Paul sets the crucified Christ against human standards of wisdom, including religious speculation as a route to spiritual perfection (1 Corinthians 1:18–25).[^s6]
- **Virtues natural and given.** Thomas Aquinas, whose ethics is centred on virtue, held that "grace perfects and builds on nature."[^s5] He combined Aristotle's acquired virtues (prudence, justice, temperance, fortitude) with the theological virtues of faith, hope and charity, which he regarded as gifts directing people to God; perfect prudence, he argued, needs the moral virtues.[^s5] Imperfect happiness is possible in this life through virtue and contemplation; perfect happiness lies in the vision of God.[^s5]
- **Diagnosing the vices.** In the fourth-century Egyptian desert, Evagrius Ponticus listed eight "generic thoughts" (gluttony, lust, avarice, sadness, anger, acedia, vainglory and pride) to help monks recognise and handle their inner struggles. John Cassian brought the scheme to the Latin West and Gregory the Great reshaped it into seven capital vices.[^s7]
- **Greed as a capital vice.** Aquinas defines covetousness as an "immoderate love of possessing": wanting external goods beyond what one's condition of life needs.[^s4] It opposes generosity (liberality) as an inner attachment and justice when it takes what belongs to others. It counts as a capital vice because money promises self-sufficiency, and it breeds fraud, falsehood, restlessness, violence and hardness to mercy (II-II q.118 aa.1, 3, 7–8).[^s4]

## Common misreadings

- **"Christian ethics is one uniform rulebook."** Emphases differ: Catholic leaders have issued social encyclicals, Protestant bodies usually speak through assemblies, and an Anglican–Roman Catholic report treated some disputed moral questions as matters of pastoral judgment.[^s2]
- **"The seven deadly sins are in the Bible."** The list grew from a monastic diagnostic scheme, Evagrius's eight thoughts, later revised by Gregory.[^s7]
- **"Greed is only about money."** Gregory, quoted by Aquinas, says covetousness can also be for knowledge or high position (q.118 a.2).[^s4]
- **"Christianity rejects reason."** Paul's critique targets wisdom used as a path to self-perfection or status, while Aquinas built a whole ethics on reason perfected by grace.[^s6][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

Christianity's teachings are judged here as a tradition, by argument and long practice rather than experiment, so the evidence status is normative; they rest partly on faith claims that this graph does not assess.[^s1][^s5] Scholars also disagree about details of its history, such as how far early hymns drew on Wisdom imagery.[^s6]

At work, Aquinas's test, whether a good is wanted beyond what one's condition of life needs, gives a practical check on ambition: name the need a target serves before chasing the next one.[^s4] Gregory's point that covetousness extends to position applies to status as much as pay.[^s4]

## Connections

Christianity was **introduced by** Jesus of Nazareth and grew within Judaism.[^s1] It was **influenced by** Hebrew wisdom literature, especially the figure of personified Wisdom in Proverbs and the Wisdom of Solomon.[^s6] It **teaches** that **greed** (covetousness) is a capital vice opposed to generosity and justice,[^s4] the scheme of the **seven deadly sins** that grew out of Evagrius's eight thoughts,[^s7] and the **theological virtues** of faith, hope and charity alongside the **cardinal virtues**.[^s5] Its teachings on the **golden rule**, **forgiveness**, **compassion** and **stewardship** link it to other traditions in this graph, and its analysis of desire invites comparison with the **Stoic** and **Buddhist** treatments of craving and contentment.[^s2][^s4]
