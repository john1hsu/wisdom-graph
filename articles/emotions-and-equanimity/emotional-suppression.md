# Emotional suppression

Emotional suppression, or more precisely *expressive suppression*, is the effort to stop a feeling from showing once it has already arisen: keeping the face still, the voice level and the body quiet.[^s1] Psychologists contrast it with reappraisal, which changes how a situation is understood before the emotion has fully formed. Because suppression acts late, it mainly changes outward behaviour, not the feeling itself.[^s1] It matters for wisdom because composure that only hides a reaction is easily mistaken for the steadiness that comes from seeing a situation clearly.

## Origin

The modern concept comes from James Gross's process model of emotion regulation, which sorts strategies by when they act: antecedent-focused strategies such as reappraisal work on the situation's meaning, while response-focused strategies such as suppression work on the response already under way.[^s1] In 2003 Gross and Oliver John published the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, which measures how habitually people use each strategy, and five studies linking those habits to emotion, relationships and well-being.[^s1] This article concerns hiding the expression of a feeling; deliberately pushing unwanted thoughts out of mind is a related but distinct strategy.

## Why it matters

Hiding a feeling is sometimes the right call, and every culture asks for it at times. The trouble is suppression as a default. In Gross and John's samples, habitual suppressors reported less positive and more negative emotion, shared both good and bad feelings less, received less social support, especially emotional support, and were rated by peers as less close to others.[^s1] They also reported more depressive symptoms and lower life satisfaction and well-being.[^s1] Suppression was strongly tied to a sense of inauthenticity, of showing a self that does not match what one feels inside, which may explain much of the extra negative feeling.[^s1]

The costs reach other people too. When pairs of strangers discussed an upsetting topic, a partner told to suppress disrupted the conversation, reduced rapport and raised the *other* person's blood pressure.[^s2] At work and at home, then, a poker face can leave others less at ease and less sure where they stand.[^s2]

## Evidence

The core experimental findings are clear. In the Butler studies, suppression, but not reappraisal, disrupted communication, hindered relationship formation and raised blood pressure in both partners.[^s2] In Jane Richards and Gross's experiments, people told to hide their reactions to a distressing film or to slides of injuries remembered less of what was said, while people who reappraised remembered as well as controls; habitual suppressors also recalled fewer of their own emotional life events.[^s3] The authors attribute this to the constant self-monitoring that suppression demands.[^s3]

The picture is less simple across cultures. Gross and John found that men reported more suppression than women, and that ethnic minority participants in the United States reported more than European Americans.[^s1] Among 71 European American and 100 Hong Kong Chinese students, suppression predicted poorer life satisfaction and more depressed mood only for the European Americans, which suggests cultural norms about restraint shape its effects.[^s4] Sharpening the point, Chen, Burton and Bonanno separated how *often* people suppress from how *well* they can suppress when needed: frequency was tied to more depression in U.S. but not Chinese students, while the ability to suppress went with fewer symptoms in both groups.[^s5] Their data were cross-sectional and not preregistered, so cause and effect cannot be settled.[^s5]

Most habit findings are correlational, the experiments are short laboratory tasks, and cultural moderation is well documented, so the evidence status here is *mixed*: suppression as a rigid default is reliably linked to costs, but the flexible, situational use of restraint is not.[^s1][^s4][^s5]

## How to spot it

You may be relying on suppression if your usual response to a strong feeling is to clamp down on any sign of it, and the feeling stays just as strong.[^s1] Other signs are a gap between how you look and how you feel, rarely telling anyone how something affected you, and finding that you missed or forgot parts of a tense conversation.[^s1][^s3] People close to you saying they never know what you are thinking is another.[^s1][^s2]

## How to fix it

The aim is flexibility, not constant expression. Researchers treat the ability to dial expression up or down as the situation demands as adaptive, while a fixed habit of suppression is what carries the costs.[^s5] When a feeling starts, try changing how you read the situation before you manage your face: what else could this mean, and what matters here?[^s1][^s3] If the setting does call for restraint, hold back for now but come back to it later, by naming the feeling to yourself and sharing it with someone you trust.[^s1] Notice the norms around you, since what counts as healthy restraint varies across cultures and settings.[^s4] If low mood or emotional numbness persists, that is a reason to talk to a doctor or mental-health professional, not to try harder to hide it.

A common misreading treats Stoicism as the philosophy of the stiff upper lip. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy calls the idea that Stoics aimed to suppress emotion "grossly incorrect": their *apatheia* was freedom from passions rooted in mistaken judgements, something closer to equanimity, and they counted healthy feelings such as delight among the sage's.[^s6] The everyday sense of "stoic", enduring without showing feeling, is a much thinner idea.[^s6]

## Connections

Emotional suppression is a form of **emotion regulation** that **contrasts with cognitive reappraisal**, its antecedent-focused alternative, and it **undermines social support**, especially emotional support.[^s1] Its costs are **demonstrated by** the Butler study of the **social consequences of suppression**[^s2] and by Richards and Gross's **emotion-regulation and memory** experiments.[^s3] The view that suppression is always harmful is **challenged by** the cross-cultural **suppression paradox** study, which separates frequency from ability,[^s5] alongside the Hong Kong comparison.[^s4] It is often **confused with equanimity** and with Stoic *apatheia*, which aim at a calm that comes from judgement rather than concealment.[^s6] Related entries include **compassionate reappraisal**, which applies reappraisal to grievances, and **rumination**, **resilience** and the **dichotomy of control**.
