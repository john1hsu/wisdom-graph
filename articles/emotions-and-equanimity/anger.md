# Anger

Anger is the heat that follows the judgment that we have been wronged or obstructed. Psychologists describe it as a normal and often healthy emotion, ranging from mild irritation to rage, that comes with a faster heart rate and higher blood pressure.[^s6] For wisdom, the question is not whether we feel it but what it does to our judgment, and how we answer it: who with, about what, how strongly and for how long.[^s1]

## Origin

The wisdom traditions disagree sharply about anger. In the *Nicomachean Ethics* (IV.5), Aristotle treats it as raw material for a virtue. The good-tempered person is one who is "angry at the right things and with the right people," in the right way, at the right time and for the right length of time.[^s1] He names vices on both sides: the hot-tempered, choleric, sulky and bad-tempered on the side of excess, and on the side of deficiency an unnamed failing in people who never get angry, which he calls foolish and even slavish when it means putting up with insults to oneself or one's friends.[^s1] There is no formula for the right amount; it depends on the particular case and on perception.[^s1]

Seneca's *On Anger* rejects this middle path. On the Stoic analysis, anger is a desire for revenge that rests on assenting to the judgment that one has been wronged.[^s2] Involuntary first jolts, the flush and start that even a sage feels, are not yet anger; the emotion proper begins only when we agree that revenge is called for.[^s2] Because emotions are irrational, Seneca argues, they cannot be tamed, only replaced. A moderate emotion is as absurd as "moderate insanity," and the wise person punishes or defends because it is appropriate, not out of anger.[^s2] The same work describes his nightly self-examination (III.36).[^s2]

In Mahāyāna Buddhism, Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra* (ch. 6) argues that anger badly harms the person who feels it, always involves a conceptual mistake, and must be countered through meditation on patience.[^s3] His central argument is causal. Harmful acts arise from conditions, and people who harm us are driven by afflictive emotions, so the fitting response is a wish to help them become free of those emotions rather than rage.[^s3] Scholars disagree about whether this means no one ever deserves blame, or whether ordinary practices of holding people responsible survive.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Anger changes how we think, not only how we feel. In Lerner and Tiedens' review, anger brings appraisals of certainty, personal control and someone else's blame, and these carry over into later judgments that have nothing to do with the original provocation.[^s4] Angry people make more optimistic estimates of risk and more risk-seeking choices than fearful people. They blame individuals more and punish more harshly, trust less, and lean on stereotypes and quick heuristics instead of weighing arguments carefully.[^s4] That works against the habits wise reasoning depends on: entertaining other interpretations and weighing evidence carefully.[^s4] At work it looks like a sharp email sent in the first minute; at home, like an old grievance that resurfaces in every argument.

## Evidence

Laboratory work supports the claim that anger biases judgment, though most studies induce mild anger in students and measure short-term choices, so real-world effect sizes are uncertain.[^s4] Lerner and Tiedens also stress that anger differs from other negative emotions: its sense of certainty and control makes people more optimistic, not more cautious.[^s4] The claim that "letting it out" helps has fared badly. A 2024 meta-analysis of 154 studies, with over 10,000 participants, found that activities that lower physiological arousal reduced anger and aggression substantially (g ≈ −0.63), consistently across gender, age and culture. Activities that raise arousal had no reliable overall effect, and the authors conclude that the findings do not support venting.[^s5] Anger's effects are real but context-dependent, and a popular remedy fails.

## How to spot it

- **Certainty about motives.** You feel you know exactly why they did it, without having asked.[^s4]
- **A hunger to punish.** You want the other person to pay, sometimes for something unrelated to what made you angry.[^s4]
- **Physical signs.** A faster heart rate and rising blood pressure accompany it; noticing them is an early warning.[^s6]
- **Wrong target or wrong size.** On Aristotle's checklist, the anger is aimed at the wrong person, is bigger than the offence, comes at the wrong moment or lingers long after.[^s1]
- **Cynicism or passive aggression.** Anger that is swallowed rather than dealt with can leak out sideways.[^s6]

## How to fix it

- **Lower the heat first.** Slow breathing, muscle relaxation, mindfulness and similar calming activities are the best-supported quick remedies.[^s5][^s6]
- **Do not vent to feel better.** Hitting things or ranting to "get it out" tends to keep anger going rather than releasing it.[^s5][^s6]
- **Catch the judgment, not only the feeling.** Stoic and Buddhist arguments both turn on noticing the thought behind the anger ("they wronged me on purpose") and testing it, or seeing the conditions that drove the other person.[^s2][^s3] Psychologists teach a similar move: replace exaggerated, absolute thoughts with accurate ones.[^s6]
- **Delay consequential decisions.** Since anger carries over into unrelated choices, postpone punishments, big purchases and sharp replies until you are calm.[^s4]
- **Then act, assertively.** Calm is not silence. State the need behind the anger plainly and respectfully, and solve the problem it points to.[^s6][^s1]
- **Get support when it persists.** If anger feels out of control or is damaging your relationships or work, a psychologist or other licensed professional can help.[^s6]

## Connections

Anger **undermines deliberation** and **wise reasoning** through its appraisals of certainty and blame.[^s4] It is **explained by the appraisal-tendency framework**.[^s4] The benefit of calming over venting is **demonstrated by** Kjærvik and Bushman's **meta-analysis of arousal-lowering and arousal-raising activities**.[^s5] Anger is often **confused with aggression**, though the emotion can be expressed assertively without hurting anyone.[^s6] Aristotle's **good temper** and the **doctrine of the mean** place anger between two vices; Seneca and the **Stoics** seek to replace it, in the analysis Seneca sets out in **De Ira** (*On Anger*); **Śāntideva** and **Buddhism** counter it with **patience**.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Seneca's **evening review** was meant partly as a check on anger.[^s2] Related remedies include **self-distancing**, **cognitive reappraisal** and **forgiveness**; **rumination** keeps anger alive, and **emotional suppression** is the opposite mistake to venting.[^s6]
