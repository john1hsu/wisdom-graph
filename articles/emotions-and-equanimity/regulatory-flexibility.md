# Emotion regulatory flexibility

Emotion regulatory flexibility, usually shortened to *regulatory flexibility*, is the ability to adjust how you cope with and regulate emotion to fit what a situation demands, rather than relying on a single favourite strategy.[^s1] The psychologists George Bonanno and Charles Burton treat it as a set of abilities on which people differ, not as a trait of any one strategy.[^s1] It matters for wisdom because it applies to feelings the same insight wise reasoning applies to judgments: what is right depends on the particulars.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin

The idea grew out of Bonanno's research on how people cope with loss and potential trauma. In a 2004 study of 101 students who had started college in New York City just before the September 11 attacks, the ability both to exaggerate and to hide emotional expression on cue each predicted less distress almost two years later, after controlling for initial distress.[^s2] A combined flexibility score predicted adjustment too.[^s2] In 2013 Bonanno and Burton set out the full framework, criticising what they called the *fallacy of uniform efficacy*: the habit of labelling strategies such as suppression or problem-solving as always good or always bad, although their value depends on fit with the situation.[^s1] Amelia Aldao, Gal Sheppes and James Gross offered a parallel framework in 2015 for studying how people adjust regulation across situations in both everyday and clinical settings.[^s6]

## Why it matters

The framework names three abilities used in sequence.[^s1] *Context sensitivity* is reading what the moment demands and allows. *Repertoire* is having a range of strategies to draw on, such as expressing or hiding a feeling, rethinking a situation, or engaging with or stepping back from a problem. *Feedback responsiveness* is noticing whether the chosen strategy is working and then keeping, adjusting or switching it.[^s1] Seen this way, the problem with habitual emotional suppression is less the strategy itself than its use as a default regardless of setting.[^s1]

In the follow-up study, the ability to both enhance and suppress expression was moderately stable over three years, and students low in it were rated by friends as less well adjusted under heavy life stress, while those high in it did not show that decline.[^s3] Bonanno and Burton also report that bereaved people with complicated grief showed smaller expressive repertoires.[^s1]

## What good looks like

A flexible person can stay composed in a meeting where a display would backfire, and then speak openly with a friend in the evening.[^s1][^s3] They do not insist that venting is always healthy, nor that keeping a poker face is always strong.[^s1] They notice when a strategy has stopped helping, for instance when holding a feeling in has left them distracted, and try something else.[^s1] In daily-life studies, people who leaned on a few chosen strategies in a given moment, rather than using all of them at once, reported less negative feeling.[^s5]

## How to practise it

Researchers have mostly measured this ability rather than trained it, so the following applies the framework rather than offering a tested programme.[^s1][^s6] Before reacting, ask what this setting calls for and what it permits: who is present, what is at stake, and whether there will be a better moment later.[^s1] Widen your repertoire deliberately: if you always hide feelings, practise naming one to a trusted person; if you always vent, practise waiting and rethinking.[^s1][^s2] Afterwards, check the result: did the feeling ease, did the conversation go better, and what would you try next time?[^s1] Pay attention to cultural norms, since the costs and benefits of restraint differ across cultures.[^s1][^s4] If distress, numbness or grief persists, that is a reason to seek support from a doctor or mental-health professional rather than to search for the perfect strategy alone.

## Evidence and limits

A meta-analysis of 329 studies and 58,946 participants found a small-to-moderate association between coping flexibility and psychological adjustment (r = .23), but with wide variation across studies.[^s4] Effects were larger when flexibility was defined as fit between strategy and situation or as perceived ability, and smaller when it meant only a large repertoire or variety; they were also larger in less individualistic countries and older samples.[^s4] The authors note that the concept has been defined inconsistently.[^s4] Experience-sampling data from four samples linked using a mix of strategies in a given moment to lower negative feeling, but the data were correlational, not preregistered and did not measure situational context directly.[^s5] The landmark laboratory studies used still pictures, student samples and modest numbers.[^s2][^s3] Bonanno and Burton add that too much switching could become erratic and that measuring context sensitivity is hard.[^s1] The evidence status here is therefore *mixed*.

## Connections

Regulatory flexibility is a form of **emotion regulation** that **addresses emotional suppression** by treating restraint as one option to be used when it fits, rather than a fixed habit.[^s1] It was **introduced by George Bonanno**, with Charles Burton.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** the 2004 study of students after September 11[^s2] and **supported by** the coping-flexibility meta-analysis[^s4] and, more tentatively, by the daily-life study of strategy variability.[^s5] It is **applied in adversity**, where flexible people kept their adjustment under heavy life stress.[^s3] Related entries include **resilience**, which the flexibility account helps explain, **compassionate reappraisal**, a specific strategy for the repertoire, and **wise reasoning**, which shares its attention to context.[^s1][^s6]
