# Rumination

Rumination is the habit of going over and over your distress, its possible causes and its likely consequences, in a passive loop that rarely ends in a decision or in acceptance. In the account developed by the psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema, it is a passive, repetitive focus on one's low mood and what that mood implies, a way of responding that helps bring on and prolong depression.[^s1][^s2] It is close kin to worry, which loops on what might happen; rumination usually loops on what has already happened and what it says about you. Both fall under the broader heading of repetitive negative thinking.[^s2][^s4]

Rumination is tempting because it feels like reflection, and much of the wisdom in dealing with it lies in telling the two apart.[^s3]

## Origin

The worry that we torment ourselves with our own thoughts is old. In Letter 13, Seneca tells his friend Lucilius that more things frighten us than actually crush us, and that "we suffer more often in imagination than in reality."[^s5] He blames the habit of exaggerating, imagining and anticipating sorrow, and notes that we rarely test or examine the things we fear.[^s5] (Popular versions paraphrase this line; the wording here is Gummere's translation.)

The modern concept comes from Nolen-Hoeksema's response styles theory, first set out in 1991, which proposed that the way people habitually respond to a sad mood shapes whether depression starts and how long it lasts.[^s1][^s2] Later work split rumination into two parts that relate to depression differently: *brooding*, a moody, self-critical and evaluative pondering, and *reflective pondering*, a deliberate turning inward to understand and solve one's problems.[^s6][^s2] Edward Watkins later argued that repetitive thought is not harmful in itself: it can help people plan and recover from upsetting events, or keep them stuck.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Rumination undermines the very things it promises. A major review links it to the onset and worsening of depression, poorer problem solving and the erosion of social support.[^s1] It is also associated with anxiety, binge drinking and binge eating.[^s1] Abstract, evaluative rumination makes people worse at solving interpersonal problems, while thinking about the same situation concretely can improve it.[^s2]

For wisdom, the cost is judgement. In the laboratory, people who relived an angering conflict from the inside dwelt on specific events and blame and stayed angrier, while the wider view dropped out of sight.[^s3] At work the pattern shows up as replaying a meeting or a rejected proposal for days; in relationships, as rehearsing a grievance until it hardens.

## How to spot it

- **The loop test.** You have covered this ground before and nothing new has come of it.[^s2]
- **"Why me?" rather than "what next?"** The questions are abstract and evaluative, about causes, meanings and what the event says about you, rather than about what happened and how to act.[^s2]
- **Re-living instead of reviewing.** You are back inside the scene, feeling the original anger or shame, rather than seeing it as an observer.[^s3]
- **Self-critical brooding.** The tone is moody and judgemental toward yourself.[^s2][^s6]
- **Mood follows thought.** You feel worse after thinking, not clearer.[^s2]

## How to fix it

- **Step back before you ask why.** In experiments, people who recalled an angering experience and asked why they felt as they did reacted less only when they viewed the scene from a distance, as an observer; asking why while immersed in it kept the anger alive.[^s3] Imagining what you would tell a friend is one way to take that distance.
- **Get specific.** Shift from abstract questions to concrete ones: what exactly happened, in what order, and how could I take one step on it?[^s2]
- **Test the fear.** Seneca faulted us for not putting our fears to the test or examining them; do that instead of rehearsing them.[^s5]
- **Take a deliberate break.** In experiments, people who were already low felt worse after a few minutes of induced rumination than after the same time spent imagining unrelated scenes, so an absorbing activity can stop the loop deepening; return later with a concrete question.[^s2]
- **Seek help when it persists.** If rumination comes with lasting low mood, sleeplessness or hopelessness, that is a reason to speak with a doctor or mental health professional, or a crisis line if you are in danger, not to try harder alone. Structured therapies can reduce it.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The link with depression is well established: rumination predicts the onset of depressive episodes (more consistently than their duration), and in experiments induced rumination worsens sad mood in people who are already low, though not in others.[^s1][^s2] A meta-analysis of 36 randomized trials of depression treatments found that cognitive-behavioural therapies reduced repetitive negative thinking, with a small-to-moderate effect that shrank after adjustment for publication bias. Rumination-focused CBT and concreteness training were among the stronger approaches, but trials were of modest quality and relied on self-report.[^s4]

The limits matter. Not all repetitive thought is harmful: reflective pondering predicted lower depression a year later in one longitudinal analysis, and the content, context and style of the thinking shape the outcome.[^s2][^s6] Researchers also use "abstract" and "concrete" in different ways: Watkins finds abstract, evaluative thinking unhelpful, while Kross and colleagues find distanced, insight-oriented thinking helpful, so the common thread is that immersed, self-critical replaying does harm.[^s2][^s3] Most experiments use students and brief laboratory tasks. This article is a general reflection, not clinical advice.

## Connections

Rumination is **explained by response styles theory**, Nolen-Hoeksema's account of how responses to low mood shape its course.[^s1] It **undermines problem solving** and **undermines social support**.[^s1] It is often **confused with self-reflection**, the adaptive, distanced processing of difficult feelings.[^s3] It sits under **repetitive negative thinking** and beside **worry**.[^s2][^s4] Remedies elsewhere in this graph include **self-distancing**, which tested this distinction directly, **compassionate reappraisal** and **forgiveness**, which replace the replay of a grievance, and the bounded Stoic exercise of **negative visualization**, which is meant to end in preparation rather than dread; the steadiness all of them aim at is **equanimity**.[^s3][^s5]
