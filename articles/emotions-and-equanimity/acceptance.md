# Acceptance

Acceptance, in the psychological sense, is the habit of meeting your own painful emotions and thoughts without judging them or trying to push them away: noticing that you feel anxious, hurt or ashamed and letting that be a natural reaction rather than a further problem.[^s1] Researchers describe it as an open, welcoming stance toward feelings, thoughts or events whose aim is not to change the feeling but to have it without struggling to control it.[^s3] A related, older sense is acceptance of facts that cannot be changed, such as a loss, a decision already made or a diagnosis.[^s4] Neither sense means approving of what happened or giving up on what can still be done.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The idea is old. Epictetus advised his students not to demand that events turn out as they wish, but to want them to happen as they actually do, and said that life would then go well (*Handbook* 8).[^s6]

The modern psychological concept grew mainly out of behaviour therapy, especially acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), which treats willingness to have unwanted experiences as one of several linked processes alongside mindfulness, defusion from thoughts and commitment to values.[^s3] Emotion researchers later began to study acceptance as one strategy among others. It has an awkward place in their models: James Gross's process model does not list it explicitly, and some researchers file it under attentional deployment while others count it as a form of reappraisal.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Much distress is secondary: feeling bad about feeling bad. Across three studies, Brett Ford and colleagues found that people who habitually accept their negative emotions and thoughts reported greater well-being and fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety, even after accounting for reappraisal, rumination and life stress.[^s1] In the lab they felt less negative emotion while giving a stressful speech, and in a two-week diary study they reported less daily negative emotion, which in turn predicted better psychological health about six months later.[^s1] The authors suggest that acceptance helps people avoid reacting to, and so prolonging, their negative states, letting those states pass sooner.[^s1] It was not linked to fewer positive emotions.[^s1]

For wisdom, acceptance clears the ground for judgment: effort spent fighting a feeling, or arguing with a fact that is already settled, is effort not spent on what can still be shaped, which is why active acceptance goes with better outcomes than resignation.[^s4]

## What good looks like

- **Feeling without fighting.** A skilled person can say "I'm furious" or "I'm scared" plainly, without adding self-criticism for feeling it, and without being run by it.[^s1][^s3]
- **Accepting experiences, not necessarily circumstances.** Ford's team found that accepting one's mental experiences was distinct from accepting situations, and that only the former was consistently tied to better health; resigning oneself to a situation that could be changed may do harm over time.[^s1]
- **Active, not resigned.** In a study of 534 adults responding to hypothetical unchangeable events, Nakamura and Orth separated active acceptance, which went with positive outcomes, from resigning acceptance, which went with negative ones.[^s4] Active acceptance takes in the fact and then turns toward what is still possible.[^s4]

## How to practise it

- **Name what is there.** Put the feeling or thought into plain words, such as "this is disappointment", without arguing with it.
- **Drop the second layer.** Notice judgments about the feeling ("I shouldn't be this upset") and let them go as well.
- **Sort the situation.** Ask which parts are settled and which are still open; take in the settled parts as they are, as Epictetus advised.[^s6]
- **Turn toward a valued step.** ACT pairs acceptance with action guided by values; ask what matters now and take one step toward it.[^s3]
- **Check the result.** If you notice that "accepting" has become an excuse not to act on something changeable, that is resignation, not acceptance.[^s4]

## Common misreadings

Acceptance is often heard as passivity, approval or "just get over it", but it concerns how you relate to your own experience, and it can sit alongside strong action to change a situation.[^s1][^s4] It is also not suppression, which tries to hide or stop a feeling; experimental reviews treat the two as quite different strategies.[^s3] Finally, acceptance does not mean enduring serious or persistent distress alone. This article is general, not clinical advice; if painful feelings stay overwhelming or you feel unsafe, contact a doctor, a mental health professional or a crisis line.

## Evidence

Evidence for habitual acceptance is fairly consistent but correlational and mostly self-reported.[^s1] Experimental results are more mixed. A 2012 meta-analysis of 30 studies found that acceptance strategies beat other emotion regulation strategies for pain tolerance (g = 0.43), but not for pain intensity or negative affect.[^s2] A 2020 review notes that experiments define and instruct acceptance inconsistently, often blending several ACT components into one label, which makes results hard to compare.[^s3] Acceptance-based therapy as a whole has a strong record: a review of 20 meta-analyses covering 12,477 participants found ACT effective across anxiety, depression, substance use and pain, outperforming inactive controls and usual care, though not CBT.[^s5] That supports the package, not acceptance alone. Status: **mixed**.

## Connections

Acceptance is a form of **emotion regulation**. Its health benefits were **demonstrated by** the Ford et al. study of accepting negative emotions and thoughts,[^s1] and it is **supported**, with limits, **by** the 2012 **meta-analysis of acceptance strategies**, which found its clearest advantage in pain tolerance.[^s2] It **contrasts with emotional suppression**, which tries to hide or block a feeling rather than allow it,[^s3] and is **part of acceptance and commitment therapy**, where it sits beside mindfulness, defusion and values.[^s3][^s5] It is often **confused with resignation**, which research separates from active acceptance, and it is **applied in adversity**, when people face events they cannot change.[^s4] It is **rooted in Stoicism** through Epictetus's counsel to want events as they happen,[^s6] and so stands close to the **dichotomy of control** and the **Serenity Prayer**. It sits beside **equanimity**, **cognitive reappraisal** (some researchers class acceptance as a kind of reappraisal[^s3]), **regulatory flexibility**, **amor fati**, **impermanence**, **grief and loss** and **knowing when to quit**.
