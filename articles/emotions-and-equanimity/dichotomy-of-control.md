# Dichotomy of control

The dichotomy of control is the Stoic teaching that some things are up to us and others are not, and that a good life depends on telling them apart. Epictetus opens his *Handbook* with it: our opinions, impulses, desires and aversions are within our power, while our body, property, reputation and offices are not (*Enchiridion* 1).[^s2] In Margaret Graver's summary, what is up to us is how we use our impressions, and so our convictions, attitudes, intentions and actions; "externals" include bodily comfort, possessions, relationships, the outcomes of projects, and standing or power.[^s1]

## Origin

The teaching belongs to Epictetus (c. 50–135 CE), a Greek Stoic who was once enslaved in Nero's Rome, lectured there after gaining his freedom, and later ran his own school at Nicopolis; Arrian wrote up his teaching in the *Discourses*, and the *Handbook* is a brief abridgment of them.[^s1] Its key term is *prohairesis*, usually rendered "volition" or "moral choice": Graver explains it as the capacity for choice itself, which Epictetus calls unimpeded by nature and treats as the true self.[^s1] The idea draws on older Stoic ethics. Cicero, reporting the school's view, compares a person aiming a spear or arrow: the end is to do everything to aim straight, while actually hitting the mark is, in the Stoic phrase, "to be chosen" but not "to be desired" (*De Finibus* 3.22, ch. 6).[^s3] John Sellars traces the archer image to the earlier Stoic Antipater and notes that the Stoics counted the art of living among "stochastic" crafts, like medicine or navigation, whose results partly depend on luck.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Epictetus argues that much of our distress comes from wanting what is not ours to command: desire that fixes on externals is bound to be frustrated, and people are troubled less by events than by their judgments about them (*Enchiridion* 2, 5).[^s2] His remedy is to wish events to happen as they do, which he promises will let life go well (*Enchiridion* 8).[^s2] The point is not resignation but a reallocation of care. Because the use we make of externals is up to us even when externals are not, the teaching sends attention back to the one place where effort and character can make a difference.[^s1] Graver also notes Epictetus's answer to the worry that our character is itself shaped by birth and upbringing: reason can examine and correct its own views, so the self that chooses can improve over time.[^s1]

## How to apply it

- **Sort before you react.** When a situation upsets you, name which parts depend on your judgments and choices and which depend on other people, your body or chance.[^s2]
- **Hold the impression for a moment.** Epictetus advises pausing to examine an impression before assenting to it, so that a quick verdict ("this is a disaster") is checked before it drives action.[^s1]
- **Aim like the archer.** Choose the outcome you want, prepare for it fully, and then measure yourself by how well you acted rather than by where the arrow landed.[^s3][^s4]
- **Act on what is controllable.** Psychological research suggests the useful skill is matching the response to the situation: changing what can be changed and adjusting your own view of what cannot.[^s6]

At work, this means distinguishing the quality of a proposal from the committee's decision on it; in relationships, it means owning your words and actions while accepting that another person's feelings and choices are theirs.[^s1][^s2]

## Common misreadings

- **"Stop caring about outcomes."** The Stoics did not tell the archer to stop aiming. Outcomes are still worth choosing and pursuing; they simply should not be the measure of a good life.[^s3][^s4]
- **"Only think about it."** Graver lists actions and intentions among the things up to us, so the principle calls for action as much as for changed attitudes.[^s1]
- **Misjudging what is controllable.** Calling a fixable problem "out of my hands" can become an excuse. In a study of 170 adults who had recently faced a stressful event, better reappraisal ability went with lower depression when the stressor was uncontrollable but with higher depression when it was controllable.[^s5]
- **Misattributed sayings.** The Serenity Prayer, which asks for acceptance of what cannot be changed and courage to change what can, expresses a similar thought but is not a Stoic text. Its authorship was long disputed; quotation researcher Fred Shapiro now credits Reinhold Niebuhr, while saying the question is not fully settled.[^s7]

## Evidence

As a philosophical teaching, the dichotomy of control is judged by argument and long practice, so its status here is normative.[^s1] Modern research tests a related claim rather than the doctrine itself: that people adjust better when their coping fits how controllable a stressor is. A meta-analysis of 122 samples (58,946 participants) found that coping flexibility was modestly linked to better psychological adjustment (r = .23), with one of the larger effects for strategy–situation fit (r = .27).[^s6] The authors caution that the studies were mostly correlational and self-reported, heterogeneous, and drawn largely from middle-income samples in developed countries.[^s6] The reappraisal study above is also cross-sectional, so it shows an association, not a cause.[^s5] This principle is a reflective tool, not therapy: if worry or low mood persists, seek professional support, and in a crisis contact local emergency or crisis services.

## Connections

The dichotomy of control is **rooted in Stoicism** and was **introduced by Epictetus**, who placed it at the head of the **Enchiridion**.[^s1][^s2] It aims to **develop equanimity**, freedom from disturbance about what lies outside our power.[^s2] Its modern counterpart, fitting the response to the controllability of a problem, is **supported by the reappraisal–controllability study**[^s5] and by the **coping-flexibility meta-analysis**.[^s6] It belongs to **Stoic ethics**, where the archer image shows how its view of outcomes works,[^s3][^s4] and sits beside the Stoic exercises of **negative visualization** and the **view from above**. Outside the Stoic school it has kin in **acceptance**, **cognitive reappraisal**, whose usefulness depends on controllability,[^s5] and the **Serenity Prayer**.[^s7]
