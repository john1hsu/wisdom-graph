# Equanimity

Equanimity is evenness of mind toward everything you meet, whether it feels pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.[^s1] Contemplative researchers define it as an even-minded mental state, or a lasting tendency, that holds across all three kinds of experience, and argue it may be one of the most important elements in what meditation does for well-being.[^s1] It is not a lack of feeling. The Buddhist teacher and scholar Nyanaponika Thera describes it as a balance of mind rooted in insight, and warns that it is easily mistaken for dull or cold indifference.[^s5] The equanimous person still feels the sting of a setback or the pull of good news, but is not carried off by either.

## Origin

The word most often translated "equanimity" is the Pali *upekkhā* (Sanskrit *upekṣā*). In early Buddhist texts it is the fourth of the four "sublime attitudes" or immeasurables, alongside goodwill, compassion and empathetic joy.[^s4] In the Aṅguttara Nikāya (AN 10.196) a disciple free of covetousness and ill will fills every direction with an awareness imbued with equanimity, described as expansive and without hostility.[^s4] Nyanaponika calls equanimity the "crown and culmination" of the four, and grounds it in two insights: that beings are heirs of their own actions, and that much suffering comes from clinging to "mine" and "self".[^s5]

Greek philosophy reached a parallel ideal by a different road. For the Stoic Epictetus, a flourishing life includes *ataraxia* (imperturbability) and *apatheia*, freedom from the disturbing passions that arise from falsely judging external things to be good or bad.[^s6] The Stoic sage is not numb: he still has *eupatheiai*, "good feelings", such as rational joy.[^s6] The two traditions differ in their metaphysics, but both treat steadiness as something trained through understanding, not as temperament alone.[^s5][^s6]

## Why it matters

Strong reactions narrow attention to the immediate threat or prize, which is exactly when judgment is weakest. Equanimity keeps the other good qualities working under pressure. Nyanaponika argues that it gives love patience and gives compassion the courage to face suffering without being overwhelmed, so that a carer can stay present and useful instead of burning out.[^s5] At work it shows up as the manager who can hear bad numbers without blaming or freezing; in relationships, as the partner who can listen to criticism without counter-attacking.

## What good looks like

- **Feeling without being flooded.** Emotions arise and are noticed, but they inform action rather than dictate it.[^s1]
- **Even-handedness toward pleasure and pain.** Psychologists describe a second strand, *hedonic independence*: wanting and avoiding are less tightly coupled to how pleasant or unpleasant something feels.[^s2]
- **Warm, not cold.** Real equanimity includes and steadies compassion and goodwill rather than replacing them.[^s5]
- **Focused on what is yours to do.** Like Epictetus' distinction between what is and is not up to us, it spends effort on one's own judgments and responses.[^s6]

## How to practise it

- **Notice, then allow.** Monitor and Acceptance Theory holds that attention alone is not enough; pairing awareness of experience with an accepting stance is what reduces reactivity.[^s3]
- **Train it in meditation.** Contemplative traditions cultivate equanimity deliberately, and researchers have catalogued meditation techniques aimed at it.[^s1][^s5]
- **Practise on small things first.** Nyanaponika suggests loosening attachment gradually, beginning with minor possessions and preferences before the things you hold most tightly.[^s5]
- **Sort what is up to you.** Ask which part of the situation depends on your own judgments and choices, and which does not.[^s6]

## Common misreadings

Equanimity is often confused with **indifference**: not caring, emotional withdrawal or passivity. Nyanaponika treats this as its counterfeit, because true equanimity rests on understanding and vigilance, not emptiness.[^s5] Nor is it the suppression of feeling; Stoic *apatheia* removes disturbing passions, not all feeling.[^s6] And it does not mean tolerating harm: a calm mind can still decide to say no, set limits or leave. Equanimity is also no substitute for help when distress is persistent or overwhelming; that is a reason to seek professional or crisis support.

## Evidence and limits

Equanimity entered psychology only recently, and there is no agreed definition or measure.[^s1][^s2] One validated questionnaire splits it into an even-minded state and hedonic independence; in 265 French adults the even-minded factor was linked to less rumination and catastrophising and more adaptive emotion regulation, but it overlapped strongly with low neuroticism, and the two factors barely correlated with each other.[^s2] In randomised dismantling trials of mindfulness training, adding acceptance to monitoring reduced daily stress more than monitoring alone, and in a two-week trial lowered cortisol and blood-pressure reactivity, though an eight-week trial did not replicate the biological effect.[^s3] The evidence is therefore **mixed**: promising, largely correlational or from a few research groups, and not yet clear about what exactly is being measured.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

Equanimity is **rooted in Buddhism**, where it is the fourth sublime attitude,[^s4] and has a close counterpart **rooted in Stoicism** in *ataraxia* and *apatheia*.[^s6] It **complements compassion**, steadying it so that it can face suffering,[^s5] and is commonly **confused with indifference**, its cold imitation.[^s5] It **complements acceptance**, which emotion researchers treat as part of the same skill set,[^s3] and may help **address rumination**, though that link is so far correlational.[^s2] It is **studied in contemplative science** as an outcome of meditation.[^s1] It sits beside **negative visualization**, **memento mori** and the **dichotomy of control**, which practitioners use to cultivate it, and beside **impermanence** and **forgiveness**.
