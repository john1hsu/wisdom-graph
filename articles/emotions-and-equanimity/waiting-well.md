steps:
  - Write down what, if anything, you can still do before the news arrives, do it once (update the CV, gather the documents, line up a backup plan), and then stop re-checking.
  - Find out, if you can, when the news is due, so the wait has an end you can see.
  - Plan a few absorbing, moderately challenging activities for the hardest stretches, such as a sport, a craft, a puzzle or demanding work, rather than passive scrolling or television.
  - Practise a short daily mindfulness session for the worry that returns between activities, noticing anxious thoughts without arguing with them.
  - Look for moments of awe, such as a walk somewhere vast or beautiful, and for ordinary pleasant experiences too.
  - Tell one or two people what you are waiting for and when, and ask for company or distraction at the moments you expect to be hardest.
  - Save bracing for the worst for the final stretch; shortly before the news, consider the bad outcome and what you would do next, rather than rehearsing it from the first day.
example:
  situation: An engineer has interviewed for an internal promotion and is told the panel will decide "in the next couple of weeks".
  before: "Check email every twenty minutes, replay every interview answer, ask colleagues daily whether they've heard anything, and assume from day one that it's a no."
  after: "Ask the panel chair for the decision date, update the CV once in case it's a no, take on an absorbing design problem and book two evening climbing sessions, do ten minutes of mindfulness each morning, tell a friend the date, and think through the 'no' scenario properly only the day before."
  why: Most of the wait cannot change the outcome. The wiser response does what can be done, gives the mind something better than worry to hold, and keeps bracing for when it is useful, so the waiter arrives at the news less worn out and still able to respond well either way.

# Waiting well

Waiting well is the practice of getting through a stretch of waiting for important news, such as bar exam results, a hiring decision or a biopsy report, without letting worry fill it. Psychologist Kate Sweeny, whose laboratory at the University of California, Riverside has studied such waits for years, describes them as a combination of uncertainty and lack of control, two hard states at once.[^s5] Worry has a use when action can still prevent harm, but during a wait, she says, worry tends to get stuck.[^s5] The practice gathers what her group and others have found about what helps: acting where action is possible, then filling the remaining time with absorbing activity, mindfulness, awe and support, and timing any bracing for bad news well.[^s5][^s6]

## Origin

Sweeny's research tracks real people through real waits, including law graduates awaiting bar results, PhD students on the academic job market and patients awaiting test results, alongside laboratory waits for feedback on intelligence tests or peer ratings.[^s1][^s3][^s6] In a 2020 interview she listed the strategies her lab had found helpful: mindfulness, positive thinking about the future, experiences of awe and, the one she was most enthusiastic about, flow.[^s6] For bad news that may come, she recommends preparing emotionally and practically, gathering support and bracing for the worst as the news draws near, crediting the bracing idea to her mentor James Shepperd.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Waiting is where patience is tested most plainly. Trait patience, defined by Sarah Schnitker as a propensity to wait calmly in the face of frustration or adversity, was studied during waits by Sweeny and Schnitker across four studies; more patient people worried less and felt more positive emotion while waiting.[^s4] The associations were modest (in the pooled replications, about .28 with positive emotion and about -.14 with worry), and whether less patient people can learn to wait more calmly is, the authors say, still untested.[^s4] Waiting well is one practical answer to that question: a way to regulate the impatience and worry a wait provokes, which is what patience asks of us. It also matters for judgment, since a person who has spent two weeks rehearsing disaster arrives at the news depleted, whatever it turns out to be.

## How to apply it

- **Act, then stop.** Do the preparation that is actually useful, such as updating a CV before possible layoffs, and then let the matter rest.[^s6]
- **Seek flow, not just distraction.** Flow is full absorption in a challenge matched to one's skill. Sweeny says flow can make the time seem to fly by during a wait.[^s5] In the bar-exam study, many participants could name only passive activities such as reading and television, which rarely produce flow.[^s1]
- **Use mindfulness for the long haul.** Sweeny describes flow as an in-the-moment fix and mindfulness as the more important tool for managing worry over time.[^s5]
- **Look for awe.** In two experiments, a short awe-inducing video raised positive emotion and lowered anxiety during a wait compared with a neutral video.[^s3]
- **Time your support and your bracing.** In the bar-exam research, partners helped most just after the exam and just before results, less in the middle of the wait.[^s5] Mindful participants braced for the worst later in the wait rather than early, a pattern linked to coping better.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

The commonest is treating any distraction as good. Passive distraction leaves room for worry, and in Sweeny and Schnitker's data more patient people actually used less distraction and suppression.[^s1][^s4] A second is bracing from the first day: it costs weeks of distress for a benefit that matters only when the news arrives.[^s2][^s6] A third is confusing worst-case planning with worry. Sweeny notes that planning for the worst can give a comforting sense of control, which is different from turning the same fears over without acting.[^s5] A fourth is expecting any technique to remove the discomfort; the aim is a wait that is more bearable, not painless. Waits for serious medical news can be very hard, and distress that is intense or persists after the news is a reason to seek professional support, not only to try harder at these habits.

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but modest and uneven. In the flow research, flow went with less negative and more positive emotion across a bar-exam study (N = 125), a job-market study (N = 141) and a Tetris experiment (N = 309), but the experimental flow manipulation did not reduce worry, and the field studies were correlational.[^s1] Sweeny herself has called the research on how flow works a mess, with little good evidence yet.[^s5] The mindfulness research combined a correlational study (N = 150) with a meditation experiment (N = 90) in law graduates; benefits were largest for people low in optimism and high in intolerance of uncertainty.[^s2] The awe experiments (N = 729 in total) found only partial support, and awe did not always beat an ordinary pleasant video.[^s3] Most of this work comes from one laboratory and from student or graduate samples, so independent replication is still needed.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

Waiting well is offered here as a way to **develop** **patience** during the waits where it matters most, though Sweeny and Schnitker note that training patience in this way has not yet been tested.[^s4] It **addresses** **worry**, the main target of the flow research, and is **applied in** **uncertain waiting periods**.[^s1][^s5] It is **supported by** the flow-during-waiting studies, the mindfulness study of bar-exam takers and the awe-during-waiting experiments, all from Sweeny's laboratory, and **introduced by** **Kate Sweeny**, who has done most to popularise it.[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s5] It sits under **emotion regulation** and draws on **awe**, **social support** and **acceptance**; its late bracing resembles a timed form of **negative visualization**, and separating what one can still do from what one cannot echoes the **dichotomy of control**.
