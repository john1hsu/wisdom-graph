# Hedonic adaptation

Hedonic adaptation is the tendency for a lasting change in life, good or bad, to lose its emotional charge as it becomes ordinary. In its classic form, the "hedonic treadmill", people react briefly to events and then drift back to where they started.[^s1] It matters for wisdom because it bends judgment in two ways: it makes us overrate how much the next gain will change our lives, and, when a gain stops thrilling us, it tempts us to conclude that we chose wrongly and need something more.[^s4]

## Origin

Diener, Lucas and Scollon credit Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell (1971) with the treadmill model, in which people return to a neutral baseline after good and bad events alike.[^s1] Its best-known support came from Brickman, Coates and Janoff-Bulman's 1978 study, read as showing that lottery winners were no happier than others and people with spinal-cord injuries not much less happy than those who could walk.[^s1] That study compared just 22 Illinois lottery winners with 22 controls.[^s3]

The idea has a long pre-scientific cousin. Epicurus taught that "empty" desires, for great wealth or fame, can never be satisfied, and placed the goal of life in freedom from bodily pain and mental disturbance rather than in ever-greater acquisition.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Adaptation is double-edged. It helps people recover from many setbacks, yet it also erodes the joy of a promotion, a house or a relationship. Sheldon and Lyubomirsky's hedonic adaptation prevention model describes two routes back to baseline: the positive emotions and events that a change brings become less frequent, and the change becomes the new normal, so aspirations rise and people want more.[^s4] At work this can look like a raise that satisfies for a quarter; in relationships, like taking a partner for granted. The wise response is neither to chase each new high nor to dismiss all change as pointless, but to judge which changes are worth making and how to keep appreciating them.

## Evidence

The strong treadmill claim has been substantially revised. Reviewing longitudinal panel data, Diener and colleagues concluded that set points are not neutral, differ between people partly through temperament, can differ across components of well-being, and can change, and that people vary in how they adapt.[^s1] They reanalysed the 1978 study and found the injured group about 0.75 standard deviations less happy than controls, a large gap.[^s1] In German panel data, about a quarter of people changed significantly in long-term life satisfaction over 17 years.[^s1] Adaptation to disability was small, and unemployment left a lasting mark even after people found work again; widowed people adapted most, but over about eight years and not fully.[^s1]

A meta-analysis of 313 samples (65,911 people) across eight family and work events found that events differ in both their effects and how those effects change over time, that life satisfaction and mood respond differently, and that the size of an effect does not simply track how desirable the event is assumed to be.[^s2] British panel data point the same way: the boost from marriage peaks around the wedding and fades, while unemployment starts bad and largely stays bad.[^s5][^s1]

Money shows the split between feeling and evaluation. Among about 3,360 Swedish lottery players surveyed 5 to 22 years after their draw, larger prizes raised life satisfaction, with no sign of fading over time, but did not detectably change happiness or mental health.[^s3] The authors note that the 1978 lottery finding, rescaled, is close to their own happiness estimate, so the two studies do not conflict.[^s3] Adaptation, in short, is powerful but incomplete and uneven, which is why the evidence status here is *mixed*.[^s1][^s2]

## How to spot it

Signs include a purchase or achievement that felt huge now feeling like nothing much; a sense that you need the next upgrade to feel as good as you did; and comparing the present with your recent best rather than with life before the change.[^s4] In a 481-student study, thinking about a recent positive change, the emotions it brought, and the appreciation and variety people felt all declined within weeks.[^s4]

## How to fix it

Two moderators slowed the drift in Sheldon and Lyubomirsky's study: variety in how people engaged with a positive change, and continued appreciation of it. Both weakened the link from the change to rising aspirations and strengthened the link from positive emotions to well-being.[^s4] So vary how you use what you have, deliberately notice and value it, and catch rising expectations before they reset what counts as enough.[^s4] Epicurus's test of whether a desire has any natural limit offers a further check before chasing the next goal.[^s6] The study was short, self-reported and limited to U.S. students, so these are promising rather than proven remedies.[^s4]

Do not lean on adaptation for hard losses. Unemployment, disability and bereavement often leave lasting effects,[^s1] so expecting to "bounce back" can delay practical action. Persistent low mood after a loss or setback is a reason to seek professional support.

## Connections

Hedonic adaptation is **demonstrated by** the life-events **meta-analysis** of Luhmann and colleagues,[^s2] and the claim that windfalls leave well-being unchanged is **challenged by** the Swedish **lottery-wealth study**.[^s3] It is **explained by** the **hedonic adaptation prevention model**, and it **undermines** lasting gains in **well-being** after positive change.[^s4] The treadmill model was **introduced by Philip Brickman** with Donald Campbell.[^s1] It links to **gratitude** and **savoring** through the role of appreciation,[^s4] and to the **classification of desires**, **contentment** and **greed** through Epicurus's account of empty desires.[^s6] **Negative visualization** is a Stoic practice aimed at the same fading of joy.
