# Process model of emotion regulation

The process model of emotion regulation is the psychologist James Gross's framework for the ways people influence which emotions they have, when they have them, and how they feel and show them.[^s1] It treats an emotion as something that unfolds over time, from a situation, to what you pay attention to, to what you make of it, to your response, and it sorts regulation strategies by where in that sequence they act.[^s1][^s4] It matters for wisdom because it turns "manage your feelings" into a set of distinct choices, each with its own costs, and invites you to fit the choice to the moment rather than to habit.[^s4]

## Origin

Gross set out the model in a 1998 review that helped define emotion regulation as a research field.[^s1] He named five families of strategy: *situation selection* (approaching or avoiding people, places or things), *situation modification* (changing the situation, such as asking a neighbour to quiet a loud party), *attentional deployment* (distraction, concentration or rumination), *cognitive change* (reappraising what a situation means) and *response modulation* (acting on the response once it has begun, for example by relaxation, exercise or hiding your expression).[^s1] The first four act before the emotion is fully under way; the last acts on the response.[^s1] In the same year, a laboratory study tested that distinction: 120 students watched a disgusting film of an amputation while either reappraising it in detached, technical terms, hiding their feelings, or just watching.[^s2] Both strategies reduced visible disgust, but only reappraisal reduced felt disgust, while suppression raised signs of sympathetic arousal.[^s2]

Later work added a second layer, sometimes called the *extended* process model. Regulation itself becomes a sequence of decisions: *identification* (should I change this feeling?), *selection* (which kind of strategy?), *implementation* (which concrete action?) and *monitoring* (keep going, switch or stop?).[^s4]

## Why it matters

Gross made no prior assumption that regulation is good or bad: the same strategy can serve good or bad ends, and short-term relief can carry long-term costs, as when a shy person who avoids social events grows isolated.[^s1] Gross and colleagues argue that each family can work but has different costs and benefits, so regulating well means matching the strategy to the feeling, the person and the context.[^s4] When a frustrating situation can be improved, changing it is often more sensible than reappraising it; when little can be changed, cognitive strategies may fit better.[^s4] They also locate flexibility in the monitoring stage: too little flexibility keeps people using a strategy that has already failed, as in continued worry or rumination, while too much leads to switching or stopping before a strategy has had time to work.[^s4] This is why the model underpins the idea of emotion regulatory flexibility.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Use the model as a checklist, roughly in order.[^s1][^s4] First decide whether, and in which direction, you want the feeling to change. Then look upstream: can you choose or change the situation? If not, where will you put your attention, and can you rethink what the situation means? Use response modulation, such as composing your face, as a short-term tool.[^s2][^s3] Afterwards, check the result and switch if needed; Gross and colleagues suggest that practice in switching strategies as circumstances change may help monitoring.[^s4] The same framework applies to building up pleasant or useful feelings, for instance by calling a friend when you feel low.[^s4] At work it can separate a sensible moment of composure in a meeting from a habit of never addressing what is wrong; in relationships it can separate fixing a recurring problem from endlessly reinterpreting it.

## Common misreadings

The model is a map, not a ranking. Reading it as "reappraisal good, suppression bad" ignores the framework's own emphasis on fit and cost, and ignores that changing the situation is sometimes the wiser move.[^s1][^s4] Nor does it say that regulating more is always better: deciding not to change a feeling is a legitimate outcome of the identification stage.[^s4] And it is not a self-treatment plan. Persistent distress, worry or low mood is a reason to seek support from a doctor or mental-health professional.

## Evidence and limits

A meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons found that, on average, attentional deployment had no effect (d = 0.00), response modulation a small one (0.16) and cognitive change a small-to-medium one (0.36).[^s3] Within these families results diverged: distraction helped while concentrating on the emotion made things worse; reappraising the situation (0.36) and taking a different perspective (0.45) outperformed reappraising one's own response (0.23); and suppressing expression reduced visible emotion while trying to suppress the feeling itself did not work.[^s3] Effects varied with the emotion, the study design and other moderators.[^s3] The extended model's decision stages are less tested. A 2025 systematic review of 21 studies in post-traumatic stress found that no included study examined monitoring, and that most treatments were not designed with the model in mind.[^s5] Much of the evidence comes from short laboratory tasks with students.[^s2][^s3] The evidence status here is therefore *mixed*.

## Connections

The model is a framework within **emotion regulation**, **introduced by James Gross** in *The Emerging Field of Emotion Regulation* (1998).[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** his study of reappraisal and suppression[^s2] and **supported by** the *Dealing with Feeling* meta-analysis.[^s3] It **develops emotion regulatory flexibility** by naming the repertoire and the monitoring that flexibility depends on.[^s4] With low confidence, it **addresses rumination**, which its authors treat as a monitoring failure in which people persist with a strategy that is not working.[^s4] Related entries include **cognitive reappraisal** and its specific form, **compassionate reappraisal**; **emotional suppression** and **distraction**, two of its strategies; **self-distancing**, close to the perspective-taking strategy in the meta-analysis; and the **dichotomy of control**, whose question about what can be changed parallels the choice between changing a situation and reappraising it.[^s3][^s4]
