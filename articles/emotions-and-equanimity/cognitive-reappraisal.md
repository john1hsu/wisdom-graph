steps:
  - Name the feeling and the specific thought driving it, for example "I'm anxious because I think this mistake will get me fired."
  - Ask whether you can still change the situation itself; if you can, plan that action first and use reappraisal only to steady yourself enough to act.
  - Test the interpretation against the facts, as you would a colleague's claim, and look for what you actually know versus what you are assuming.
  - Try one or two alternative readings that are also true, such as what the event will matter in a year, what you can still influence, or how a neutral observer would describe it.
  - Pick the reading you can honestly believe, not the most comforting one, and let it guide your next step.
  - After a while, check whether the feeling has shifted; if the reframe isn't landing, switch to another strategy such as acceptance, talking it through or taking a break.
example:
  situation: A project lead learns in a meeting that the client has rejected the proposal their team spent three weeks on.
  before: "This is a disaster and it proves I'm not good at this. I'll just smile, say it's fine, and stew about it tonight."
  after: "This stings, and it's a real setback. What do I actually know? They rejected this version, not the team. Some of it I can still change: I'll ask for their reasons tomorrow. The part I can't change I'll treat as one lost bid in a long year, not a verdict on us."
  why: The wiser response keeps the feeling honest, uses reappraisal on the part that can't be changed, and keeps the frustration that should drive action on the part that can, instead of masking everything and ruminating later.

# Cognitive reappraisal

Cognitive reappraisal means changing how you construe an emotional situation so that its emotional impact changes: the event stays the same, but what you take it to mean does.[^s1] In emotion research it is the best-known form of "cognitive change" in James Gross's process model, and it is usually contrasted with expressive suppression, which leaves the feeling in place and only hides its outward signs.[^s1][^s4] Its place in a graph about wisdom comes from the judgment it calls for: reappraisal helps most when it is honest and when it is used on the right kind of problem.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The idea is old. Epictetus opens chapter 5 of his *Handbook* with the line "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things," and goes on to advise looking to our own judgments rather than blaming others when we are upset.[^s6] That is a philosophical teaching about judgment rather than a psychological technique, and the modern construct was not simply derived from it. In contemporary psychology, Gross and John's 2003 studies made reappraisal measurable as an everyday habit, using questionnaire items such as controlling emotions "by changing the way I think about the situation."[^s1] Later work split reappraisal into tactics. The two most studied are reinterpretation, which changes what a situation means, and distancing, which changes the vantage point from which you view it, for example by stepping back in space or time or looking as an outside observer would.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Across five studies with well over a thousand participants, people who habitually reappraised reported, and were seen by peers as showing, more positive and less negative emotion; they had closer relationships and reported fewer depressive symptoms and higher life satisfaction than habitual suppressors.[^s1] For wise living, reappraisal is a way to keep a strong first reaction from becoming the whole story, so that you can see a setback, a slight or a loss in proportion and still respond to it.[^s1][^s4] It also carries into relationships: in a two-year study of 120 married couples, spouses who learned to write about their conflicts from a neutral third party's viewpoint kept their love, trust and commitment, while control couples showed the usual decline.[^s7]

## How to apply it

Start by naming the feeling and the thought behind it, then ask whether the situation itself can still be changed. That question matters because reappraisal is mainly a tool for the part of a situation you can't change.[^s2] When you reframe, use a tactic deliberately. People often reach for a "reality challenge" (questioning whether things are as bad as they look), but in one set of studies the tactics people picked most often were not always the ones that lowered negative feeling the most, and trying to reappraise and failing left people feeling worst of all.[^s5] Distancing tactics can be used in almost any situation because they don't depend on finding a new meaning in its details.[^s4] Finally, check whether the reframe has worked and switch strategies if it hasn't.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Reframing what you should fix.** Among 170 adults who had recently faced a stressful event, better reappraisal ability went with less depression when the stressor was uncontrollable but with more depression when it was controllable, possibly because feeling better removed the push to change things.[^s2]
- **Talking yourself out of a useful signal.** Reappraisal can dull the fear or outrage that should guide a decision; studies reviewed by Ford and Troy link it to accepting unfair offers and riskier choices, and to feeling inauthentic when the emotion is anger at an injustice.[^s3]
- **Comfortable rather than true.** A reframe you don't believe tends not to work; in some laboratory studies, between a third and a half of participants felt worse after trying.[^s3]
- **Forcing it in overwhelming situations.** Reappraisal works less well when a stressor is very intense or offers little to reinterpret, and frequent use there has been linked to worse outcomes.[^s3]

## Evidence

Laboratory instructions to reappraise reliably reduce negative emotion, with moderate pooled effects for specific tactics such as reinterpretation and distancing in a large meta-analysis summarised by Powers and LaBar.[^s4] The everyday benefits reported by Gross and John are correlational, and much of the evidence on reappraisal's costs is also cross-sectional, so its causal effects in daily life are less settled than its lab effects.[^s1][^s3] Hence the status here is *mixed*: reappraisal is a real and useful skill whose value depends on the person's skill, the intensity of the situation and whether it can be changed.[^s2][^s3] It is not a treatment for persistent depression, anxiety or trauma; distress that lingers or overwhelms is a reason to seek professional or crisis support.

## Connections

Cognitive reappraisal falls under **emotion regulation** and is **explained by the process model of emotion regulation**, which places it at the cognitive-change stage, with distancing and reinterpretation as its main tactics; that is why **self-distancing** and the **view from above** sit close to it.[^s4] It **contrasts with emotional suppression**, the response-focused strategy Gross and John compared it with, and that comparison was **demonstrated by** their individual-differences studies.[^s1] It is **challenged by the reappraisal-controllability study**, which showed that it can backfire on controllable problems, a finding that links it to the **dichotomy of control** and to **regulatory flexibility**, the skill of fitting strategy to situation.[^s2][^s3] It is **applied in adversity**, where the stressor often can't be changed, and in **interpersonal conflict**, where a third-party reappraisal helped couples.[^s2][^s7] **Compassionate reappraisal** is a specific variant aimed at people who have wronged you, and **acceptance**, which some researchers count as one of the reappraisal tactics, is close kin.[^s5] Epictetus's teaching that judgments, not events, disturb us links it to the Stoic pursuit of **equanimity**.[^s6]
