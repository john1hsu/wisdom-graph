# Classification of desires

The classification of desires is Epicurus's tool for deciding which wants deserve to be satisfied. In *Principal Doctrine* 29 he sorts them into three kinds: natural and necessary; natural but not necessary; and neither natural nor necessary, arising from groundless opinion.[^s2] The point is practical: someone who can tell these apart knows which wants to meet, which to enjoy without depending on them, and which to let go, because chasing them can never end.[^s3]

## Origin

Epicurus set the scheme out in two short texts preserved in Book 10 of Diogenes Laertius's *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*. The *Letter to Menoeceus* divides desires into natural and vain, splits the natural ones into the necessary and the merely natural, and says the necessary ones serve happiness, the ease of the body, or life itself; their right handling aims at health of body and peace of mind.[^s1] The *Principal Doctrines* add the reasons. Nature's wealth is limited and easy to get, while the wealth that empty fancies promise recedes without end (PD 15).[^s2] Desires that bring no pain when unmet are unnecessary and easily dropped (PD 26), and when people cling to them anyway, the fault lies in their mistaken opinion, not in the desire's nature (PD 30).[^s2]

In David Konstan's account, the natural and necessary desires are those tied to happiness, physical well-being or survival; the natural but unnecessary ones are for pleasant extras such as good food and drink; and the empty ones are for things like great wealth, fame or immortality, which have no real limit and so can never be satisfied.[^s3] These empty desires rest on *kenodoxia*, empty belief, which Epicurus treated as the main source of disturbance in civilised life.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The classification turns a vague hope to want less into a judgment that can be made case by case. Epicurus held that chasing limitless wealth and power exposes people to the very dangers they hoped to escape, and that piling up desires tends to increase mental agitation rather than calm it.[^s3] Seeing how little is needed to remove the pain of want frees a person from needing things that only struggle can win (PD 21).[^s2] He also argued that people with simple tastes enjoy luxuries most when they come, because they do not need them.[^s1] The goal is not hardship but *ataraxia*, freedom from mental disturbance, together with freedom from bodily pain, and the self-sufficiency that makes it easier to reach.[^s3]

## How to apply it

The sorting is an exercise of practical wisdom. Epicurus called prudence the beginning and chief good of the whole project, and reasoning is what shows that some pleasures should be passed up because they bring greater pains later.[^s1][^s3]

1. **List the wants pressing on you now**, from rent to a new phone to recognition at work.
2. **Ask of each one whether going without it would actually hurt.** Pain from going without marks a necessary desire; if none follows, the desire is optional however strongly it is felt (PD 26, 30).[^s2]
3. **Ask whether it has a natural limit.** Food and shelter can be satisfied; more status, more money "just in case" or a reputation can always grow, which is the mark of an empty desire.[^s3]
4. **Meet the necessary desires first and reliably**, enjoy the optional ones when they come cheaply, and refuse to buy the empty ones with your peace or time.[^s1][^s3]
5. **Revisit the list when circumstances change**, since what counts as necessary depends partly on a particular life and setting.[^s4]

## Common misreadings

The popular picture of an "epicure" as a lover of luxury reverses the teaching: Epicurus held that bread and water give the greatest pleasure to someone who needs them, and he praised self-sufficiency over abundance.[^s1] Nor is the scheme a call to asceticism, since natural but unnecessary pleasures are welcome as long as one does not come to depend on them.[^s3] And the boundaries are not fixed rules. Scholars agree on the classification's practical point but disagree about which desires belong in which class; Robitzsch argues the Epicureans allowed some variation in human nature and that the scheme is best read by its function in a particular life.[^s4]

## Evidence

The classification is a philosophical teaching, judged by argument and long practice rather than by experiment.[^s3] Modern research tests a nearby idea, the cost of making money and status-conferring possessions central to one's goals. A meta-analysis of 259 samples and 753 effect sizes found materialist values reliably but modestly associated with lower well-being (multifaceted measures r = −.19), with the strongest links to negative self-appraisals and risky behaviour and the weakest to life satisfaction.[^s5] The data are mostly correlational, and the authors call for more experimental and longitudinal work.[^s5] A student study by Larsen and McKibban found that wanting what you already have and having what you want were far from the same thing, and that each went with greater happiness; this fits the idea that wanting matters as much as having, but it too is correlational.[^s6] None of this shows that Epicurus's three categories are the right ones; it suggests that unbounded acquisitive wanting tends to go with lower well-being. The teaching is not a treatment for depression or anxiety; persistent distress is a reason to seek professional support.

## Connections

The classification is rooted in Epicureanism and was introduced by Epicurus in the *Letter to Menoeceus* and the *Principal Doctrines*.[^s1][^s2][^s3] It develops contentment by showing how little is needed for self-sufficiency, and it addresses greed by exposing desires whose objects recede without end.[^s2][^s3] It is supported, at a distance, by the meta-analysis linking materialism to lower well-being and by the study of wanting what you have.[^s5][^s6] It sits close to satisficing, the choice to stop at good enough; to hedonic adaptation, which explains why new possessions stop pleasing; to the Stoic dichotomy of control, another sorting exercise that directs care where it pays; and to practical wisdom, which Epicurus made the judge of every choice.[^s1][^s3]
