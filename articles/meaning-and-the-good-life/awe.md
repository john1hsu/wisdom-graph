# Awe

Awe is the feeling of standing before something too large to take in at once: a mountain range, a storm, a piece of music, an act of extraordinary courage, or an idea that reorganises how you see the world. In the definition that launched modern research, Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt (2003) gave it two core features: *perceived vastness*, which can be physical, social or conceptual, and a *need for accommodation*, the sense that your existing mental framework has to stretch to make sense of what you are meeting.[^s1] Later work treats awe as one of the self-transcendent experiences, in which attention shifts away from the self toward something larger.[^s1]

## Origin

Awe is ancient in human reflection long before it was a research topic. Awe is central to many religious traditions, and scholars of religion describe the "numinous" as a sense of contact with the sacred.[^s1] Greek philosophy tied a close cousin, wonder (*thauma*), to the love of wisdom itself. In Plato's *Theaetetus*, Socrates tells a puzzled young mathematician that his wonder shows he is a philosopher, since wonder is where philosophy begins (155d).[^s5] Aristotle opens the *Metaphysics* the same way: people first began to philosophise, and still do, because they wondered, and the person who is puzzled and wonders recognises their own ignorance (I.2, 982b).[^s6] For Aristotle the wisdom that wonder sets in motion is knowledge of first principles and causes.[^s6]

Keltner and Haidt's paper turned this old theme into a psychological construct. They also proposed that awe comes in different "flavours", coloured by threat, beauty, ability, virtue or a sense of supernatural causes.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Awe matters for wisdom because it works on two things wise reasoning needs: a smaller ego and an open mind. People who have just felt awe describe themselves as smaller and less important, and draw smaller self-portraits; Berkeley researchers call this the "small self".[^s1] Dispositional awe predicts humility as rated by friends, and induced awe makes people more ready to credit others for their successes.[^s1] In early studies, people who felt awe were also less persuaded by weak arguments.[^s1]

A three-study project with 605 adults linked awe directly to measures of wisdom. People prone to awe showed more wise reasoning about interpersonal conflicts and more epistemic humility, even allowing for general happiness; and watching vast nature scenes raised both compared with neutral and amusing videos.[^s3] The authors suggest that self-transcendent experiences prompt people to consider perspectives beyond their own.[^s3]

Awe is also ordinary. Nature is its most common trigger, but people also report it in response to art, music, spiritual experience and the skill or goodness of others, including people they know.[^s1] Its forms differ by culture: in one diary study, Americans more often cited their own accomplishments and Chinese participants more often cited other people.[^s1]

## How to cultivate it

The best-tested routine is the awe walk. In a randomised study, 60 healthy older adults took a 15-minute outdoor walk each week for eight weeks; half were simply asked to notice what was vast or surprising around them.[^s4] Compared with ordinary walkers, the awe group felt more awe and joy on their walks, their selfies increasingly showed the scenery rather than themselves, and in daily life they reported more prosocial positive emotions such as compassion and gratitude, and less distress.[^s4] Anxiety, depression and life satisfaction did not change in either group.[^s4]

Berkeley's Greater Good Science Center turns this into a simple practice: leave your phone behind, go somewhere with vastness or novelty, slow your breathing, and move your attention between large features and small details, asking what is new or unexplained.[^s7] It notes that the practice has not been tested widely across groups and cultures, and that pressure to *find* awe can feel discouraging when life is hard, so "noticing" may be a better aim.[^s7]

## Common misreadings

**Awe is always pleasant.** Threatening awe, from storms or disasters, raised heart rate and stress responses and did not bring the well-being benefits of positive awe.[^s1]

**Awe is a treatment.** Awe is a reflective experience, not therapy; in the awe-walk trial it did not shift anxiety or depression scores.[^s4] Persistent distress is a reason to seek professional support.

**A moment of awe makes people better.** Direct replications found that brief awe inductions generally did not change prosocial choices, so awe is best read as an opening that reflection can use, not a guarantee of kinder or wiser judgment.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence

The science of awe is young. A 2018 review called many findings preliminary, resting on one or a few studies, mostly Western samples, self-report and correlational designs.[^s1] A 2026 series of six replication studies with about 2,000 analysed participants put several well-known findings to the test, including claims that awe increases prosocial choices and life satisfaction.[^s2] Video, writing and virtual-reality inductions usually did raise awe, but the downstream effects were generally not reproduced, including in preregistered direct replications with larger samples than the originals.[^s2] The authors suggest that brief lab or online inductions may produce awe too weak to change behaviour.[^s2] Evidence that people *feel* awe and a smaller self is solid; claims that a short dose reliably makes people kinder, more satisfied or less pressed for time outrun the evidence, so the status here is contested.[^s2][^s1]

## Connections

Awe was **introduced** as a research construct by **Dacher Keltner** and **Jonathan Haidt**.[^s1] It is a form of **self-transcendence** and a near relative of the **view from above** and the **overview effect**, the awe astronauts report on seeing Earth from space.[^s1] It may **cause humility** through the small self,[^s1] and it is **supported by** the **awe-walks study**[^s4] and by work linking self-transcendent experiences to **wise reasoning**, which awe may modestly promote.[^s3] Its popular claims are **challenged by** the **2026 awe-induction replication series**.[^s2] Through wonder it connects to **theoretical wisdom** and **Socratic ignorance**, since for Plato and Aristotle the recognition of not knowing is where the love of wisdom starts.[^s5][^s6]
