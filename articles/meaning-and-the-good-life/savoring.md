steps:
  - Pick one ordinary pleasure in the coming day, such as a meal, a walk, a conversation or a finished task, and decide in advance to give it your full attention.
  - While it is happening, slow down and sharpen your senses; notice one or two specific things that make it good and name to yourself why they appeal to you.
  - Mark the moment so it lasts, for example by telling someone about it, smiling or saying it aloud, or taking a mental snapshot to recall later.
  - Remind yourself that the moment is passing and will not come again in quite this form, and let that make it more precious rather than sad.
  - Later that day or week, spend a few minutes reminiscing about it, or look forward to something good that is coming.
  - Notice when you are dampening a good moment, by finding fault, worrying about what comes next or telling yourself you don't deserve it, and gently return your attention to what is good.
example:
  situation: After months of work, your team ships a project on time and the client sends a warm thank-you note, but you have three new deadlines waiting.
  before: "Nice, but that's done. Let's not get complacent; the next quarter looks rough. Archive the email and open the next task."
  after: "Before I move on, I'll read this note again, forward it to the team with what each person made possible, and take ten minutes at lunch to enjoy that we pulled it off. The deadlines will still be there at two."
  why: Sharing the success and pausing on it keeps a real achievement from vanishing unnoticed, while the kill-joy reflex of jumping to the next worry spends the good moment before it is felt.

# Savoring

Savoring is the deliberate regulation of positive feelings: noticing something good and using attention, thought and behaviour to deepen it or make it last.[^s1] Psychologists distinguish it from simply having pleasant experiences. Two people can eat the same meal or receive the same compliment; the one who attends to it, appreciates it and lets it register gets more from it.[^s1] Savoring can face three ways in time: anticipating a good event, enjoying it in the moment, and reminiscing about it later.[^s1]

## Origin

The modern concept was developed by the psychologist Fred Bryant, working with Joseph Veroff, and set out in their book *Savoring: A New Model of Positive Experience* (2007).[^s1] Bryant had earlier built the Savoring Beliefs Inventory (2003), which measures how capable people feel of enjoying good experiences, as distinct from their ability to obtain them.[^s1] Bryant and colleagues describe four basic savoring processes, crossing whether attention is on the world or the self with whether it is reflective or absorbed: thanksgiving (gratitude), marveling (awe), basking (pride) and luxuriating (physical pleasure).[^s1] They also catalogued ten strategies people use, nine that amplify good feelings and one, kill-joy thinking, that stifles them.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Good things tend to slip by. A savoring practice is designed so that positive moments leave a lasting impression instead of fading quickly, and so that familiar pleasures do not become invisible.[^s4] That matters for living well: a life can contain many good things that are hardly experienced because attention is elsewhere.

Wisdom traditions have long linked appreciation to an awareness that things pass, and psychology offers a modern echo. In Jaime Kurtz's studies at the University of Virginia, students led to feel that graduation was close, rather than far away, reported more appreciation of college life and higher well-being, and did more of what they would miss, such as seeing friends or taking scenic routes to class; results were not fully consistent across her three studies.[^s5]

Savoring also bears on how people relate to wealth and wanting more. In a Belgian survey of working adults, greater wealth went with a lower self-reported ability to savor, and savoring was more strongly linked to happiness than wealth was; a small follow-up found that people shown a picture of money spent less time enjoying a piece of chocolate.[^s6] The authors suggest abundance may blunt everyday enjoyment.[^s6] The survey was correlational, and money-priming experiments in general show signs of publication bias, so the priming part deserves caution.[^s7]

## How to apply it

Bryant's strategy list doubles as a menu: share good moments with others, build memories (a mental snapshot or a keepsake), congratulate yourself, sharpen your senses, compare the moment favourably with worse times, let yourself be absorbed, express the feeling outwardly, stay aware of how fleeting it is, and count your blessings.[^s1] The Greater Good Science Center's savoring walk is a simple structured version: a daily twenty-minute solo walk for a week, noticing pleasant sights, sounds and smells, pausing on each and naming what makes it enjoyable, and varying the route so nothing becomes taken for granted.[^s4] At work, savoring can mean pausing to mark a team success before rushing on; in relationships, sharing and reminiscing about good times together.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Kill-joy thinking.** Finding fault, worrying about what comes next or telling yourself you shouldn't feel so good dampens positive feelings; Bryant's research treats it as savoring's opposite.[^s1]
- **Forcing it.** Trying too hard to extract enjoyment can turn a moment into a task; the walk exercise asks only that you notice and acknowledge.[^s4]
- **Ignoring culture.** Dampening positive feelings is more common among East Asian than North American adults, and the authors argue it regulates positive emotion in culturally normative ways, so "more amplification" is not a universal ideal.[^s1]
- **Treating it as treatment.** Savoring helps people appreciate what is good; it is not a substitute for facing what is hard, and trials in people with mental or physical health conditions are few and of low quality.[^s3] Persistent low mood or distress is a reason to seek professional or crisis support rather than rely on a self-help exercise.

## Evidence and limits

A 2026 meta-analysis pooled 20 randomized controlled trials with 4,805 participants and found a moderate overall effect of savoring interventions (g = 0.51), with benefits for positive psychological states and for negative emotional symptoms; effects were numerically larger against passive controls, though that difference was not significant, and tests found little sign of publication bias.[^s2] Confidence intervals were wide.[^s2] A systematic review of nine trials in adults with mental or physical health conditions found some gains in well-being and reductions in depression and anxiety, but judged the studies to be of low quality and the effects relatively weak compared with control groups.[^s3] Evidence is therefore promising but mixed: the practice seems helpful for many people, while the size of the benefit, especially in clinical groups, remains uncertain.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

Savoring was **introduced by Fred Bryant** and set out **in *Savoring: A New Model of Positive Experience***, and it is a form of **emotion regulation** directed at positive feelings.[^s1] It **develops gratitude**, since thanksgiving is one of its core processes and counting blessings one of its strategies,[^s1] and it **addresses hedonic adaptation** by keeping good experiences from fading.[^s4] It **contrasts with kill-joy thinking**, the habit of dampening good feelings.[^s1] It is **supported by the savoring-interventions meta-analysis**,[^s2] **challenged by the review of savouring in clinical populations**, which found low-quality evidence,[^s3] and **demonstrated by** the **perceived-temporal-scarcity study**[^s5] and the **wealth-and-savoring study**.[^s6] It is **studied in positive psychology**.[^s3] It sits beside **contentment** and **greed**, given the link between abundance and blunted enjoyment;[^s6] beside **impermanence** and ***mono no aware***, which also join appreciation to transience;[^s5] and beside **negative visualization**, the **positivity effect in ageing** and **reflective journaling**.
