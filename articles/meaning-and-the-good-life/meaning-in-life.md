# Meaning in life

Meaning in life is the sense that your life makes sense, is heading somewhere and matters. The psychologist Michael Steger and colleagues defined it broadly as the sense made of, and the significance felt about, one's own existence, deliberately leaving people to apply their own criteria.[^s1] Frank Martela and Steger later argued that the term bundles three distinct facets: **coherence** (life is comprehensible), **purpose** (central goals give it direction) and **significance** (life has value and is worth living).[^s2] Philosophers ask a related but different question: not whether a life *feels* meaningful, but what, if anything, actually makes an individual life meaningful.[^s5]

## Origin

Philosophers distinguish the meaning *of* life, whether humanity or the universe as a whole has a point, from meaning *in* life, whether a particular person's life is meaningful; most recent analytic work concerns the second.[^s5] Thaddeus Metz's survey sorts the answers into supernaturalist views, on which meaning depends on God or a soul, and naturalist ones, on which a meaningful life is possible in a purely physical world. Naturalists in turn split between subjectivists, for whom meaning comes from what you care about, and objectivists, for whom some pursuits are worthwhile whatever you feel. Susan Wolf's hybrid view needs both: in her phrase, "subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness".[^s5] Nihilists deny that any life can have meaning at all.[^s5]

Psychology took up the question through measurement. Steger's Meaning in Life Questionnaire (2006) separated the **presence** of meaning, how meaningful you find your life, from the **search** for it, how actively you are looking; the two turned out to be largely independent.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Meaning is not the same as happiness, and that difference is where wisdom comes in. Metz notes that a pleasant life in an experience machine might be happy without being meaningful, and that meaning is not simply moral goodness either, since discovery or creative work can add it.[^s5] Philosophers often summarise what confers meaning as the good, the true and the beautiful: caring relationships and helping others, understanding, and creativity.[^s5] A wise person therefore asks not only "does this feel good?" but "does this connect me to something worth caring about?"

Meaning also comes in degrees and belongs to stretches of life, not only to whole lives, so a hard season can be rich in it and an easy one thin.[^s5] At work, the three facets offer a practical diagnosis: a job can feel meaningless because nobody explains how the pieces fit (coherence), because it points nowhere (purpose), or because its value to anyone is invisible (significance), and each calls for a different remedy.[^s2]

## How meaning arises

Research suggests that meaning is more ordinary than the image of a lifelong quest implies. Pooling large surveys, Samantha Heintzelman and Laura King found that most people rate their lives as meaningful, including in a Gallup poll of over 130 countries and in groups facing illness or bereavement.[^s3] In experiments, social inclusion, mild positive mood and seeing reliable patterns in the world all raised people's ratings of meaning, while even brief exclusion lowered them.[^s3] They argue that meaning tracks things relevant to survival, so its absence is a rare and serious signal.[^s3]

Searching is not the same as finding. In Steger's samples, presence of meaning went with life satisfaction and joy and against depression, while search was unrelated to satisfaction and modestly linked to sadness and anxiety.[^s1] Searching need not be harmful, the authors suggest, since people may seek new or deeper sources of meaning as their lives change.[^s1]

## Common misreadings

- **Meaning must be discovered once, as a calling.** For most people it is already present and drawn from ordinary relationships, routines and contributions.[^s3]
- **Meaning and purpose are the same.** Purpose is one facet; a life can feel coherent and worthwhile while its direction is unsettled.[^s2]
- **Anything you care about intensely is meaningful.** On objectivist and hybrid views, caring about something trivial does not make a life meaningful.[^s5]
- **Meaning means feeling good.** Meaning and happiness are distinct, and a meaningful life can include struggle.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

A meta-analysis of 66 studies (about 73,500 participants) found that meaning in life was associated with better physical health, with a small-to-moderate average correlation of about 0.26; longitudinal and cross-sectional studies did not differ significantly.[^s4] The authors caution that most studies were correlational and in healthy samples, that some meaning scales contain well-being items, and that self-reported health may inflate the link.[^s4] A meta-analysis of 33 randomized trials found that interventions such as mindfulness and narrative programs raised meaning, with large effects against passive controls but much smaller ones against active controls, and considerable heterogeneity.[^s6] Much of the evidence rests on self-report; Heintzelman and King themselves address the objection that social desirability or positive illusions may inflate how meaningful people say their lives are.[^s3] The status here is therefore **mixed**.

Low presence of meaning goes with depression in this research.[^s1] Persistent emptiness, hopelessness or loss of interest is therefore not a philosophical puzzle to solve alone: it is a reason to speak with a doctor or mental health professional, or a crisis line if you are in danger.

## Connections

Meaning in life is often **confused with** the meaning *of* life, the cosmic question of whether humanity as a whole has a point.[^s5] It **contrasts with** happiness, which can be present without meaning and absent where meaning is.[^s5] Its structure is **explained by** the three-facet model of coherence, purpose and significance,[^s2] of which purpose in life is one part. Its link with health is **supported by** a meta-analysis of 66 studies,[^s4] and the claim that meaning can be cultivated is **supported by** a meta-analysis of randomized trials of meaning interventions.[^s6] It is **studied in** positive psychology, where Martela and Steger's model appeared.[^s2] It relates to logotherapy, eudaimonia and the existential vacuum, and to the debate over whether meaning must be chosen, given or found.[^s5]
