# Gratitude

Gratitude, in the sense philosophers mostly study, is the fitting response of someone who has benefited to the person who benefited them: you are grateful *to* someone *for* something.[^s1] It joins a belief (that this was a good, given with goodwill), a feeling of warmth toward the giver, an act of thanks, and a readiness to return goodwill or at least never to harm the benefactor.[^s1] A looser sense, being grateful *that* something is so, such as good weather or good health, overlaps with appreciation or gladness.[^s1] The psychologist Robert Emmons captures both senses in two steps: affirming that there is good in your life, and recognising that its sources lie at least partly outside yourself.[^s2] As a virtue, gratitude is the settled disposition to respond this way well, even on days when the warm feeling is absent.[^s1]

## Origin

The Stoic Seneca wrote the fullest ancient treatment, *On Benefits* (*De Beneficiis*), seven books on giving, receiving and returning favours. He observes that no vice is more common than ingratitude (Book I.1) and argues that a benefit lies not in the object handed over but in the mind of the giver (Book I.6).[^s6] On his account, receiving a kindness gratefully is itself the first part of repaying it (Book II).[^s6] Later moral philosophers treated failures of gratitude with the same seriousness: Hume counted ingratitude among the most horrid crimes, and Kant called gratitude a sacred duty that can never be fully discharged.[^s1] Modern psychology took up the topic in earnest around 2000, when Emmons, Michael McCullough and colleagues began experimental studies of gratitude in daily life.[^s3][^s2]

Gratitude is honoured in many religious and ethical traditions, often directed toward God, nature or ancestors as well as people; this article describes the virtue without endorsing any one account of where ultimate thanks belong.[^s2][^s1]

## Why it matters

Gratitude belongs to wisdom about the good life because it corrects a common error: treating what we have as simply owed to us. Emmons calls it a relationship-strengthening emotion, since it makes us notice how others have supported us.[^s2] People who are grateful by disposition report fewer mental-health problems and better relationships, though such correlations do not show which way the influence runs.[^s5] Gratitude also matters morally: Seneca ranked ingratitude among the commonest vices, and the sociologist Georg Simmel called gratitude the moral memory of mankind.[^s6][^s2]

## What good looks like

The grateful person gets three things right. **What** is a benefit: forced or manipulative "gifts" generate no debt of gratitude, and gratitude may lapse if a benefactor later demands repayment or uses the gift as leverage.[^s1] **Who** to thank and how much: the virtue sits between failings. Too little is **ingratitude**, which can be forgetful, cold-hearted, too proud to say thanks, or too lazy to return a kindness, and at its worst becomes resentment of the giver.[^s1] Too much is **overgratitude**, deference toward someone who has not earned it, which can shade into servility and a loss of self-respect.[^s1] **How** to respond: philosophers broadly agree that thanking and acting kindly toward a benefactor are the parts most within our control, while feelings cannot be ordered up on demand.[^s1]

Gratitude also differs from **indebtedness**. Feeling that you owe someone and must even the score can breed guilt or resentment rather than warmth.[^s2] Philosophers debate whether gratitude creates strict obligations at all, or is better understood as goodwill freely returned.[^s1]

## How to practise it

- **Notice and name the giver.** Each day or week, write down three to five good things and who made each possible; a weekly journal, or the "three good things" exercise, are common formats.[^s2][^s3]
- **Attend to intention.** Ask what effort or care a kindness cost the person, in line with Seneca's point that the benefit lives in the giver's mind.[^s2][^s6]
- **Say it out loud.** Write a letter to someone you never properly thanked and, if it suits the relationship, deliver it in person.[^s2]
- **Subtract the good.** Imagine how your life would be had a good relationship or event never happened, then return to the fact that it did.[^s2]
- **Return goodwill, not a receipt.** Look for natural chances to help a benefactor or pass the kindness on, without treating it as settling an account.[^s1][^s6]

## Evidence and limits

Emmons and McCullough's 2003 experiments randomly assigned people to list blessings, hassles or neutral events. The gratitude groups reported more positive moods and rated their lives more favourably; one study found more weekly exercise, a sample of adults with neuromuscular disease reported better sleep, and some benefits did not replicate across all three studies.[^s3] The samples were small and relied mostly on self-report.[^s3]

Popular claims have run well ahead of this. A meta-analysis of 27 studies with 3,675 participants found only a small effect of gratitude exercises on symptoms of depression and anxiety, and only a small edge over neutral writing tasks.[^s4][^s5] Its authors recommend established treatments, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, for those conditions, while still calling gratitude good in itself and noting gains for relationships.[^s5] Gratitude can also backfire, for example when thanks is pressed on people in situations they rightly object to.[^s2][^s1] Gratitude exercises are reflective practices, not treatment: if low mood or anxiety persists, seek professional support.

## Connections

Gratitude **addresses ingratitude**, its vice of deficiency, and is flanked on the other side by overgratitude.[^s1] It **contrasts with indebtedness**, the uneasy sense of owing that can breed resentment.[^s2] It is **rooted in Stoicism** through Seneca's *On Benefits*.[^s6] It is **supported by the counting-blessings study**,[^s3] and its value as self-help for depression and anxiety is **challenged by the gratitude-interventions meta-analysis**.[^s4] It is a form of **appreciation**,[^s1] and sits beside **negative visualization** and **mental subtraction**, which cultivate it, and **forgiveness** and **savoring**.[^s2]
