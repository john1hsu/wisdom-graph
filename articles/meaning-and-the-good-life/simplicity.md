  - Write down the three to five things you most want more of in your life, such as time with family, health, a craft or community.
  - List where your money, hours and attention actually went last month, and mark each item as serving one of those priorities or not.
  - Choose one item that serves none of them and cut or shrink it for a trial month, rather than overhauling everything at once.
  - Redirect what you free up, whether hours or money, deliberately into one of your priorities, preferably something shared with other people.

# Simplicity

Simplicity, or voluntary simplicity, is the deliberate choice to consume and pursue less than one could, so that life is organised around what one values most rather than around acquiring. Researchers describe it as a lifestyle centred on reduced consumption, with personal growth and ecologically responsible behaviour as closely related aims.[^s1] The word *voluntary* matters: this is a chosen limit by someone who has options, not the hardship of someone who has none.[^s1] Its wisdom lies less in having few things than in judging which things are worth the time, attention and money they take.

## Origin

The ideal is ancient and appears in several traditions. Epicurus taught that nature's wealth is limited and easy to get, while the wealth pursued out of empty opinion recedes without end (*Principal Doctrines* 15).[^s5] His point was practical: a person who knows how little is needed is freer than one who keeps chasing more.[^s5]

The *Tao Te Ching* makes a related case in a different key. Chapter 19 urges plainness, simple honesty and few selfish desires; chapter 33 says that whoever is satisfied with their lot is rich; and chapter 46 names discontent and constant wanting as among the worst of faults (Legge's translation).[^s6] Here simplicity is part of returning to what is natural rather than a calculation of pleasure, and the two traditions should not be merged into one doctrine.[^s5][^s6]

In the modern West the best-known statement is Henry David Thoreau's *Walden* (1854). Explaining why he lived in the woods, he says he wanted to live deliberately, and he complains that ordinary life is wasted on petty details. His remedy is the famous refrain "Simplicity, simplicity, simplicity!" and the advice to keep one's affairs to a few rather than a thousand (ch. 2).[^s7]

## Why it matters

Simplicity is a response to a common error of judgment: assuming that more possessions, upgrades and commitments will add up to a better life. A meta-analysis of 259 samples found that people who place more importance on materialistic values and goals report somewhat lower well-being, with correlations around −.16 to −.19 and stronger links to negative self-appraisals and risky consumer and health behaviour.[^s3] The authors' mediation analyses suggest the link may be explained by poorer satisfaction of basic psychological needs.[^s3] Simplicity is one way of reversing that trade.

It also matters at work, where raises and promotions often bring spending and obligations that quietly cancel the freedom they were meant to buy, and in relationships, where the payoff of simplifying is time and attention for other people, the route through which simple living has been linked to well-being.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Start from priorities, not from possessions. Simplicity is a tool for alignment, so its first step is to name what you want more of, then look at where money, hours and attention actually go, and trim what serves none of those ends, in the spirit of Thoreau's advice to keep one's affairs few.[^s7] Make changes in small, reversible trials rather than grand purges, and pay attention to what replaces what you remove. A large New Zealand survey found that the well-being linked to voluntary simplicity ran mainly through psychological and achievement fulfilment, social connection and community involvement, not through having fewer things in itself.[^s2] The same study found that age, income and gender moderated the link, so the right degree of simplicity varies from person to person.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as an aesthetic.** Decluttering a room while still shopping, comparing and upgrading misses the point; Epicurus's target was desires with no natural limit, not furniture.[^s5]
- **Confusing it with deprivation.** The teaching distinguishes natural, easily met needs from empty desires; it does not ask anyone to go without what they need.[^s5] For people living with real scarcity, "simplify" is not useful advice.[^s1]
- **Simplifying alone and in secret.** The well-being gains appear to come through connection and community, so withdrawing from people defeats the purpose.[^s2]
- **Making it a new competition.** Counting possessions or performing frugality for an audience keeps attention on possessions and status, the orientation that research links to lower well-being.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is encouraging but limited, so its status here is **mixed**. A systematic review found only four peer-reviewed studies, with 3,233 participants in total, and concluded that the link between voluntary simplicity and well-being does exist while important questions remain.[^s1] These studies, like the later New Zealand survey, show association, not that simplifying causes greater well-being: people who choose simplicity may already differ in values, income or health.[^s1][^s2]

The stronger causal evidence concerns materialism rather than simplicity itself. In three longitudinal samples of American and Icelandic adults, people whose materialistic aims declined over time tended to become happier, and those whose aims rose tended to decline; in an experiment, highly materialistic adolescents given an intervention that reduced materialism later showed gains in self-esteem compared with controls.[^s4] As a traditional teaching, the claim that knowing what is enough makes for a freer life is judged by argument and long practice, and is normative.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Simplicity is **rooted in Epicureanism**, through the doctrine that natural wealth is limited,[^s5] and in **Daoism**, through the *Tao Te Ching*'s plainness and few desires.[^s6] Its best-known modern statement was **introduced by Henry David Thoreau** in *Walden*.[^s7] It aims to **develop contentment**, being satisfied with one's lot.[^s6] It **addresses greed**, whose objects recede without end,[^s5] and **materialism**, whose reduction is linked to rising well-being.[^s4] It is **supported by the voluntary simplicity review**,[^s1] by the **materialism and well-being meta-analysis**[^s3] and by the **changes-in-materialism studies**.[^s4] It applies the Epicurean **classification of desires**, works against **hedonic adaptation**'s pull toward the next upgrade, resembles **satisficing** in choosing good enough, and sits close to **temperance** and **gratitude**.[^s3][^s5]
