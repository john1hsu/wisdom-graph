# Ikigai

Ikigai (生き甲斐) is an ordinary Japanese word for what makes life worth living, and for the felt sense of having something that does.[^s2][^s4] It has been rendered in English as "purpose in life" (following psychiatrist Mieko Kamiya) and as "life worth living" (following Gordon Mathews).[^s4] In a survey of 846 Japanese adults, people described ikigai as devoting themselves to pursuits they enjoy, with a sense of accomplishment, fulfilment and direction; that profile places it close to what psychologists call eudaimonic well-being, while *shiawase* (happiness) was felt as present-focused delight and calm.[^s3] Ikigai can be large or small: research on the concept does not require several outside conditions to line up at once.[^s5]

## Origin

The word grew from *ikiru-kahi*, roughly "the worth of living", an expression traced back to the Heian era; ikigai itself did not appear in mainstream Japanese dictionaries until 1908.[^s4] The psychiatrist Mieko Kamiya made it a subject of study in her 1966 book on ikigai, arguing that it is tied more closely to a person's sense of self and values than happiness is, and that it depends on *hankyo*, a felt resonance with others that comes mainly through relationships.[^s4] Japanese researchers later built questionnaires such as the Ikigai-9 scale and found that ikigai is not a single dimension: it combines personal meaning, an orientation toward the future and social connectedness.[^s5]

The four-circle diagram that circulates in Western workshops (what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, what you can be paid for) came later. Writers on the concept credit it to a blog post by entrepreneur Marc Winn, and note that Japanese studies do not use it.[^s6] Japanese researchers describe the diagram as a possibly useful heuristic for career planning, but one that differs from the ikigai examined in empirical work.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Ikigai is a useful counterweight to two common mistakes about the good life. The first is equating it with feeling happy: Japanese respondents distinguish having ikigai from feeling *shiawase*, and say the former involves values, effort and goals that reach into the future.[^s3] The second is equating it with a perfect job. Because the concept does not require passion, skill, need and pay to align, people can find ikigai in family, a craft, a hobby club or helping a neighbour, at any stage of life and whatever their income.[^s5][^s2] That makes it especially relevant in later life, for instance after retirement, when a work-defined purpose ends but a life worth living need not.[^s2]

It also matters that ikigai is partly relational. In studies of Japanese university students, ikigai rested on personally valued experiences (*keiken*) and on *ibasho*, relationships in which one can be one's authentic self.[^s4] Mathews's comparison found commitment to groups more typical of Japanese accounts and self-realisation more typical of American ones, a reminder that what makes life worth living is shaped by culture as well as temperament.[^s4]

## How to apply it

- **Notice, rather than design.** Instead of searching for the one intersection of four circles, ask what already makes an ordinary week feel worth living, and protect time for it.[^s5][^s3]
- **Look to people as well as projects.** Ask where you can be yourself with others, since authentic relationships were a core source of ikigai in Japanese research.[^s4]
- **Keep several sources.** Ikigai spans personal meaning, future orientation and social connection, so a life with only one source is fragile if that source is lost.[^s5]

At work, the distinction is practical: a manager who tells a team member to "find your ikigai" in the job is using the Venn diagram; asking which parts of the work, and which colleagues, make the days feel worthwhile is closer to the original idea.[^s5][^s4]

## Common misreadings

- **The Venn diagram is the Japanese philosophy of ikigai.** It is a recent Western overlay that Japanese research does not use.[^s6][^s5]
- **Ikigai means your career purpose.** It is broader, and includes enjoyed pursuits, relationships and growth as well as purpose.[^s2][^s3]
- **Ikigai is the same as happiness.** Japanese speakers distinguish it from *shiawase*.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

In the Ohsaki cohort of 43,391 Japanese adults followed for about seven years, people who reported no ikigai had higher all-cause mortality than those who did (adjusted hazard ratio 1.5), driven by cardiovascular disease and external causes rather than cancer.[^s1] An outcome-wide study of older Japanese adults over three years linked having ikigai to lower risk of functional disability, fewer depressive symptoms, less hopelessness and greater happiness, but found no significant association with mortality, and many outcomes were unrelated.[^s2] Both studies measured ikigai with a single question, both are observational, and the authors note that reverse causation and unmeasured confounding cannot be ruled out.[^s2][^s1] The status here is **contested**: the association with well-being is consistent, but popular claims that ikigai is a secret to long life, or that the Venn diagram captures a Japanese tradition, go beyond the evidence.[^s2][^s6] If a loss of meaning comes with persistent low mood or hopelessness, that is a reason to seek professional support, not a puzzle to solve with a worksheet.

## Connections

Ikigai **contrasts with** *shiawase*, the Japanese word for hedonic happiness, and sits closer to eudaimonic well-being.[^s3] It was **introduced** as a subject of study **by** Mieko Kamiya.[^s4] It is widely **confused with** the four-circle ikigai Venn diagram, which researchers distinguish from the empirical concept.[^s5] Its link with longer survival is **supported by** the Ohsaki cohort study,[^s1] and **challenged by** an outcome-wide study that found broad well-being associations but no significant mortality effect.[^s2] It overlaps with purpose in life, which the outcome-wide study treats as one part of the broader experience of ikigai.[^s2]
