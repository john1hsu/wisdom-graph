# Hedonic and eudaimonic well-being

The hedonic–eudaimonic framework is the most common way psychologists divide up "being well". Hedonic well-being is feeling good: pleasure, frequent positive mood, little negative mood and a judgment that life is satisfying, usually measured as *subjective well-being*.[^s1] Eudaimonic well-being is living well: realising one's potential, growing, finding meaning and acting from one's deepest values, often measured as *psychological well-being* with dimensions such as autonomy, purpose, mastery and positive relationships.[^s1] Researchers now use the terms for several different things (orientations or motives, behaviours, momentary experiences and overall functioning), which is one reason findings about them disagree.[^s2]

## Origin

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci's 2001 *Annual Review of Psychology* chapter organised the field around the two traditions and traced them to ancient philosophy.[^s1] They placed the hedonic view's roots in Aristippus, who held that the goal of life is to experience as much pleasure as possible, and followed it through Hobbes and Bentham into modern happiness research.[^s1] The eudaimonic view they traced to Aristotle, who regarded hedonic happiness as a vulgar ideal and located true happiness in living virtuously.[^s1] Alan Waterman brought the term *eudaimonia* into modern research as personal expressiveness: the experience of living in line with one's true self, which correlates with enjoyment but feels different, tied to effort, challenge and growth.[^s1]

The modern construct is looser than Aristotle's idea. For Aristotle, *eudaimonia* is not a mood but the activity of the rational soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life, and it needs some external goods such as friends and resources.[^s6] He counted pleasure as a good, but not the highest good, since a pleasant life is improved by adding wisdom to it.[^s6]

## Why it matters

The distinction names a real choice people face: whether to organise time around what feels good or around what seems worthwhile. In a set of four studies, Veronika Huta and Ryan found that the two motives did different jobs. Seeking pleasure, fun and relaxation was linked more to carefreeness and positive mood; seeking excellence, growth and acting on one's values was linked more to meaning and to "elevating" experiences such as awe and inspiration.[^s3] In a 10-day experiment, students who added pleasure-oriented activities felt better sooner, but many of those gains faded by three months, while those who added meaning-oriented activities reported more meaning right away and, at three months, more vitality and elevating experiences.[^s3] People high in both motives reported the highest well-being overall, so the authors concluded that the two pursuits complement each other.[^s3]

## How to use it

Treat the framework as a reflective lens rather than a scorecard. When planning a week, a holiday or a career move, ask both what will bring enjoyment and rest and what will express your values or help you grow, and expect them to pay off on different timescales.[^s3] Notice when one is crowding out the other: a life of constant striving with no ease, or of comfort with no challenge.[^s1][^s3] At work, it helps make sense of an experimental finding Ryan and Deci review: succeeding at a task under pressure raised happiness but not vitality, while succeeding with a sense of autonomy raised both, a pattern worth weighing when you set goals for yourself or others.[^s1]

## Common misreadings

- **Eudaimonia is "real" happiness and hedonia is shallow.** Researchers who study both argue this hierarchy is a value judgment imported from philosophy, not something the data establish.[^s4]
- **Psychology's eudaimonic well-being is Aristotle's eudaimonia.** The modern measures ask how meaningful or self-directed people feel; Aristotle meant an excellent, virtuous life as a whole, which is not a feeling.[^s6][^s2]
- **The two can be cleanly separated.** Ryan and Deci themselves described well-being as multidimensional, with separable but correlated parts.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The scientific standing of the split is **contested**. Todd Kashdan, Robert Biswas-Diener and Laura King argued that eudaimonia is poorly defined and inconsistently measured, and that the two kinds of well-being overlap and probably work together rather than as distinct types.[^s4] A study of 7,617 people from 109 countries found a latent correlation of .96 between self-reported hedonic and eudaimonic well-being, with one-factor and two-factor models fitting almost equally well and the pattern holding across seven world regions.[^s5] Its authors suggested studying narrower constructs, such as meaning in life or particular strengths, instead of the two broad categories, while noting that their single self-report method could not rule out distinct types.[^s5] Defenders reply that the term covers motives, experiences and functioning, and that clearer definitions would reduce the disagreement.[^s2] The Huta and Ryan experiment is encouraging but small, used undergraduates, lacked a control group and lost many participants by follow-up.[^s3]

## Connections

The framework was **introduced by** Richard Ryan (with Edward Deci) as an organising map of the field, and its eudaimonic half by Alan Waterman's work on personal expressiveness;[^s1] it is **influenced by** Aristotle's ethics.[^s1][^s6] Its eudaimonic side is **explained by** self-determination theory, which holds that meeting needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness fosters well-being.[^s1] Its claim that the two pursuits do different work is **supported by** the "Pursuing pleasure or virtue" study,[^s3] and it is **challenged by** the "Different types of well-being?" cross-cultural study[^s5] and by the "Reconsidering happiness" critique,[^s4] both from researchers **studying** it in positive psychology.[^s4] It sits under well-being in general and relates to eudaimonia, purpose and meaning in life, hedonic adaptation and the Epicurean and Aristotelian debates over pleasure and the good life.[^s1][^s6]
