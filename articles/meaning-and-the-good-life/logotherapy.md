# Logotherapy

Logotherapy is the approach to psychotherapy founded by the Viennese psychiatrist Viktor Frankl, named from the Greek *logos*, "meaning".[^s1] Its central claim is that the deepest human motive is a will to meaning, which Frankl set against the will to pleasure he saw in Freud and the will to power he saw in Adler.[^s1] It holds that meaning can be found in three ways: by creating a work or doing a deed, by experiencing something or loving someone, and by the attitude a person takes toward suffering that cannot be avoided.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Frankl first proposed a meaning-centred approach to mental healing in 1926 and called it logotherapy; in 1939 he named its philosophical basis "existential analysis", and in 1948 it was described as the third Viennese school of psychotherapy, after Freud's and Adler's.[^s2] In 1942 he and his family were deported to Theresienstadt; he later passed through Auschwitz and two Dachau subcamps, and his father, mother, brother and wife died in the camps.[^s2] In 1946 he dictated, in nine days, the account later published in English as *Man's Search for Meaning*, which made logotherapy widely known.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Logotherapy turns a large question into a practical one. Instead of asking what life owes us, it asks what a given situation calls on us to do, love or endure, so meaning becomes something found in concrete tasks rather than a general theory.[^s1] Frankl described a state he called the existential vacuum, a sense of emptiness when instinct and tradition no longer tell people what to do and they have not found their own aims.[^s1] He also stressed self-transcendence: on his account a full life is directed beyond oneself, toward a task or another person.[^s1]

The third source, attitude toward unavoidable suffering, does not say suffering is good or should be sought; it says that when a loss cannot be changed, the stance one takes toward it still matters.[^s1][^s4] This is why modern meaning-centred therapies for people facing serious illness draw on Frankl's ideas, including the claim that life keeps its meaning to the end and that legacy links past, present and future.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Two clinical techniques are well known. **Paradoxical intention** asks a person to deliberately wish for, or even exaggerate, a feared symptom, breaking the loop in which anticipatory anxiety feeds the symptom.[^s1] **Dereflection** counters hyper-reflection, the anxious self-monitoring that spoils what it watches, such as trying too hard to fall asleep, by turning attention outward to something else.[^s1] In one case Frankl describes, he helped a grieving widower by asking what would have happened had he died first, so that the man could see his grief as the price of sparing his wife that pain.[^s1]

Outside therapy, the three sources of meaning can serve as a review in a hard week (see the steps above): what am I creating, what or whom am I experiencing, and what attitude am I taking to what cannot be changed?[^s1][^s4] At work, this can mean finding meaning in craft and in colleagues even when a project fails; in relationships, it can mean attending to the other person rather than to how one is coming across.[^s1]

## Common misreadings

- **"Between stimulus and response there is a space…"** is widely credited to Frankl, but the Viktor Frankl Institute lists it as an alleged quote: Stephen Covey reported reading it in a library book whose author and title he did not record.[^s6] It fits Frankl's emphasis on freedom of attitude, but it is not a verified Frankl text.[^s6][^s1]
- **Meaning is what we demand from life.** Frankl reversed the question: rather than asking what we expect from life, we should see that life expects something from us.[^s1]
- **Suffering is required for meaning.** The attitudinal route concerns suffering that cannot be avoided, and it sits beside creative and experiential sources of meaning rather than above them.[^s1][^s4]
- **Logotherapy replaces other treatment.** Its modern clinical forms are structured, time-limited therapies delivered by trained clinicians, often compared with supportive therapy.[^s3][^s4] Anyone in persistent distress should seek professional or crisis support rather than rely on a reading of Frankl.

## Evidence and limits

Frankl's observations in the camps, which shaped much of *Man's Search for Meaning*, were personal testimony, not controlled research.[^s1][^s2] Formal testing came later, mostly of therapies derived from his ideas. A 2015 meta-analysis of 15 randomised trials of existential therapies (1,792 participants) found that meaning-centred therapies had a large effect on meaning in life after treatment (d = 0.65) that was still present at follow-up, but no significant effect on psychopathology across only two studies.[^s5] The authors judged the evidence small and of low quality, noted that most participants had cancer or another physical illness, and warned that short, directive formats and closely matched outcome measures may inflate effects.[^s5]

In one well-known trial, 253 people with advanced cancer were randomised to eight sessions of meaning-centred group psychotherapy or supportive group therapy; among those who attended at least three sessions, the meaning-centred group improved more on spiritual well-being, quality of life, depression and hopelessness, while intent-to-treat analyses confirmed gains only for quality of life, depression and hopelessness.[^s3] The status here is therefore **mixed**: promising, mostly in serious illness, with modest and uneven evidence and little direct testing of Frankl's original techniques.[^s5][^s3]

## Connections

Logotherapy was **introduced by** Viktor Frankl.[^s2] It **addresses** the existential vacuum and, through dereflection, hyper-reflection.[^s1] It **contrasts with** psychoanalysis, replacing Freud's will to pleasure with a will to meaning as the primary motive.[^s1] It is **applied in** serious illness, where individual meaning-centred psychotherapy is built explicitly on its principles,[^s4] and it is **supported**, with caveats, by a meta-analysis of existential therapies[^s5] and by a randomised trial of meaning-centred group psychotherapy.[^s3] It relates to meaning and purpose in life, which it locates in concrete tasks;[^s1] to self-transcendence, his name for directing life beyond oneself;[^s1] and, through its teaching that we can choose our attitude even under extreme conditions, to Stoicism and to post-traumatic growth.[^s1]
