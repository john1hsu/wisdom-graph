# Purpose in life

Purpose in life is a central, lasting aim that sets the direction of a life: it generates and ranks a person's goals, steers everyday behaviour and gives a felt sense that life is going somewhere.[^s2] Psychologists Patrick McKnight and Todd Kashdan compare it to a compass: it points the way, but you still choose whether to walk.[^s2] William Damon and colleagues add a second ingredient, defining purpose as a stable, general intention to accomplish something that matters to the self and also makes a difference beyond it.[^s3] Purpose is therefore narrower than meaning in life: it is the forward-looking, goal-directed facet of meaning, alongside coherence (life makes sense) and significance (life is worth living).[^s4]

## Origin

Questions about what a life is *for* are ancient, and philosophers still divide over where purpose can come from. On a God-centred view, a life matters by fulfilling a purpose God assigns; naturalist views locate it either in what a person cares about, in what is objectively worthwhile, or, in Susan Wolf's influential hybrid, where subjective attraction meets objective worth.[^s6] The modern psychology of purpose grew from research on adult well-being and development; Damon's Stanford group made purpose a central topic in the study of adolescence, arguing that youth is when many people first commit to an aim larger than self-advancement.[^s3] Martela and Steger later argued that "meaning" had been used loosely and that purpose deserves to be studied as its own facet.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Purpose is where wisdom about the good life becomes practical. McKnight and Kashdan describe it as a higher-level goal manager: without one, a person can be busy with many goals and still drift; with one, competing demands can be weighed against something that does not change week to week.[^s2] They also note that purpose varies in scope (how much of life it touches), strength (how hard it pulls) and awareness (how clearly you could say what it is), so it is a matter of degree rather than something you either have or lack.[^s2]

It also matters because of what purpose is *for*. Damon's definition deliberately includes an outward-facing element, contributing to others or to something beyond oneself, which separates purpose from private ambition.[^s3] Philosophers add a caution: caring intensely about an aim is not enough on most views, since devotion to something worthless (collecting string, in one example philosophers discuss) does not seem to make a life meaningful.[^s6] A wise purpose is one you both care about and could defend as worth caring about.[^s6]

## Common misreadings

- **Purpose is a goal.** Goals have endpoints; purpose generates and organises goals, and you can have many goals without a purpose.[^s2]
- **Purpose is found once, like a hidden treasure.** It develops through multiple pathways, often over time, and the capacity to form one depends on reflection and planning that circumstances can support or impair.[^s2]
- **Purpose and meaning are the same thing.** Purpose is one of three facets of meaning; a life can feel coherent and worthwhile while its direction is still unsettled.[^s4]
- **Any strong purpose is good.** Intensity is not worth; the outward reach and the value of the aim matter.[^s3][^s6]

At work, the same distinctions apply: a quarterly target is a goal, while a durable aim such as helping patients recover or teaching people to read can organise many targets and help you decide which ones are worth chasing.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

A 2016 meta-analysis of 10 prospective studies (about 136,000 people, mean follow-up 7.3 years) found that higher purpose was associated with lower all-cause mortality (adjusted relative risk 0.83) and fewer cardiovascular events (adjusted relative risk 0.83).[^s1] A 12-year study of over 11,000 adults aged 50 and older found that changes in purpose predicted later changes in several health markers more strongly than the reverse, though better health was also linked to higher purpose.[^s5]

The limits are real. The meta-analysis found large heterogeneity, treated different questionnaires as interchangeable, could not consistently control for depression, social support or health behaviours, and showed some signs of publication bias in adjusted analyses.[^s1] These are observational associations, so purpose has not been shown to cause longer life, and health partly shapes purpose in turn.[^s5][^s1] McKnight and Kashdan themselves noted that self-report scales may miss much of what purpose is.[^s2] The status here is therefore **mixed**: a consistent, moderate association with good outcomes, without proof of cause or of how best to build purpose deliberately.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

Purpose is **part of** meaning in life, one of its three facets alongside coherence and significance.[^s4] It is often **confused with** life goals, though it is the structure that generates and manages them rather than one more goal.[^s2] Its widely used definition in youth research was **introduced by** William Damon and colleagues.[^s3] Its link with longer life and fewer cardiovascular events is **supported by** a meta-analysis of prospective studies,[^s1] and the idea that purpose may protect health is, more cautiously, **supported by** a bidirectional study showing purpose predicting later health more than the reverse.[^s5] It relates to eudaimonia and to the philosophical debate over whether a life's point must be chosen, given or discovered,[^s6] and to ikigai, one of several measures the meta-analysis pooled as equivalent indicators of purpose.[^s1]
