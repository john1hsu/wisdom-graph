# Calling

A **calling** is a way of relating to work in which the work feels like a meaningful end in itself, bound up with who you are and usually with doing something of value for others. In the classic three-way distinction, people with a *job* orientation work mainly for pay and necessity, people with a *career* orientation for advancement and standing, and people with a *calling* orientation for the fulfilment of the work itself.[^s1] Researchers now distinguish two flavours: an inner, self-focused calling marked by passion, enjoyment and personal meaning, and an outer, other-focused calling felt as a duty or destiny to meet a real need in the world.[^s2]

## Origin

The word began in religious use: people were understood to be called by God to morally and socially significant work, a history that the sociologist Max Weber traced in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*.[^s1] Modern psychology took up the idea in 1997, when Amy Wrzesniewski and colleagues asked 196 full-time university and college employees which of three short descriptions fitted them best; the orientations cut across occupations, so that even a small group of administrative assistants split roughly evenly between them.[^s1] Bunderson and Thompson later called the older, duty-laden sense of the word *neoclassical*, to set it apart from the more recent, self-expressive sense of doing what you love.[^s3]

## Why it matters

How you see your work may matter as much as what the work is. In the 1997 study, people with a calling orientation reported higher life and job satisfaction and missed fewer days of work than those with a job or career orientation, and the authors argued that satisfaction depended more on this outlook than on pay or prestige.[^s1] A 2023 meta-analysis of 201 studies and about 186,000 people found that calling was linked with finding work meaningful (r ≈ .61), with job satisfaction (r ≈ .46), with psychological well-being (r ≈ .45) and with life satisfaction and positive affect (r ≈ .28), and with somewhat lower strain such as burnout (r ≈ −.23).[^s2] For anyone weighing a career move, a promotion or a retirement, the concept asks a wise question: what is this work *for*, and for whom?

## Living a calling wisely

- **Treat it as something you live, not only something you have.** In a survey of 746 working adults, feeling called paid off in well-being mainly through actually living the calling and through life meaning, and income and motivation shaped how far that happened.[^s6]
- **Craft the work you have.** People whose calling lies elsewhere, or who feel more than one, can still pursue it by emphasising certain tasks, expanding the job, reframing the role, learning from others who do the work, or taking it up as a serious hobby.[^s5]
- **Count the costs as well as the meaning.** Zookeepers who felt called identified strongly with their work and found it significant, but were also readier to give up pay, personal time and comfort for it and held their employer to a higher moral standard.[^s3]
- **Keep a boundary between devotion and exploitation.** In a series of experiments, people judged it more acceptable to ask passionate workers to do unpaid extra hours or demeaning tasks, partly because they assumed such workers would have volunteered anyway or that the work was its own reward.[^s4]

## Common misreadings

A common misreading is that a calling is found once, like hidden treasure, and that without one work cannot be meaningful. The 1997 data showed callings in clerical and professional roles alike, and the job-crafting research shows people building meaning into roles they did not choose for love.[^s1][^s5] A second misreading is that a calling is always good for you. Bunderson and Thompson describe it as both ennobling and binding, and the exploitation studies show that admiration for passion can license others to ask too much of the passionate.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The positive links are consistent but mostly correlational. The 2023 meta-analysis notes that most calling studies are cross-sectional and self-reported, so they cannot show whether calling produces well-being or whether people who are already thriving describe their work as a calling; samples were also largely from the United States, and heterogeneity across studies was high.[^s2] The original 1997 sample was small, mostly female and volunteer, and the calling group earned more and had more prestigious jobs, which the authors flagged as a confound.[^s1] The darker findings rest on qualitative interviews and vignette experiments rather than long-term field data.[^s3][^s4] In short, a calling tends to go with a richer working life, with real costs that call for judgement. Where devotion to work has turned into exhaustion or persistent distress, that is a reason to talk to a doctor, counsellor or other professional, not to push harder.

## Connections

Calling is a work-centred form of meaning in life and purpose in life, its **broader** concepts: the meta-analysis found its strongest link with meaningful work and a strong link with psychological well-being, the eudaimonic side of hedonic and eudaimonic well-being.[^s2] It is **demonstrated by** the 1997 jobs, careers and callings study, which brought the distinction into psychology and was **influenced by** Max Weber's account of the word's religious history.[^s1] It is **supported by** the 2023 meta-analysis of calling and the good life, and **demonstrated by** the zookeeper study that revealed its double edge.[^s2][^s3] It is **challenged by** the passion-exploitation experiments, which question the uncritical celebration of passion at work.[^s4] It **complements** job crafting, which gives people ways to live an unanswered calling inside or alongside their current role.[^s5] One account of why living a calling matters is **explained by** self-determination theory, with motivation shaping how far a felt calling turns into well-being.[^s6] It is **related** to ikigai, which is broader than work; to knowing when to quit, when a calling has become a trap; to identity experiments, for testing a possible calling;[^s5] and to wisdom in the professions, where serving others well through one's occupation is central.[^s3]
