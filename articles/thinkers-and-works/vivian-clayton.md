# Vivian Clayton

Vivian P. Clayton is an American psychologist whose graduate research in the 1970s is described by the wisdom researcher Igor Grossmann as the first attempt to study wisdom empirically.[^s1] She later worked as a geriatric neuropsychologist in California.[^s1][^s5] Her three-part picture of wisdom, cognitive, reflective and compassionate, became a starting point for much of the research that followed.[^s3][^s5]

## Origin

Clayton came to wisdom through the psychology of ageing. Her 1975 paper, "Wisdom as contradictive cognition", applied Erik Erikson's theory of lifespan development to older adults, and later writers cite it for the idea that wisdom is at home with paradox in a way that strict logic is not.[^s6] Reviewing ancient texts, she noticed that the people they called wise were mostly decision makers, so she asked law students, law professors and retired judges to list the traits of a wise person.[^s5]

Working with the gerontologist James Birren, she then had young, middle-aged and older adults judge how similar pairs of words were, among them "wise", "aged", "myself" and a dozen traits drawn from her earlier study, and mapped the answers with multidimensional scaling.[^s3] Grossmann describes her dissertation sample as 83 lay Americans.[^s1] Three clusters emerged: **cognitive** qualities (knowledgeable, experienced, intelligent, pragmatic, observant), **reflective** ones (introspective, intuitive) and **affective** ones (understanding, empathetic, peaceful, gentle).[^s3] Clayton and Birren published the results in 1980 as "The development of wisdom across the life-span: A reexamination of an ancient topic."[^s2][^s3]

## Key ideas

- **Wisdom is more than knowing.** In a 1983 paper she defined intelligence as logical, abstract and conceptual thinking, and wisdom as the ability to grasp human nature, which is paradoxical, contradictory and always changing.[^s4]
- **"Should I?" as well as "How do I?"** Intelligence, she argued, helps people work out how to carry out the tasks of living; wisdom weighs the consequences of an action for oneself and others, and so asks whether it should be done at all.[^s4]
- **Head, reflection and heart together.** On her account no single quality makes someone wise; knowledge needs reflection to yield insight, and compassion to put that insight to use for others.[^s3][^s5]

## Why it matters

Clayton helped turn wisdom from a topic for philosophers and theologians into something psychologists could measure.[^s1][^s2] Jeste and colleagues name Clayton and Birren in the United States, alongside Baltes and Smith in Germany, as the pioneers of empirical wisdom research in the 1970s, and note that Monika Ardelt later expanded the Clayton–Birren model.[^s2] Ardelt calls their study groundbreaking and says it guided her three-dimensional wisdom model, whose cognitive, reflective and compassionate dimensions mirror Clayton's clusters.[^s3]

Her distinction between intelligence and wisdom also anticipated a theme of later work: that being clever is not the same as judging well, and that wisdom deserves to be studied in its own right.[^s4][^s1]

## Using her ideas

Her framework can serve as a quick check before a weighty choice. Ask the intelligence question (how could this be done?) and then the wisdom question (should it be done, and what will it mean for the people affected?).[^s4] Check all three of her elements: whether you know enough, whether you have stepped back to reflect, and whether you have considered the people involved with understanding.[^s3][^s5] At work, a technically brilliant plan that skips the second and third checks is the kind of clever-but-unwise decision her distinction warns against.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

Clayton's studies mapped what people *believe* wisdom is, not whether wise people actually behave differently, and her samples were small and American.[^s1][^s3] The "first" label is Grossmann's; other histories share the founding credit among several 1970s groups.[^s1][^s2] Some details of her early work, such as the 1975 paper, are known today mainly through later citations.[^s6] Her three components remain influential, but they are a description of a folk concept, not a tested theory of how wisdom develops.[^s3]

## Connections

Clayton is credited with **introducing** the **psychology of wisdom** as an empirical field,[^s1] while sharing its founding with Paul Baltes's Berlin group.[^s2] She **authored** *The development of wisdom across the life-span* with James Birren[^s3] and "Wisdom and intelligence" (1983).[^s4] She **influenced** Monika Ardelt, whose **three-dimensional model** builds on her clusters,[^s3] and was **influenced by** Erik Erikson's lifespan theory.[^s6] Her work is an early landmark in **implicit theories of wisdom**,[^s3] bears on **ageing**, where her research began,[^s4] and links to **practical wisdom** and **wise reasoning** through her contrast between knowing how and judging whether.[^s4] Her affective and reflective dimensions relate to **compassion** and **self-reflection**.[^s3][^s5]
