# Robert J. Sternberg

Robert J. Sternberg is an American psychologist, now a professor in Cornell University's College of Human Ecology and an honorary professor at Heidelberg University, whose research spans intelligence, creativity, wisdom, leadership, thinking styles and love.[^s2] In the study of wisdom he is known for three things: an early study of what ordinary people mean by wisdom, the balance theory of wisdom, and a sustained argument that intelligence without wisdom can make clever people dangerously foolish.[^s1][^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Sternberg took his BA in psychology at Yale in 1972 and his PhD at Stanford in 1975, then spent three decades at Yale, where he founded the Center for the Psychology of Abilities, Competencies and Expertise.[^s3] He later moved into university leadership, as dean of arts and sciences at Tufts, provost at Oklahoma State and, briefly in 2013, president of the University of Wyoming, before joining Cornell in 2014.[^s3] He has served as president of the American Psychological Association and edited *Psychological Bulletin* and *Perspectives on Psychological Science*.[^s2] He describes his outlook as closely aligned with the ecological, contextualist tradition of Urie Bronfenbrenner.[^s3]

## Why it matters

**Asking what people mean by wisdom.** In a 1985 paper written at Yale, Sternberg distinguished *explicit* theories, which scientists build and test against task performance, from *implicit* theories, the beliefs people already hold, and argued that the latter should be discovered rather than invented.[^s1] He asked physicists, philosophers and professors of business and art, along with members of the public, to describe and rate the ideally wise, intelligent and creative person.[^s1] Laypeople's pictures of wisdom and intelligence overlapped heavily, and in a later experiment adults judging simulated recommendation letters rated candidates' wisdom in line with those prototypes, suggesting that folk theories steer real judgments of people.[^s1] He also warned that such beliefs can be mistaken and need checking against explicit research, and that his samples came from one culture at one time.[^s1]

**The balance theory.** In 1998 Sternberg proposed that wisdom is the use of intelligence, creativity and knowledge for a common good, achieved by balancing one's own interests, other people's interests and wider interests, over the short and long term, guided by ethical values.[^s4][^s6] The wise person also chooses well among adapting to a situation, reshaping it or leaving it for a better one, and how wise a solution is depends on how well it fits its context.[^s4] Later versions add an account of why people often reward clever, self-serving "wise guys" over those who are actually wise.[^s7] He has built wisdom into a broader leadership model, WICS (wisdom, intelligence and creativity, synthesized), and into recent work on wise leaders.[^s2]

**Foolishness in smart people.** In his 2003 APA presidential column, Sternberg argued that bright, highly rewarded people are especially prone to foolishness through egocentrism, a sense of omniscience, a sense of omnipotence and a sense of invulnerability.[^s5] Later work adds unrealistic optimism and ethical disengagement to the list.[^s6] For anyone making decisions, these give a practical checklist: am I weighing only my own interests, assuming my expertise covers everything, or betting that consequences won't reach me?

## Applying his ideas

Sternberg's chief practical aim has been education. He argues schools should attend to how students use what they know, not only to what they know, and should teach them to take other points of view seriously, with reasoning, resilience and responsibility treated as "the other three Rs".[^s5] With colleagues he designed ways to infuse wise thinking into existing subjects such as history, using dilemmas in which people must weigh their own needs against those of others, the kind of problem that knowledge or analytical skill alone does not solve.[^s8] At work, the balance theory suggests a simple discipline before a major decision: list whose interests are affected (yours, the people around you, the wider organisation or community), what happens in the short and long run, and which values decide the trade-offs.[^s4][^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The balance theory has been more influential as a conceptual map than as a tested model. A 2018 measurement study by Grossmann's group notes that the link between balancing interests and wisdom had remained largely theoretical, with little effort to turn balance criteria into empirical measures, and that common wisdom measures, from coded interviews to self-report scales, have their own weaknesses.[^s4] The 1985 implicit-theories work drew on a single, mostly American setting, a limit Sternberg acknowledged himself.[^s1] His list of foolish fallacies is an argument drawn from cases and theory rather than an experimentally established taxonomy.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Sternberg **introduced** the study of **implicit theories of wisdom** as a research programme, contrasting lay beliefs with expert models.[^s1] He **introduced** the **balance theory of wisdom**, which Brienza and colleagues cite as the central statement that balancing interests is the heart of wisdom.[^s4] He **introduced** the **WICS model** of leadership.[^s2] His work belongs to the **psychology of wisdom**, where it sits alongside cognition-focused expert models such as the **Berlin wisdom paradigm**, and his fallacies of omniscience and invulnerability are the shadow side of **intellectual humility**.[^s4][^s5] His recent writing on wise leaders links his ideas to **wise leadership**.[^s2]
