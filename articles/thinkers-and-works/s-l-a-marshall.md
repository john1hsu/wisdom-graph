# Samuel Lyman Atwood Marshall

Samuel Lyman Atwood Marshall (1900–1977), known as S. L. A. Marshall or "SLAM", was an American journalist, Army historian and military analyst who observed soldiers in battle in five conflicts, among them both world wars, Korea and Vietnam.[^s4] He matters to anyone interested in learning from experience for two opposite reasons. He pioneered a way of gathering people straight after a hard event to reconstruct, together and candidly, what actually happened, which became the model for the after-action review.[^s1][^s4] And his most famous finding, the "ratio of fire", turned out to be far less solid than his confident prose suggested, a reminder that even good fact-finders can outrun their evidence.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Marshall came to military history from newspaper journalism, and his critics and admirers alike trace both his strengths and his weaknesses to that background.[^s2][^s5] As a member of the Army's team of historians in the Second World War, he was first sent to the Pacific, where he found it hard to reconstruct battles from official records alone.[^s1] His answer was the "interview after combat": assembling the survivors of an action soon after the fighting and leading them, as a group, through the battle from start to finish.[^s1] By the war's end, more than 2,000 such interviews had been carried out in Europe alone, and Marshall and his colleagues extended the method to Korea and Vietnam.[^s1] At Makin Island in 1943 he questioned the survivors of a night fight together for several hours a day over four days, and he held that a day of company combat needed two to four days of interviewing to reconstruct.[^s5]

## Why it matters

The interview after combat produced detailed accounts of what happened that the paperwork did not contain, and the Army later took it as the model for the after-action review, adopting the method's objectivity and non-punitive atmosphere while discarding the old one-way critique by umpires.[^s1] A Korean War escort recalled that Marshall's sessions were held soon after combat, ran one to two hours, promised the men anonymity and were informal and open-ended, which helped soldiers speak freely.[^s3] Historians credit him with turning attention from generals to the experience of ordinary soldiers; Spiller judged his insistence on understanding war through those who fight it a lasting contribution.[^s2][^s5]

## Key works and ideas

- **The interview after combat.** Group, chronological, soon after the event, with participants filling in each other's gaps; a real innovation in historical method.[^s1][^s2]
- ***Men Against Fire* (1947).** His most influential book argued that only about one in four infantrymen fired at the enemy in a given action, and that more and better fire was the key to winning; the Army treated the claim almost as doctrine.[^s2]
- **Popular combat histories.** Marshall had 29 publications, which carried his interview-based accounts to a wide audience.[^s4]

## Lessons for living more wisely

Marshall's method suggests how to learn from a hard shared experience: meet soon, while memories are fresh; go through events in order before anyone judges them; let everyone speak, not only the most senior; and make it safe to tell the truth, for example by setting aside blame.[^s1][^s3] These are the same features that make a modern after-action review work.[^s1]

His story also carries a cautionary lesson about one's own claims. The firing question seems to have come up only incidentally in his interviews, his notes were sparse, and he later spoke of his figures with more certainty than his records could bear.[^s2][^s3] A vivid, round number that fits a strong conviction is exactly the kind of claim to check hardest, especially when it is your own.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

Because this is a biographical article, its status is **historical**. The ratio of fire, however, has been heavily challenged. Spiller found no statistical compilation in Marshall's surviving field notebooks, no sign of data collection in his correspondence, and no memory from his assistant John Westover of Marshall asking who had fired; Marshall's own count of interviews also shifted between accounts.[^s2] Spiller concluded that the systematic data behind the ratio "appears to have been an invention".[^s2] Chambers, drawing on a Korean War escort's recollections, judged the one-in-four figure most likely a guess rather than the product of sampling.[^s3] Marshall also exaggerated some events in his other writings.[^s3] None of this undoes the value of the group interview itself, which critics and the Army continued to credit.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Marshall's interview after combat was the model on which the **after-action review** was built, so he is linked to that practice and to **learning from experience** more broadly.[^s1] His reputation is **challenged by** Spiller's **critique of the ratio of fire** and by Chambers's **new evidence on fire ratios**.[^s2][^s3] The gap between his confident numbers and his records connects him to **honesty**, **intellectual humility** and **self-deception**: the habit of holding one's own favourite findings to the same standard as anyone else's.[^s2][^s3]
