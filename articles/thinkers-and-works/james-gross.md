# James J. Gross

James J. Gross is the Ernest R. Hilgard Professor of Psychology at Stanford University, where he directs the Stanford Psychophysiology Laboratory and the Stanford Center for Affective Science.[^s1] His research centres on emotion regulation, and in particular on two everyday strategies, reappraisal and suppression.[^s1] For a reader interested in wisdom he matters because he gave psychology a precise vocabulary for something wisdom traditions have long discussed: the difference between seeing a situation differently and merely hiding how one feels about it.[^s2]

## Origin

Gross studied philosophy and psychology at Yale (BA 1987), spent a year as a graduate visiting student at Oxford, and took a PhD in clinical psychology at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1993.[^s1] He has since published more than 700 papers, co-founded the Society for Affective Science and its journal *Affective Science*, and been elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences; Stanford has also recognised him repeatedly for teaching and mentoring.[^s1]

His landmark 1998 paper, "The emerging field of emotion regulation", drew together work that had been scattered across psychoanalysis, stress and coping research and developmental psychology.[^s2] He credited Freud's account of defences and, especially, Richard Lazarus's coping and appraisal research as forerunners, while arguing that emotion regulation should look at specific emotions, positive as well as negative.[^s2] He defined it broadly as the processes by which people influence which emotions they have, when, and how they experience and express them, and treated it as neither good nor bad in itself.[^s2]

## Key ideas

The **process model** at the heart of that paper orders regulation strategies by where they act as an emotion unfolds: choosing a situation, changing it, steering attention, changing how the situation is appraised, and modulating the response once it has begun.[^s2] The first four act before the emotion is fully under way; the last acts afterwards.[^s2] Gross used the model to contrast two strategies: **reappraisal**, a form of cognitive change, which in his early experiments generally reduced negative feeling, and **expressive suppression**, a form of response modulation, which hid the display, reduced only some feelings and raised sympathetic nervous-system arousal.[^s2]

With Oliver John he then built the ten-item **Emotion Regulation Questionnaire**, which measures how habitually a person reappraises (for example, thinking about a stressful situation in a way that keeps them calm) and how habitually they suppress (for example, keeping their emotions to themselves).[^s3] The questionnaire made it possible to study these two habits across large samples, and it is the basis of much of the later research on what suppression costs and what reappraisal buys.[^s3] In a later **extended process model**, Gross drew on research into action control to describe how people decide whether to regulate at all, which strategy to choose and how to carry it out, not only which strategies exist.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Gross's distinction gives practical shape to an old piece of advice: a calm that comes from understanding a situation differently is not the same as a calm face over unchanged feelings.[^s2] Wisdom researchers now treat the regulation of emotion as part of what they measure; the San Diego Wisdom Scale, for example, includes emotional regulation as one of its components alongside self-reflection, prosocial behaviour and tolerance of other views.[^s6] At work and at home his model offers a simple check: before deciding how to manage your face in a tense meeting, ask whether you could first change the situation, where you put your attention, or what the moment means.[^s2] It is a framework for reflection, not treatment; persistent distress or emotional numbness is a reason to seek professional support.

## Common misreadings

- **"Reappraisal good, suppression bad."** Gross's model describes strategies and their typical effects; his 1998 review explicitly framed regulation as neither inherently good nor bad, and holding back a display can fit a situation.[^s2]
- **Suppression as all of regulation.** The process model lists five families of strategies, and hiding a reaction is only the last of them.[^s2]
- **Habits as fixed traits.** The questionnaire asks how a person usually handles emotions; a high suppression score describes a habitual tendency, which the flexibility research suggests can be used well or badly depending on the situation.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

Gross's framework has been extended and also qualified. George Bonanno and his colleague Burton argued that the field had slipped into a "fallacy of uniform efficacy", treating reappraisal as almost always adaptive and suppression as almost always harmful.[^s4] They pointed to a meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons in which suppressing the expression of an emotion generally worked, while suppressing the experience or the thoughts behind it did not, and to findings that reappraisal ability went with less depression under uncontrollable stress but more under controllable stress.[^s4] Their alternative, **regulatory flexibility**, stresses reading the context, having a repertoire and switching when a strategy fails, though they acknowledge that this proposal itself needs more longitudinal evidence.[^s4] Commentators on the extended process model have likewise asked for more attention to automatic regulation and to regulation between people.[^s5] As a person entry, Gross's evidence status is historical; the evidence for particular strategies is weighed in their own articles.

## Connections

Gross **introduced** the modern construct of **emotional suppression** as a response-focused strategy in his process model, and he **authored** the 1998 review that set out that model and, with Oliver John, the **Emotion Regulation Questionnaire**.[^s2][^s3] He was **influenced by** **Richard Lazarus**'s coping and appraisal research.[^s2] His implied contrast between good and bad strategies is **challenged by** Bonanno and Burton's **regulatory flexibility** review.[^s4] He relates to **cognitive reappraisal**, **emotion regulation**, **equanimity** and the **psychology of wisdom**, where emotional regulation appears as one component of measured wisdom.[^s2][^s6]
