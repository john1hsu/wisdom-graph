# Susan Nolen-Hoeksema

Susan Nolen-Hoeksema (1959–2013) was an American clinical psychologist whose research changed how psychologists, and many general readers, think about the difference between thinking a problem through and merely going over it. She is best known for response styles theory and for the modern concept of rumination: passively dwelling on the causes and consequences of one's distress without moving to solve anything.[^s1][^s3] Her second great theme was why women are more likely than men to become depressed.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin

She was born in Springfield, Illinois, in 1959, took a BA in psychology at Yale in 1982 and a PhD at the University of Pennsylvania in 1986.[^s1] Her obituary in *American Psychologist* names Irving Janis, Robert Sternberg and Martin Seligman among her mentors and records that her doctorate was in clinical psychology.[^s2] She taught at Stanford from 1986 to 1995 and at the University of Michigan from 1995 to 2004, where she directed the Institute for Research on Women and Gender, then returned to Yale, where she later chaired the psychology department.[^s1][^s2] She was the founding editor of the *Annual Review of Clinical Psychology* and received a Research Career Award from the National Institute of Mental Health. She died on 2 January 2013, aged 53, after heart surgery.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Her central idea, set out in 1991, was that how long a low mood lasts depends partly on how people respond to it. Responding by ruminating on one's symptoms, their causes and their consequences was proposed to lengthen depression, compared with taking action or turning to absorbing activity, because it lets the low mood bias thinking and gets in the way of problem solving and goal-directed behaviour.[^s3] Later work found that rumination also worsens negative thinking, impairs problem solving and erodes social support.[^s4]

This matters for anyone trying to reflect wisely. Wisdom traditions prize self-examination, and her research showed that self-focused thought is not automatically insight: it can be a trap that feels like thinking.[^s4][^s5] Her work on gender asked why, although there is no gender gap in depression in prepubescent children, girls become about twice as likely as boys to be depressed after about age 15. With Joan Girgus she concluded that girls enter adolescence with more risk factors and then meet more of its new challenges.[^s6] Her studies suggested that a greater tendency to ruminate is part of this gap.[^s5]

## Key works and ideas

- **Response styles theory (1991).** Ruminative versus distracting or active responses to low mood shape how long it lasts.[^s3]
- **Brooding and reflection (2003).** With Wendy Treynor and Richard Gonzalez she cleaned depression-related items out of the standard rumination questionnaire and found two distinct parts, *brooding* and *reflective pondering*, that relate differently to depression and to its gender gap. This refined her own theory.[^s5]
- **"Rethinking Rumination" (2008).** A review with Blair Wisco and Sonja Lyubomirsky concluding that rumination predicts the *onset* of depression more consistently than its duration, contrary to the original emphasis.[^s4]
- **Books for general readers.** *Women Who Think Too Much* (2003) translated the research on "overthinking" into strategies for breaking free of it, and *The Power of Women* followed. She also wrote widely used textbooks.[^s1][^s7]
- **Later work.** She linked rumination to substance misuse and eating disorders, and with her lab developed a school-based programme teaching emotion-regulation skills to adolescent girls prone to rumination.[^s1][^s7]

## Lessons for living more wisely

Her research suggests a few portable habits. Notice when going over a setback has stopped producing anything new. Ask what can be done next, rather than only why it happened and what it says about you. When you are already low, an absorbing activity can stop the spiral deepening; experiments repeatedly found that positive distraction lifts a depressed mood, though correlational evidence is less consistent.[^s3][^s4] Keep the reflective, problem-focused kind of self-examination, and let go of the brooding kind.[^s5] These are general reflections, not treatment: persistent low mood, sleeplessness or hopelessness are reasons to speak with a doctor or mental-health professional, or a crisis line in an emergency.

## Evidence and limits

The core link between rumination and depression has been supported in laboratory and field studies, and her theory proved productive enough to be revised by her own later work.[^s3][^s4] The limits deserve note. The 2008 review found rumination predicts onset of depression more consistently than duration, so the original 1991 emphasis was too narrow.[^s4] Early rumination questionnaires overlapped with depression itself, which the 2003 analysis was designed to correct.[^s5] Popular figures about "overthinking" come from a single community survey reported in a press release: for example, 73% of 25–35-year-olds against 20% of 65–75-year-olds, and 57% of women against 43% of men; treat them as illustrations rather than settled rates.[^s7]

## Connections

She is the researcher behind **rumination** and **response styles theory**, which explains it.[^s3][^s4] Her brooding-versus-reflection distinction bears on **self-reflection**, and her overthinking research sits beside **worry**.[^s5][^s7] She was **influenced by Martin Seligman** and, as a mentor named in her obituary, **Robert Sternberg**.[^s2]
