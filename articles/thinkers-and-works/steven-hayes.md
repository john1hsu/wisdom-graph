# Steven C. Hayes

Steven C. Hayes is a clinical psychologist and behaviour analyst, now professor emeritus in the Department of Psychology at the University of Nevada, Reno, where he held a Nevada Foundation Professorship in the behaviour analysis programme.[^s1] He is best known as the developer of relational frame theory, an account of human language and higher cognition, and of acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), which his university describes as a popular evidence-based psychotherapy built on mindfulness, acceptance and values.[^s1] For a reader interested in wisdom, he matters because ACT turned an old piece of contemplative advice, holding one's thoughts lightly while acting on what one cares about, into a named set of skills that can be taught and tested.[^s2]

## Origin

Hayes took a BA in psychology at Loyola Marymount University in 1970 and an MA and PhD in clinical psychology at West Virginia University, finishing in 1977.[^s1] His profile credits him with 47 books and more than 675 scientific articles, presidencies of APA Division 25 (behaviour analysis) and the Association for Contextual Behavioral Science, service as the first secretary-treasurer of the Association for Psychological Science, and a lifetime achievement award from the Association for Behavioral and Cognitive Therapies.[^s1]

ACT grew out of a long programme to build a modern form of behaviour analysis that could explain language.[^s2] Its earliest form, called "comprehensive distancing", was tested in small randomised trials by Zettle and Hayes in 1986; nearly fifteen years passed before the next wave of trials, after the main ACT treatment book by Hayes, Strosahl and Wilson appeared in 1999.[^s2] The approach rests on a pragmatic philosophy, functional contextualism, which judges ideas by whether they work for a stated goal.[^s2]

## Key ideas

**Relational frame theory** holds that the core of human language is a learned ability to relate events to one another arbitrarily, so that words and thoughts can come to carry the force of the things they stand for.[^s2] On this view much suffering comes less from the content of thoughts than from the contexts that let them run behaviour.[^s2]

ACT names two processes that make people rigid. In **cognitive fusion**, rules and verbal stories steer action more than what is actually happening; in **experiential avoidance**, people try to change or escape unwanted feelings and thoughts even when doing so harms them.[^s2] The alternative, **psychological flexibility**, means contacting the present moment fully and then changing or persisting in behaviour as one's chosen values require.[^s2] It is built from six processes: acceptance, **cognitive defusion**, contact with the present moment, a sense of self as the perspective from which experience is observed, values, and committed action.[^s2] Defusion aims to change what a thought does rather than what it says, as in the "milk, milk" exercise, where a word is repeated aloud until it loses its meaning.[^s2] Hayes later helped develop **process-based therapy**, which organises treatment around such change processes rather than diagnostic labels.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Hayes's work gives a behavioural form to the contemplative idea that a thought can be noticed without being obeyed: ACT's authors present four of its processes as a workable behavioural definition of mindfulness, and describe the sense of self as observer as giving ordinary people a transcendent, spiritual side, while steering action by chosen values rather than by the wish to avoid discomfort.[^s2] Framed this way, the ideas can be measured and their effects studied.[^s2] In everyday terms, at work or at home, the same framing turns an anxious thought before a presentation from a verdict into a passing event, and moves attention to the next step that serves what one cares about.[^s2] ACT is a clinical treatment, not a self-help substitute; persistent distress is a reason to see a qualified professional.

## Common misreadings

- **Acceptance as resignation.** In ACT, acceptance applies to private experiences such as feelings and thoughts, and is paired with committed action to change what can be changed.[^s2]
- **Defusion as positive thinking.** Defusion does not try to replace a thought with a nicer one or prove it false; it changes the thought's grip on behaviour.[^s2]
- **ACT as proven superior to CBT.** Hayes and colleagues wrote in 2006 that there were not yet enough well-controlled studies to say ACT generally beats other active treatments.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The 2006 review pooled early trials and reported large effects against wait-list and usual-care conditions and moderate effects against active treatments, while calling the literature young, its samples small and its process measures mostly self-report.[^s2] In an updated meta-analysis of 60 randomised trials, Lars-Göran Öst found a small overall effect (about g = 0.42), no significant advantage over CBT, and weaker study methods than in comparable CBT trials; he judged ACT not yet well established for any disorder.[^s3] Hayes and colleagues answered that Öst's review contained many factual errors, used ratings that agreed poorly with independent raters, and should be set aside; they argued ACT is probably efficacious for several problems and well established for chronic pain.[^s4] The dispute is unresolved in its details, and the evidence for particular ACT techniques is weighed in their own articles. As a person entry, Hayes's evidence status is historical.

## Connections

Hayes **introduced** **acceptance and commitment therapy** and its **cognitive defusion** technique, and he developed **relational frame theory**; he also helped create **process-based therapy**.[^s1][^s2] He **authored** the 2006 review of ACT's model, processes and outcomes and co-authored the 2017 response to Öst.[^s2][^s4] His claims for ACT are **challenged by** Öst's updated meta-analysis.[^s3] He relates to **acceptance** and **cognitive fusion**, which ACT names as core processes; to **James Gross** and **regulatory flexibility**, a neighbouring account of flexible emotion regulation; and to **self-distancing**, another way of stepping back from one's own thoughts.[^s2]
