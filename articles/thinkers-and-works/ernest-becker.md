# Ernest Becker

Ernest Becker (1924–1974) was an American cultural anthropologist and writer whose book *The Denial of Death* (1973) argued that the knowledge that we will die, and our efforts to keep that knowledge at bay, shape much of what people strive for, defend and fight over.[^s1][^s4] The book won the 1974 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction, awarded two months after Becker died.[^s2][^s3] He is widely credited with turning the old philosophical theme of mortality into a working hypothesis about human motivation, the idea this graph files under **death awareness**.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Becker was born in Springfield, Massachusetts, to Jewish immigrant parents.[^s1] As an infantryman at the end of the Second World War he helped liberate Nazi concentration camps.[^s2] He took a PhD in cultural anthropology at Syracuse University in 1960; his dissertation became his first book, a critique of Zen.[^s1][^s2] His teaching career was unsettled: his appointment at the University of California, Berkeley was not renewed in the mid-1960s after he sided with student protesters, despite a petition signed by about 2,000 students, and he moved on to San Francisco State and then, in 1969, to Simon Fraser University in Vancouver.[^s2] He wrote some nine books, including *The Birth and Death of Meaning*, *The Structure of Evil* and *The Denial of Death*, and died of cancer in March 1974 at forty-nine; *Escape from Evil* appeared after his death in 1975.[^s1][^s2]

## Key ideas

- **The human problem.** Becker held that self-conscious creatures know they exist and know they will die, and that this double awareness brings both awe and terror.[^s2] Kept constantly in view it would be crippling, so it is pushed out of mind; in his words, consciousness of death is "the primary repression, not sexuality."[^s4]
- **Culture as a hero system.** Societies offer people roles through which to feel significant and lasting, from modest everyday heroics to grand ones, and self-esteem is the feeling of succeeding in them.[^s1][^s4] Nations, careers and scientific work can serve as symbolic immortality projects, while religion can offer literal immortality.[^s5]
- **Why it turns ugly.** When rival hero systems collide, the drive to feel immortal can feed aggrandisement, scapegoating and violence; *Escape from Evil* pursued this darker side.[^s2][^s4]
- **A less defended life.** Becker did not think denial could be dropped entirely, but he valued a wider horizon, a beyond that widens a person's horizon and frees them from narrow fixations, and he saw some forms of religion as doing this.[^s4] He drew on Otto Rank, Kierkegaard, Freud and existential philosophy, reinterpreting Freud's ideas around the fear of death rather than sexuality.[^s1][^s4]

## Why it matters

Becker gives a useful lens for a familiar failure of judgment: reacting to a threat to one's identity as if it were a threat to one's life. On his account, people cling hardest to the roles, beliefs and status that make them feel lasting, so criticism of a worldview or a life's work can feel existential.[^s4][^s5] At work, this helps explain why a leader may defend a failing flagship project long after the evidence turns, or why a team treats a rival department's way of working as an insult; in families and communities, why disagreements about values escalate so fast.[^s4] The wiser response his ideas point to is not bravado about death but noticing when the urge to feel significant is steering a decision, and choosing commitments that do not need an enemy.[^s4] Many wisdom traditions, from **memento mori** to Buddhist reflection on impermanence, make a related move by deliberately keeping mortality in view.

## Evidence and limits

Becker wrote as a theorist, not an experimenter. In 1986 the social psychologists Jeff Greenberg, Tom Pyszczynski and Sheldon Solomon recast his account in evolutionary and social-psychological terms as **terror management theory**, which predicts that reminders of death, known as mortality salience, will make people cling more to their worldviews and self-esteem.[^s5] A 2010 meta-analysis of 277 experiments found a moderate average effect (r = .35), larger in American and student samples.[^s6] But a 21-lab, preregistered replication of a classic study, with more than 2,200 participants, found little or no effect, whether or not the original authors advised on procedure, raising doubts about how robust the basic effect is.[^s7] A meta-analysis of death anxiety and religiosity found only a weak overall link and high heterogeneity, which its authors counted as bad news for death-centred accounts in general.[^s5] Critics also question Becker's habit of calling all worldviews illusions, and argue that motives besides fear of death, such as imitation and the need for social confirmation, shape what people believe.[^s4] As a person entry, his evidence status is historical; the empirical debate belongs to the theory he inspired. Reading Becker can stir up anxiety about death; that is a normal response to a hard subject, but persistent or overwhelming distress is a reason to talk to a doctor, counsellor or crisis line rather than to work through it alone.

## Connections

Becker **introduced** or, more exactly, popularised **death awareness** as an engine of human motivation in modern social science; the knowledge itself is as old as philosophy.[^s4][^s5] He **authored** *The Denial of Death*[^s3] and *Escape from Evil*,[^s1] and **influenced** **terror management theory**, whose founders built their model on his account of culture and religion.[^s5] He was **influenced by** **Otto Rank** and **Sigmund Freud**,[^s1] and drew on **Søren Kierkegaard** for the claim that self-awareness brings both awe and dread.[^s2][^s4] His ideas sit close to **mortality salience** and **worldview defense** research, to **existential concerns** and **existential psychotherapy**, and to **Viktor Frankl**'s focus on meaning in the face of death.[^s1][^s5]
