# Viktor E. Frankl

Viktor Emil Frankl (1905–1997) was a Viennese neurologist and psychiatrist who founded logotherapy, the "third school" of Viennese psychotherapy after Freud's and Adler's, built on the claim that the main human motive is the search for meaning.[^s1] He survived the Nazi camps, in which his father, mother, brother and wife died, and his short account of that experience, known in English as *Man's Search for Meaning*, sold millions of copies.[^s1][^s2] For readers interested in wisdom he matters twice over: as the source of a practical question about how to live through what cannot be changed, and as a case study in how an inspiring testimony can be stretched beyond what it shows.[^s4][^s6]

## Origin

Frankl was born in Vienna and studied medicine at the University of Vienna, where he was drawn to Alfred Adler's individual psychology and lectured on it; he qualified as a doctor in 1930.[^s1] In the 1930s he worked at the Am Steinhof psychiatric hospital, where from 1933 to 1937 he led a suicide-prevention programme for women, and after the 1938 annexation of Austria he headed neurology at the Rothschild Hospital, which served Vienna's Jewish community.[^s1] He named the philosophical basis of his work "existential analysis" in a 1939 paper and had drafted his first book, *The Doctor and the Soul*, by 1941.[^s2]

In September 1942 he was deported with his wife and parents to Theresienstadt, where his father died; in 1944 he was sent to Auschwitz-Birkenau and then to Kaufering and Türkheim, subcamps of Dachau, and was liberated in April 1945.[^s2] His mother and brother were murdered at Auschwitz, and his wife Tilly died at Bergen-Belsen.[^s2] The manuscript he carried into the camps was lost; he reconstructed and published it in 1946, the same year he dictated *Ein Psychologe erlebt das Konzentrationslager* ("A Psychologist Experiences the Concentration Camp") in nine days.[^s1][^s2] It appeared in the United States in 1959 as *From Death Camp to Existentialism* and later as *Man's Search for Meaning*.[^s2] He went on to direct the Vienna Neurological Policlinic for 25 years, became professor of neurology and psychiatry at the University of Vienna, earned a doctorate in philosophy in 1948 and taught widely in the United States, including at Harvard.[^s2]

## Key ideas

Frankl held that people are moved less by a will to pleasure or to power than by a need to find meaning, and that therapy should help them find it.[^s1] He called searching for meaning "the primary motivational force in man", and saw that search as healthy, spurring people toward new challenges.[^s4] He warned of a growing **existential vacuum**, an inner emptiness he believed modern people increasingly faced, and drew on his own camp observations to argue that a sense of meaning helps people survive even the worst conditions.[^s1][^s5] The practical core is a change of question: from what one wants from life to what a given situation asks of one, including the attitude one takes toward suffering that cannot be avoided (see **logotherapy**).[^s1]

## Why it matters

Frankl's work did much to make meaning a subject for psychology rather than only for philosophy and religion. Psychologists credit him among those who treated meaning in life as a basic human need, and the Purpose in Life Test, introduced in 1964 and since used in dozens of studies, was inspired by his approach.[^s5] Read wisely, his question is a tool for hard moments at work or at home: when a project collapses or a loss cannot be undone, asking what is still mine to create, care for or stand for keeps attention on what can be done.[^s1][^s4] It is a reflective lens, not treatment: persistent hopelessness or distress is a reason to seek professional or crisis support.

## Common misreadings

- **A famous line he probably did not write.** The saying that begins "Between stimulus and response there is a space" is widely attributed to Frankl, but the Viktor Frankl Institut describes its origin as involved: Stephen Covey said he found the idea in a library book whose author and title he did not note, and the Institut does not place it in Frankl's works.[^s3] Treat it as a paraphrase in Frankl's spirit, not his words.
- **Suffering as a recipe for meaning.** Frankl described finding meaning in unavoidable suffering; that is different from claiming that suffering is good, or that meaning guarantees survival.[^s1]
- **A study, not a testimony.** His remark that prisoners with a sense of meaning were more likely to survive was his own observation, not a controlled finding.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Later research supports Frankl's view that meaning matters but not every detail of his theory. Steger and colleagues found that searching for meaning and feeling that life is meaningful were only weakly and inversely related, and that the evidence fitted lack of meaning prompting search better than Frankl's expectation that searching leads to finding; searching was also linked to somewhat lower well-being.[^s4] Heintzelman and King, pooling large national and global surveys including a Gallup poll of 132 nations, concluded that most people rate their lives as meaningful, which sits uneasily with the picture of a spreading existential vacuum.[^s5] The historian Timothy Pytell, supporting the earlier critique of literary scholar Lawrence Langer, argued that Frankl shaped his account of Auschwitz to fit his psychological and philosophical theories, so it is better read as one survivor's testimony than as a general account of the camps.[^s6]

## Connections

Frankl **introduced** logotherapy and the idea of the **existential vacuum**, and he helped bring **meaning in life** into psychology; he **authored** *Man's Search for Meaning* and was **influenced by** **Alfred Adler**.[^s1][^s2][^s5] His claims are **challenged by** the 2008 study of the search for meaning and by Pytell's historical critique of his camp account.[^s4][^s6] He relates to **purpose in life**, **self-transcendence**, **existential psychotherapy** and **adversity**.[^s1][^s5]
