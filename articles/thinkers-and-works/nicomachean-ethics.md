# Nicomachean Ethics

The *Nicomachean Ethics* is the longer and better known of Aristotle's two treatises on how a human life goes well. Across ten books it asks what the highest human good is, answers that it is *eudaimonia* (happiness or flourishing), and then works through what that requires: virtues of character, practical wisdom, self-control, the right attitude to pleasure, and friends.[^s1] It is the founding text of Aristotelian virtue ethics and the main ancient source for the idea of practical wisdom (*phronesis*).[^s1]

## Origin

Aristotle did not give the book its title. The names of his two ethical works were added later, probably because the *Eudemian Ethics* was edited by his friend Eudemus and the *Nicomachean* by his son Nicomachus.[^s1] The two cover much the same ground; scholars usually take the *Nicomachean* to be the later, revised version, and three of its books (V–VII) are the same text as Books IV–VI of the *Eudemian*.[^s1] The book engages throughout with the views of Socrates and Plato, rejecting, for instance, Plato's view that virtue is simply a kind of knowledge.[^s1]

## What it argues

**Book I: the good life.** Every craft, inquiry and action aims at some good, and there must be a highest good sought for its own sake.[^s2] Aristotle's function argument concludes that the human good is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, and adds that this must hold over a complete life: one swallow, he says, "does not make a summer, nor does one day".[^s2] He warns that ethics allows only as much precision as its subject admits, and that the young, lacking experience of life, are poor students of it.[^s2] Happiness also needs some external goods, such as friends and resources, so luck plays a part.[^s1]

**Books II–V: character.** Virtues of character are stable dispositions formed by habit: we become just by doing just acts.[^s3] Each lies at a mean between excess and deficiency, relative to the person and the situation; courage, for example, sits between cowardice and rashness.[^s1] The aim of the inquiry is practical: we study ethics "not in order to know what virtue is, but in order to become good".[^s3] Books III–V then treat the particular virtues, ending with justice.[^s1]

**Book VI: practical wisdom.** Practical wisdom is the intellectual virtue that lets a good person see and reason towards what a particular situation calls for. Aristotle gives no rulebook, because circumstances vary too much, though he holds that some acts, like theft and murder, are always wrong.[^s1] It depends on good character, which trains the feelings that supply reasoning with its starting points.[^s1]

**Book VII: weakness of will and pleasure.** *Akrasia* is acting against one's own choice under the pull of passion. Aristotle separates the impetuous, who act before deliberating, from the weak, who deliberate and then fail to follow through.[^s1]

**Books VIII–IX: friendship.** Without friends, Aristotle says, no one would choose to live, even with every other good.[^s3] Friendships rest on usefulness, pleasure or goodness; the first two fade when the benefit does, while the complete kind, between good people who wish each other well for the other's own sake, is rare and takes time to build.[^s3][^s1]

**Book X: the best life.** Pleasure is a good but not the good. The life of contemplation is the happiest; the life of practical and civic virtue is happy in a secondary degree.[^s3][^s1] Because argument alone rarely makes people good, the book closes by turning to habituation, law and legislation.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The book offers a picture of wisdom as something lived rather than merely known. Faced with a hard choice at work, its questions are concrete: what does this situation, with these people, call for; which response sits between doing too little and too much; and what would an experienced, good person notice here?[^s1] Its claim that character grows from repeated small acts makes habit, not insight alone, the route to becoming patient, honest or brave.[^s3] Its friendship books give a usable test for relationships: does each of us wish the other well for their own sake, or only for what the other provides?[^s3]

## Common misreadings

The mean is not "moderation in all things": the right amount is set by the situation and can be large.[^s1] *Eudaimonia* is not a good mood but an activity sustained over a whole life, which is why the single swallow is not enough.[^s2] And the book is not a neutral manual: its ideal of the good life assumes leisure and resources, and Book X ranks contemplation above the life of civic virtue, which interpreters still debate.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

As a work of philosophy it is judged by argument and influence, not experiment. Modern researchers have drawn on it. A University of Birmingham team turned Book VI into a four-part model of practical wisdom (perceiving what matters, weighing conflicting considerations, a guiding picture of a good life, and emotion aligned with reason) and found that measures of it fitted samples of 285 adults and 207 teenagers, while describing the results as preliminary.[^s5] Its friendship ideal has been questioned: Kristjánsson argues that character friendships are less stable than Aristotle claims, because friends can develop in different directions and people often prefer confirmation of who they are to growth, and notes that longitudinal evidence on the friendship types is largely missing.[^s4]

## Connections

The book is **authored by** Aristotle and was **influenced by** Plato, whose views on virtue and the good it repeatedly engages and revises.[^s1] It sits under **Aristotelian virtue ethics**, and **introduces** the **function argument** and the ideal of **character friendship**.[^s2][^s3] Its accounts of **practical wisdom**, **theoretical wisdom**, **moral habituation**, the **doctrine of the mean**, **moral** and **intellectual virtues**, **deliberation** and **weakness of will** each have their own articles, and its idea of **eudaimonia** underlies the modern split between **hedonic and eudaimonic well-being**.[^s1] The **Aristotelian phronesis model** operationalises Book VI.[^s5] Kristjánsson's critique of friendship stability **challenges** Books VIII–IX.[^s4]
