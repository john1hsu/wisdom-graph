# Igor Grossmann

Igor Grossmann is a professor in the School of Psychology at the University of Waterloo in Canada, where he directs the Wisdom and Culture Lab.[^s1] His work asks how people make sense of a complicated social world and how culture and circumstance shape that sense-making, with wise reasoning, intellectual humility, perspective-taking and forecasting among his main topics.[^s1] More than any single theory, his contribution has been to make wisdom something researchers can observe in what people actually say and do, and to show how much it depends on the situation a person is in.[^s5][^s6]

## Origin

Grossmann took his PhD in psychology at the University of Michigan.[^s2] His early wisdom studies, with cultural psychologists including Richard Nisbett and Shinobu Kitayama, gave people stories about group and personal conflicts and asked them what would happen next; trained coders then scored the answers for wise reasoning.[^s3][^s4] His lab shares its coding manual and a situation-sensitive questionnaire, the Situated Wise Reasoning Scale, so that others could use the same measures.[^s10] He has received the American Psychological Foundation's Gittler Award, a SAGE Young Scholar Award from the Society for Personality and Social Psychology and a Rising Star award from the Association for Psychological Science, and co-hosts the *On Wisdom* podcast.[^s2]

## Why it matters

**Wisdom can grow with age, but culture shapes when.** A 2010 *PNAS* study of a community sample found that older adults more often than younger and middle-aged adults considered several perspectives, allowed for compromise and admitted the limits of what they knew, even though their fluid intelligence was lower.[^s4] A 2012 follow-up compared 225 Americans and 186 Japanese aged 25 to 75. In the American sample wiser reasoning rose with age, in the Japanese sample it did not, and younger Japanese adults already scored higher than younger Americans; controlling for education, occupational prestige and knowledge-based intelligence left the American age pattern intact.[^s3] The authors suggested that the two cultures expose people to conflict, and ways of handling it, at different points in life.[^s3]

**Wisdom is a state as much as a trait.** In daily-diary research, how wisely a person reasoned on one day predicted the next day only weakly, and variation within the same person was at least as large as variation between people.[^s5] With colleagues he proposed treating trait wisdom as a typical spread of wiser and less wise moments rather than a fixed endowment.[^s6] The same review collected evidence that people reason less wisely about their own conflicts than a friend's, the asymmetry studied with Ethan Kross as Solomon's paradox, and that adopting an outside observer's view narrows that gap.[^s5]

**A common core for the field.** Grossmann was lead author of a 2020 consensus paper, drawn from a July 2019 meeting of wisdom researchers in Toronto and a survey of scientists, which concluded that empirical approaches converge on wisdom as morally grounded metacognition applied to reasoning and problem-solving.[^s8] His group has also shown that people separate being *rational*, in the sense of logic and self-interest, from being *reasonable*, balancing self-interest with fairness, and prefer a reasonable negotiator on the other side of a dispute.[^s9][^s1]

**Humility about prediction.** In tournaments run by the Forecasting Collaborative, whose report he led as first author, 86 and then 120 teams of social scientists forecast trends such as polarization, life satisfaction and gender and racial bias. On average their forecasts did no better than simple statistical models or a sample of 802 lay people; teams did better when they had domain expertise, used simpler models and leaned on past data.[^s7] The finding sits naturally beside his wisdom work: recognising uncertainty and the limits of knowledge is a core element of wise reasoning.[^s5]

## Applying his ideas

Because wisdom shifts with circumstance, Grossmann's research points to arranging circumstances rather than only hoping for better character. His review lists ego-decentring strategies tested in experiments, such as thinking about a problem in the third person, imagining it as a distant observer or from a future vantage point, and more speculative supports such as advisory roles, reminders and if-then plans.[^s5] His 2021 distanced self-reflection diary method turned this idea into a training technique.[^s1] At work, the forecasting results suggest a habit before any confident prediction: check what the simple base rate says, and ask whether you actually have expertise in this domain.[^s7]

## Evidence and limits

Grossmann has flagged the gaps himself: most ego-decentring evidence comes from single-session experiments, and how long effects last is unknown.[^s5] The culture-and-age findings come from cross-sectional samples in two countries, and the proposed mechanism, differing experience of conflict, was not directly tested.[^s3] The 2020 consensus paper also critiques current wisdom measures and lists unresolved conceptual and methodological problems.[^s8] His lab's response has been to build measures that capture wisdom in specific situations rather than as a fixed score.[^s10]

## Connections

Grossmann **introduced** the **distanced self-reflection diary**, a training method for wise reasoning.[^s1] With Kung and Santos he **introduced** a **situated** view of wisdom in which wise and unwise states vary around a person's typical level.[^s6] His lab **introduced** the **Situated Wise Reasoning Scale**.[^s10] He is the researcher behind much of the evidence on **wise reasoning** and **Solomon's paradox**, worked with **Ethan Kross** on **self-distancing**, and helped frame the **common wisdom model** in the **psychology of wisdom**.[^s5][^s8] His study with Japanese and American adults is the **aging and wisdom culture study**, and **intellectual humility** is one of his lab's core research topics.[^s1][^s3]
