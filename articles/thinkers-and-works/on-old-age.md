# Cato Maior de Senectute

*Cato Maior de Senectute*, usually translated *On Old Age*, is a short philosophical dialogue by the Roman statesman Marcus Tullius Cicero. In it the 84-year-old Cato the Elder, a byword for Roman toughness, explains to two younger friends, Scipio and Laelius, why he does not find his age a burden.[^s1][^s2] Cicero dedicated it to his friend Atticus, saying he wrote it to lighten the old age they were both entering, and that writing it had already made his own lighter.[^s1] For centuries it has been a major reference point in Western thinking about ageing well.[^s4]

## Origin

Cicero wrote the dialogue around the turn of 45–44 BCE, when Caesar's dominance had pushed him out of public life and the death of his daughter Tullia had driven him to philosophy for consolation.[^s2] It belongs to an extraordinary two-year burst of late writing, and the Stanford Encyclopedia pairs it with *On Friendship* as two works on specific questions in ethics.[^s3] The dramatic date is 150 BCE.[^s2] Cicero drew on earlier Greek writing: he mentions a treatise on old age by Aristo of Ceos, borrows passages from Xenophon, and reworks the opening of Plato's *Republic*, where the old man Cephalus tells Socrates that how people bear age depends on their character.[^s2] As in his other philosophical works, he writes as an Academic sceptic, setting out a plausible view for readers to weigh rather than a proof.[^s3]

## Key ideas

**The four complaints.** Cato names the reasons old age is thought wretched: it withdraws us from active pursuits, weakens the body, deprives us of nearly all physical pleasures, and is not far from death (§15). He answers each in turn.[^s1]

**Character, not age.** Before the complaints, Cato argues that people who grumble about old age were usually hard to live with at every age: "the blame rests with character, not with age" (§7).[^s1] Later he says the same of old people who are sour, anxious and irritable (§65).[^s1]

**Counsel replaces strength.** The old pilot sits quietly at the stern while younger sailors climb the masts, yet the voyage depends on him; great things, Cato says, are done by reflection, character and judgement rather than by bodily speed (§17).[^s1] He adds that old people keep their mental powers as long as their interest and effort continue (§22), and he himself took up Greek late in life (§26).[^s1]

**Resist, within limits.** Age should be fought like an illness, with moderate exercise, sensible eating and, above all, by keeping the mind supplied like a lamp with oil (§35–36). Use the strength you have, and do not mourn the strength you had.[^s1]

**Planting for others.** An old farmer plants trees that will serve another generation, passing on what he himself was given (§24–25).[^s1]

**A good end to a good play.** A well-lived life is like a drama played skilfully to its last act; even a short life is long enough to live honourably, and death in old age comes as naturally as ripe fruit falling (§70–71).[^s1] The well-respected old age that Cato praises, with its authority as a "crown", rests on foundations laid in youth (§61–62).[^s1]

## Why it matters

The dialogue offers a usable stance for anyone growing older, or working beside people who are: count what age adds as well as what it subtracts, shift effort from strength to judgement, keep learning, and give your time to things that will outlast you.[^s1] At work it supports a role for experienced people as advisers and mentors rather than as people to be retired quietly.[^s1] Gerontologists still open discussions of healthy ageing with Cicero, as an early voice treating later life as a chance for growth rather than only decline.[^s5]

In practice, readers use it by asking which of the four complaints actually applies to them, and which is a fear borrowed from stereotypes; by naming one area where their judgement now counts for more than their speed; and by choosing a "tree" to plant for others. These are the questions Cato puts to his own life (§15, §17, §24–25).[^s1] A real change in health, though, is a matter for a doctor rather than for philosophy.

## Common misreadings

The text is sometimes read as proof that Cicero foresaw modern findings on ageing, and sometimes dismissed as a wealthy Roman man's rosy picture of a privileged old age. Jedan argues both readings miss its genre: it is chiefly a consolation, written to help people bear real losses, and so it is more realistic about decline than it first seems.[^s4] Cato's serene confidence is also a literary portrait, not a record of what the historical Cato said; Cicero chose him to give the arguments weight (§3).[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

*On Old Age* is a philosophical and consolatory work, not a study, and its advice about character, exercise and mental activity was not tested by Cicero. Its argument also assumes resources, status and health that many older people lack, a point its critics press.[^s4] Jedan suggests that its idea of wellbeing adapted to age is a corrective to modern "successful ageing" models that can treat decline as failure.[^s4] Modern epidemiology defines healthy ageing through many measurable domains, physical, mental and social, which goes well beyond what Cicero claimed.[^s5]

## Connections

The work is **authored by** Cicero and voiced through Cato the Elder.[^s1] It **introduces** a long tradition of reflection on **ageing** that gerontologists still cite; it did not invent the subject, which earlier Greek writers had treated.[^s5][^s2] It is **influenced by** Plato, whose Cephalus scene it reworks.[^s2] It belongs to **consolation literature** and **contrasts with** modern **successful ageing** models.[^s4] Its themes of judgement in later life, facing death calmly and planting for the future connect it to **wisdom and age**, **death awareness**, **memento mori** and **stewardship**.[^s1]
