# The Theory of Moral Sentiments

*The Theory of Moral Sentiments* is Adam Smith's first book, published in London in 1759 and revised until its sixth edition of 1790.[^s7] Long before *The Wealth of Nations* made him famous as an economist, Smith set out here an account of how ordinary people come to judge what is fitting, admirable or blameworthy. His answer rests on two ideas that still shape how we think about wise judgment: sympathy, which is our capacity to imagine ourselves into another person's situation, and the impartial spectator, an imagined fair-minded observer whose view we learn to take of our own conduct.[^s3]

## Origin

The book is a comprehensive and unusual version of Scottish moral sentimentalism.[^s3] Smith took ideas from his teacher Francis Hutcheson and, above all, from David Hume, whose thought his own circles around.[^s3] Where Hume pictured feelings passing from one person to another almost by contagion, Smith made sympathy *projective*: we imagine how we would feel in the other's circumstances, which leaves a gap between their feelings and ours.[^s3] The Advertisement to the 1790 edition says that the sixth Part, on the character of virtue, was entirely new.[^s7]

## Key ideas

**Sympathy as imagined exchange.** Smith opens by noting that our senses never carry us beyond our own person, so we cannot directly feel what someone else feels.[^s2] We form an idea of it, he argues, "by changing places in fancy with the sufferer", imagining ourselves in their situation.[^s2] Because the imagined feeling is fainter than the real one, the person affected learns to lower their display to a pitch others can go along with, and spectators learn to enter more fully into it. That mutual adjustment is where Smith locates the everyday virtues of restraint and humanity.[^s3]

**The impartial spectator.** We judge others by asking whether we could go along with their feelings. To judge ourselves, Smith says, we must try to see our conduct as a fair and well-informed stranger would.[^s1] He describes this as splitting oneself into a judge and the person judged: "I divide myself, as it were, into two persons."[^s1] Over time this imagined spectator, the inner judge Smith calls the man within the breast, becomes our conscience, and can even let a person stand apart from and criticise the views of their own society.[^s1][^s3]

**Self-deceit and general rules.** Smith is frank about how badly we judge ourselves. Before we act, passion distorts what we see; afterwards, we tend to look away from an unflattering verdict on what we did.[^s1] He calls self-deceit the source of half the disorders of human life.[^s1] His remedy is the general rules of conduct we form, in calmer moments, from watching how others' actions strike us; fixed in the mind, they check us when anger or self-love makes an act seem justified.[^s1]

**Self-command and the virtues.** Smith distinguishes the *amiable* virtues of humanity and compassion from the *awful* virtues of self-command, and in the last edition added a full treatment of prudence, benevolence and self-command.[^s3] Prudence, for him, joins foresight about consequences with the self-command to forgo a present pleasure for a greater good later.[^s7]

## Why it matters

The book maps two moves that modern research treats as central to wise reasoning: taking another's perspective and stepping back from our own. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on empathy credits Hume and Smith with putting these capacities at the centre of moral life, and notes that the impartial spectator was meant as a correction for sympathy's narrow reach.[^s4] Smith's claim that we are fairer judges of others than of ourselves anticipates what psychologists now call Solomon's paradox: across three experiments with 693 adults, people reasoned more wisely about a friend's relationship conflict than about their own, and taking a distanced, observer's view of their own problem removed the gap.[^s5] The study did not set out to test Smith, but it fits his picture of the inner spectator.[^s5][^s1]

In practice: before a heated reply to a colleague, ask how a fair, well-informed observer who knows both of you would see it. After a decision has gone wrong, review it in that observer's voice instead of building a case for yourself. In a calm moment, set yourself a few rules for the situations where you know your judgment slips.[^s1]

## Common misreadings

Smith's "sympathy" is not pity. It covers fellow-feeling with any passion, including another's beliefs and joys, much like our broad modern word *empathy*.[^s4] Nor is the impartial spectator simply public opinion; Smith treats it as an ideal observer we reach by correcting the partial views of the people around us.[^s3] Critics point out that it is still formed from local attitudes and may inherit a society's prejudices, and that the book gives no procedure for settling a particular hard case. Smith held that philosophy should deepen ordinary judgment rather than replace it.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The book is a work of philosophy, judged by argument and influence rather than experiment. Modern research has tested one of its working assumptions, that imagining another's situation reliably stirs fellow-feeling. A meta-analysis found that instructions to imagine how someone feels did not significantly raise empathic concern compared with no instructions, while instructions to stay objective lowered it.[^s6] Smith himself stressed that imagined feeling is weaker than real feeling.[^s3] Self-distancing research, by contrast, gives some support to the value of an observer's view of one's own troubles.[^s5]

## Connections

The book is **authored by** Adam Smith and was **influenced by** David Hume, whose contagion model of sympathy Smith reworked, and by his teacher Francis Hutcheson.[^s3] It sits under the broader heading of **moral sentimentalism**, and its virtue-centred approach relates it to **virtue ethics**.[^s3] Its account of imagined exchange is an early philosophical account of what is now called **perspective-taking**, and of **empathy** in the broad sense.[^s2][^s4] The impartial spectator relates it to **self-distancing** and the **view from above**, and its treatment of self-deceit to **self-deception**.[^s1] Solomon's paradox research lends it modest **support**; the perspective-taking meta-analysis **challenges** it.[^s5][^s6]
