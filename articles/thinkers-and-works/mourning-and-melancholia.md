# Mourning and Melancholia

*Mourning and Melancholia* is a short essay by Sigmund Freud, written in 1915 and published in 1917, that sets ordinary mourning beside melancholia, the older name for severe depression.[^s1] Both can follow the loss of a loved person, or of something that stood in for one, such as a country or an ideal, and both bring painful low mood, loss of interest in the world and an inability to love anew.[^s1] Freud's question was why the same loss leaves most people grieving and a few people ill. His answer gave psychology its first major theory of grief, and the idea of "grief work" that shaped bereavement theory and counselling for decades afterwards.[^s5][^s4][^s3]

## Origin

Freud wrote the essay in 1915, two years before it appeared in print. He was candid that it rested on a small number of clinical cases, and warned that its conclusions could not claim general validity.[^s1] Later he revised his own account: in his war writings and in *The Ego and the Id* (1923) he came to see identification with the lost person, which in 1917 he had tied to melancholia, as a normal part of mourning, which implies that ordinary grieving never fully ends.[^s2]

## Key ideas

**The work of mourning.** When reality shows that a loved person is gone, the mind is asked to withdraw its attachment from them. Freud observed that people resist this, and that it happens only bit by bit, memory by memory, at great cost of time and energy, which is why mourning hurts so much. When the work is done, he wrote, the ego becomes free again to attach to someone new.[^s1]

**Grief is not an illness.** Freud stressed that, painful as mourning is, we do not treat it as a disorder, expect it to pass with time and regard interfering with it as useless or even harmful.[^s1]

**Where mourning and melancholia part.** The difference lies in self-regard. "In mourning it is the world which has become poor and empty," Freud wrote; in melancholia it is the self.[^s1] The melancholic person reproaches themselves harshly. Freud read many of these accusations as anger at the lost person, mixed with love and turned inward, and argued that this ambivalence is what makes some losses so hard to resolve.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The essay offers a useful distinction for anyone living through a loss or supporting someone who is: sorrow at an emptier world is the normal price of having loved, while a collapse of self-worth and relentless self-blame is a different matter.[^s1] It also explains why grief takes time and resists being hurried, and why tangled feelings about the person who died, unspoken resentment alongside love, can make mourning harder.[^s1]

In practice: allow grief its time rather than treating it as a problem to fix; notice whether self-reproach, rather than missing the person, has come to dominate; and give mixed feelings about the dead room to be spoken.[^s1] Bereavement research also finds that people grieve along different paths, so there is no single right pace.[^s5] Persistent distress, hopelessness or loss of functioning are reasons to seek professional or crisis support, not tests of character.

## Common misreadings

Many people still take from the Freudian tradition the belief that one must "let go" and "move on" quickly. Hall notes that the grief-work model came to stress moving on as fast as possible, and that Freud himself stayed strongly attached to his daughter decades after her death.[^s5] Freud's later writings complicate that reading too, treating the lost person's lasting trace in the self as part of normal grief.[^s2] The more confrontational picture of grief work, as reliving and working through the loss, was elaborated by later clinicians such as Erich Lindemann and Colin Murray Parkes.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The essay is a clinical and theoretical work, not a study, and modern research has tested its central legacy. In a review of the grief work hypothesis, Margaret Stroebe found its definitions and measures muddled, the few tests of it inconclusive, and the idea far from universal across cultures.[^s3] Stroebe and Henk Schut later wrote that there is "hardly a single study, let alone a body of evidence" showing that working through grief improves adjustment, and that some cultures cope well by controlling grief's expression.[^s4] Research on continuing bonds suggests that a healthy, inward connection with the deceased can persist, and a prospective study of widowed spouses found stable low distress the most common pattern, with delayed grief rare.[^s5]

## Connections

The essay is **authored by** Sigmund Freud and belongs to **psychoanalysis**.[^s1] It **introduces** the idea of **grief work** and is widely treated as the start of modern theory about **grief and bereavement**.[^s1][^s5] Its emphasis on detachment is **challenged by** Stroebe's **review of the grief work hypothesis** and by research on **continuing bonds**.[^s3][^s5] The **dual process model of bereavement**, which pictures grieving people moving between facing the loss and rebuilding daily life, offers a more flexible alternative to the one-way work of detachment, and later research on **prolonged grief disorder** and on the limits of the **five stages of grief** belongs to the same move beyond older grief models.[^s5] Stroebe and Schut also point to the unclear line between grief work and **rumination**.[^s4]
