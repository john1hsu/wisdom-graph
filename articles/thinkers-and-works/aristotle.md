# Aristotle

Aristotle was a Greek philosopher whose roughly thirty-one surviving works range across logic, metaphysics, the mind, ethics, politics, rhetoric, poetics and empirical biology.[^s1] For anyone interested in wisdom, his lasting contribution is a distinction and a portrait: the distinction between theoretical wisdom about unchanging truths and practical wisdom (*phronesis*) about how to act, and a portrait of the practically wise person as someone whose good character and long experience let them see what a particular situation calls for.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

He was born in 384 BCE in Stagira, in Macedonian northern Greece; ancient biography reports that his father, Nicomachus, was physician to the Macedonian king, though the main ancient source is of questionable reliability.[^s1][^s3] At about seventeen he joined Plato's Academy in Athens and stayed some twenty years, until Plato's death in 347.[^s1] He then tutored the young Alexander of Macedon from 343 (how much that shaped Alexander is unknown), returned to Athens in 335 to found his own school, the Lyceum, and left again in 323, dying at Chalcis in 322.[^s1][^s3]

Much of what survives reads like lecture notes rather than finished books; his polished works for a general audience are almost entirely lost.[^s1] His ethics comes down to us mainly in the *Nicomachean* and *Eudemian Ethics*.[^s2]

## Why it matters

**Wisdom begins with what people already think.** His method starts from appearances and reputable opinions (*endoxa*), sets out the puzzles they raise, and keeps as much of the common view as survives scrutiny: take seriously what thoughtful people believe before deciding they are wrong.[^s1]

**A good life is something you do.** He argued that the highest human good, *eudaimonia* (usually translated happiness or flourishing), must be complete and sought for its own sake, and that it consists in rational activity carried out excellently over a whole life, not in a pleasant feeling or in honours.[^s1][^s2] He also admitted that external goods and luck matter, so severe misfortune can mar even a virtuous life.[^s2][^s3]

**Character is built by practice.** Ethical virtue, on his account, is a settled disposition to feel and act appropriately, formed by habit; in Ross's translation, "we become just by doing just acts", as builders become builders by building.[^s5] Each virtue lies at a mean between excess and deficiency, relative to the person and the situation rather than at an arithmetic midpoint.[^s2][^s3]

**Practical wisdom ties it together.** Book VI defines practical wisdom as "a reasoned and true state of capacity to act with regard to human goods".[^s4] Because it deals with particulars known through experience, he observes that the young can be good mathematicians but are rarely practically wise.[^s4] It differs from mere cleverness, the knack of reaching whatever goal one sets, which becomes smartness in the service of a bad aim.[^s4] And it works in partnership with character: in strict terms one cannot be fully good without practical wisdom, nor practically wise without ethical virtue; virtue sets the right goal and practical wisdom finds the way to it.[^s4][^s2]

## Using his ideas today

Aristotle offers no decision procedure; circumstances vary too much for rules to settle every case, though some acts are always wrong.[^s2] What he offers instead is a set of questions. Before a hard call at work, ask what someone you regard as both good and experienced would notice here that you might miss.[^s4] When building a habit such as candour or patience, start with small repeated acts, since character grows from them.[^s5] When a choice tempts you toward an extreme (avoiding a difficult conversation, or going in too hard), look for the response fitted to this person and this moment.[^s2] In relationships, his test of the fullest friendship still applies: do we wish each other well for the other's own sake, not only for use or pleasure?[^s2]

## Common misreadings

The line "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit" is widely credited to Aristotle, but it comes from Will Durant's 1926 *The Story of Philosophy*, as Durant's own paraphrase.[^s7] The genuine idea is in *Nicomachean Ethics* II.1, where moral virtue is said to come about as a result of habit.[^s5][^s7] The mean is also often misread as "moderation in all things": it is a target set by the situation, which can call for strong anger or great generosity.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Aristotle's ethics is philosophy, judged by argument rather than experiment, and he addressed it to readers who had already been well brought up.[^s2] Psychologists now draw on it: Darnell and colleagues propose that phronesis has four functions (seeing what matters ethically, weighing competing considerations, holding a blueprint of a good life, and aligning emotion with reason), to explain why moral reasoning alone predicts behaviour only modestly.[^s6] They present it as a conceptual proposal still awaiting measurement, note that Aristotle says little about how practical wisdom develops, and judge his thesis that the virtues stand or fall together empirically unrealistic, since people's virtues tend to be uneven.[^s6]

Some of his views are now rejected outright. He defended slavery as natural for some people and placed men over women in the household, positions that modern readers of his ethics set aside.[^s3]

## Legacy

Arabic philosophers called him "the First Teacher", Avicenna and Averroes wrote major commentaries, and Thomas Aquinas worked to reconcile him with Christian thought; today his influence is most visible in the revival of virtue ethics.[^s1]

## Connections

Aristotle was **influenced by** **Plato**, his teacher of twenty years, while rejecting the theory of Forms and the assumption that "good" has a single meaning.[^s1] He **introduced** **Aristotelian virtue ethics**, the account of character, the mean and practical wisdom in his ethical treatises.[^s2] He gave **practical wisdom** and the **doctrine of the mean** their classic form in the ***Nicomachean Ethics***, separating practical wisdom from **theoretical wisdom** and **cleverness**; modern **virtue ethics** and research on **hedonic and eudaimonic well-being** draw on his account of **eudaimonia**.[^s1][^s2][^s4] His distinction between impetuosity and weakness, and his partial agreement with **Socrates** about knowing wrongdoing, shape **weakness of will** and **deliberation**.[^s2] The **Aristotelian phronesis model** carries his ideas into moral psychology.[^s6]
