# Richard S. Lazarus

Richard S. Lazarus (1922–2002) was an American psychologist who spent most of his career at the University of California, Berkeley, and is best known for the idea that stress and emotion arise less from events themselves than from how a person appraises them.[^s1] He described emotions as reflecting what is happening to one's goals, and his appraisal concept became one of the main rationales for cognitive-behaviour therapy.[^s1] For anyone interested in wisdom, his work gave a scientific shape to an old observation: the same setback can crush one person and steady another, depending on what they take it to mean and what they believe they can do about it.[^s2]

## Origin

Lazarus was born in New York City, graduated from the City College of New York in 1942, served roughly three and a half years in the US Army and took his doctorate at the University of Pittsburgh in the late 1940s.[^s1] He taught at Johns Hopkins and Clark University before joining Berkeley in 1957, where he stayed until he became emeritus in 1991.[^s1] He recalled that when he began, stress and emotion drew little interest in psychology outside military research.[^s1] His memorial notice traces the appraisal idea to the psychologist Magda Arnold and, much earlier, to Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*.[^s1]

From the late 1950s his laboratory showed people distressing films, such as a subincision ritual and workshop accidents, while recording heart rate, skin conductance and self-reports.[^s2] Soundtracks or introductions that invited denial or a detached, intellectual view of the film markedly lowered reactions, while a passage stressing threat raised them; Lazarus concluded that appraisal, not the stressor alone, drives the stress response.[^s2] A 1972 study from his group went a step further and asked viewers to detach or involve themselves using appraisals of their own making rather than ones supplied by the experimenter.[^s3]

## Key ideas

**Appraisal.** Appraisal is the continuing judgement of what an encounter means for one's well-being, negotiating between the demands of the situation and a person's goals, beliefs and resources.[^s2] With Susan Folkman he separated **primary appraisal**, asking whether anything one cares about is at stake, from **secondary appraisal**, asking what can be done: whether the situation can be changed, must be accepted, or calls for holding back.[^s4]

**Coping.** He treated coping as a process, not a fixed trait: ongoing efforts in thought and action to manage demands judged to exceed one's resources.[^s2] **Problem-focused coping** changes the situation; **emotion-focused coping** changes how one attends to or interprets it, and reappraising a threat in non-threatening terms removes the cognitive basis of the stress reaction.[^s2] Folkman and Lazarus listed distancing and positive reappraisal, finding growth or benefit in an experience, among the emotion-focused forms.[^s4] Later work on emotion regulation defined **cognitive reappraisal** as construing a situation so that its emotional impact changes, citing Lazarus and Alfert's 1964 film study as its origin.[^s5]

**From stress to emotion.** Late in his career he argued that stress is best studied as part of the wider field of emotions, each with a "core relational theme": anger, for example, follows a demeaning offence against oneself or one's own, and sadness follows an irrevocable loss.[^s2] He also argued that daily hassles often cause more suffering than major life events.[^s1] His books include *Psychological Stress and the Coping Process* (1966), *Stress, Appraisal, and Coping* (1984, with Folkman), *Emotion and Adaptation* (1991) and *Stress and Emotion* (1999).[^s1]

## Why it matters

Lazarus's framework describes, in testable terms, what many wisdom traditions ask of people facing hardship: notice how you are reading the situation, sort what can be changed from what must be borne, and act on the first while finding a truer way to hold the second.[^s2][^s4] It explains why two partners in the same argument can feel different emotions, one anger at being demeaned and the other anxiety about the relationship, because each appraises it differently.[^s2] At work, it explains why a missed target can feel like a threat to one person and a challenge to another.[^s2] His framework is a research model, not therapy; persistent or overwhelming distress is a reason to seek professional support.

## Evidence and limits

The 1972 study is a useful caution for anyone using reappraisal: viewers told to detach reported feeling considerably less aroused, yet their heart rate and skin conductance did not fall, perhaps because the effort of detaching was itself arousing.[^s3] Imagining that the accident was happening to oneself, by contrast, raised heart-rate reactions.[^s3] In a study of community adults, appraisal and coping were more variable than stable across encounters and, together with personality, predicted psychological symptoms but not somatic health.[^s4] His claim that appraisal is a necessary precondition of emotion was **challenged** by Robert Zajonc, who argued that affect can arise without prior cognition; Robert Levenson's retrospective says the debate ended with a healthy helping of "both", while calling appraisal theory a keystone of modern affective science.[^s6] As a person entry, Lazarus's evidence status is historical.

## Connections

Lazarus **introduced** **cognitive reappraisal**, credited to his 1964 work with Alfert, as well as **cognitive appraisal theory** and, with Folkman, the **transactional model of stress and coping** with its primary and secondary appraisals.[^s2][^s4][^s5] He **authored** *Stress, Appraisal, and Coping* and *Emotion and Adaptation*, and he was **influenced by** **Magda Arnold**'s appraisal idea.[^s1] His view that appraisal must come before emotion was **challenged by** **Robert Zajonc**.[^s6] He relates to **James Gross**, whose **process model of emotion regulation** built on his work; to **emotion regulation**, **coping**, **adversity** and **resilience**; to the **dichotomy of control** and **self-distancing**, which echo secondary appraisal and detachment; and to **compassionate reappraisal**, a later variant of his core idea.[^s2][^s5]
