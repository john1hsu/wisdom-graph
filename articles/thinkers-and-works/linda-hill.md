# Linda A. Hill

Linda A. Hill is the Wallace Brett Donham Professor of Business Administration at Harvard Business School and faculty chair of its Leadership Initiative.[^s4] She is best known for two bodies of work: a close study of what happens to people in their first year as managers, and later research on how leaders draw innovation out of groups rather than supplying it themselves.[^s1][^s5] For a reader interested in wisdom, her work matters because it describes the moment when a capable individual gains power over others, and the misjudgements about authority, control and self-reliance that make that moment harder than it needs to be.[^s1]

## Origin

Hill's original research followed 19 people through their first year as managers, and she went on studying new managers for roughly fifteen years through case studies and leadership programmes for companies and non-profits.[^s1] The first edition of *Becoming a Manager: How New Managers Master the Challenges of Leadership* appeared in 1992, with a second edition in 2003.[^s1][^s3] The book follows its managers through that first year: they began by drawing on what they had seen of their own bosses and on their time as individual contributors, then had to reframe what a manager does as the work itself taught them otherwise.[^s6] Its later parts cover building a managerial identity, gaining authority, managing stress and self-knowledge, and dispelling the myths of management.[^s6]

With Kent Lineback she later wrote *Being the Boss: The 3 Imperatives for Becoming a Great Leader*, which turned the research into guidance for managers at any stage.[^s2] Her innovation research, published as the co-authored *Collective Genius: The Art and Practice of Leading Innovation* (2014) and *Genius at Scale* (2026), has twice placed her among the Thinkers50 top ten management thinkers.[^s4][^s5]

## Key ideas

**Becoming a manager is a transformation, not a promotion.** Hill found that people learn to lead mostly by trial and error on the job, often through adverse experiences, and that the shift from doing the work to getting it done through others requires a new professional identity, which is psychologically taxing.[^s1]

**The myths of the role.** In her 2007 *Harvard Business Review* summary, Hill set out the misconceptions her new managers brought with them and what they found instead.[^s1] Managers expect freedom but find themselves bound by bosses, peers and outside parties with conflicting demands. They expect authority to come from the position, but it comes from credibility. They expect to control their reports, but compliance does not produce the initiative good work needs. They try to manage each person one to one, when they must lead the team as a whole. And they think the job is keeping things running, when it is also changing what is not working.[^s1]

**Credibility rests on character and competence.** Hill described credibility as built from character (showing good intentions, which reports watch closely), competence (knowing how to do the right thing, not just technical skill) and influence (delivering results).[^s1] Her short summary of the control myth was "compliance does not equal commitment."[^s1]

**Three imperatives.** *Being the Boss* reorganised these lessons as managing yourself, managing your network and managing your team.[^s2] Hill warned against leaning on formal authority or fear, against assuming the boss alone is responsible for the relationship, and against reading silence as agreement, and she argued that many managers who never make the psychological shift from individual contributor get stuck or derail.[^s2]

**Leading innovation.** In *Collective Genius*, Hill and her co-authors studied 16 leaders in organisations known for innovation and concluded that innovation is a team effort; the leaders' job was less to be the visionary than to ask questions, let competing ideas develop before combining them, and judge when to step in.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Much of what Hill found reads as practical wisdom under a management heading. Her new managers seldom asked for help, partly because they believed a boss should have the answers and partly because they feared being judged; about half eventually turned to their own bosses, often in a crisis, and many found them more tolerant than expected.[^s1] She recommended seeking peers outside one's own function or organisation as advisers.[^s1] Her accent on character, on the limits of formal power and on hearing other people's views echoes the cautions about power and advice that run through this graph's articles on leading others and humble leadership.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Hill's foundational study was qualitative and small, following 19 managers.[^s1] Later researchers treat it as a cornerstone of the literature on managerial transitions: Park and Faerman cite her for the finding that new managers often feel disoriented, isolated, stressed or uncertain, that the first transition is critical to later success, and that it changes how people measure their own success.[^s3] Their five-wave interview study of 16 newly promoted public-sector managers found a comparable developmental passage and added that learning emotional and social competence is central to it, which suggests her findings carry beyond the private firms she first studied, though both studies are small and interview-based.[^s3] Her popular books are practitioner guidance rather than tested interventions.

## Connections

Hill **introduced** the research study of becoming a manager, the transition this graph treats under **leading others**; later work positions her among the founders of the managerial-transition literature.[^s3] She **authored** *Becoming a Manager*,[^s6] *Being the Boss* (with Kent Lineback)[^s2] and, as co-author, *Collective Genius*.[^s4] Her account of credibility built on character relates to **honesty**, and her warning against relying on title to **hubris**.[^s1] Her finding that people learn to manage by trial and error ties her work to **learning from experience**, and her observation that new managers avoid asking for help ties it to **seeking counsel**.[^s1] Her picture of the leader as someone who draws out others' ideas rather than supplying them overlaps with **humble leadership** and **wise leadership**.[^s5] The study of these questions belongs to **organizational behavior**.[^s3]
