# De Ira

*De Ira* (*On Anger*) is a treatise in three books by the Roman Stoic philosopher Lucius Annaeus Seneca. It opens with his brother Novatus asking him to write about how anger may be soothed, and it answers with a diagnosis of what anger is, a refutation of the case for keeping some of it, and a long manual of remedies.[^s1] It is the most detailed ancient analysis of a single emotion, and the classic Western text for anyone who wants to judge well while provoked.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Seneca (c. 1 BCE – 65 CE) was born in Corduba in Spain, exiled to Corsica in 41, later tutor and then adviser to the emperor Nero, and finally forced to take his own life after being implicated in a conspiracy.[^s2] Graver counts *De Ira* among his early works.[^s3] He nearly died under the emperor Caligula, and the treatise draws many of its cautionary tales from that reign and from other rulers, such as Cambyses of Persia, whose rage destroyed others.[^s4][^s1] It belongs to the Stoic tradition, which treated destructive emotions as errors of judgment that philosophy could correct.[^s2]

## Key ideas

**Anger is a judgment we assent to.** Book II separates an involuntary first jolt at an apparent injury, which is not yet anger, from anger proper, which rushes on to act on that impression as true and seeks revenge (II.3–4).[^s1] Earlier Stoics called such first jolts proto-emotions (*propatheiai*); because the full emotion needs the mind's assent, it is in our power.[^s2] The judgment at its root is roughly "he wronged me", followed by assent to the thought that he should pay.[^s2]

**Refuse it rather than ration it.** Book I opens by recalling that some wise men called anger a brief madness, and describes the flushed face, set teeth and stamping feet of the angry person (I.1).[^s1] Against Aristotle, who held anger necessary for courage, Seneca argues that virtue needs no help from a vice, that it is easier to keep dangerous passions out than to govern them once admitted, and that a moderate passion is only a moderate evil (I.7, I.9–10).[^s1] The wise person can still punish or defend, but does so because it is right, not out of rage (I.12).[^s2]

**Intervene early.** Most of the remedies work before assent, which fits the theory.[^s3] Delay is the greatest remedy, because anger that waits loses force (II.29).[^s1] Believe nothing until it is plain, since suspicion is a groundless cause of anger (II.24).[^s1] Avoid needless triggers: do not take on more than you can manage, keep company with even-tempered people, and remember that hunger, thirst, tiredness and illness make us irritable (III.7–9).[^s1]

**Review yourself kindly.** In the best-known passage, Seneca follows the philosopher Sextius in examining each day's words and deeds at night, pardoning his faults and resolving to do better, because anger cools when it knows it will face a judge (III.36).[^s1][^s2]

**Calming someone else.** Do not reprove people while they are angry, which only adds to their anger; let the first burst pass, and approach them gently (III.39–40).[^s1]

**Life is short.** The work ends by asking why we waste so brief a life on feuds that death will soon end, and urges us to practise humanity while we live among people (III.42–43).[^s1]

## Why it matters

Anger is the emotion most likely to feel like clear sight while it distorts judgment, and *De Ira* offers both a theory of why and a set of habits for slowing it down.[^s2][^s1] Its advice translates directly to work and family life: wait before sending the reply, check the story you are telling yourself about someone's motive, notice when hunger or overload is doing the talking, and do not argue with a colleague at the height of their anger.[^s1] Its nightly review gave the West one of its most durable reflective practices.[^s2] Romm's edition presents the work as a still-usable guide that sets forgiveness and compassion against rage.[^s4]

## Common misreadings

The treatise is not a counsel of suppression or passivity. Seneca allows firm action and even punishment, but asks that it come from judgment rather than rage.[^s2] Nor does it blame people for their first flush of feeling: involuntary reactions are explicitly not anger.[^s1] Scholars also debate how tidy its psychology is. Some read it as fully consistent with the Stoic view that the mind is a single rational faculty, while others see it as dividing the mind or appealing to physiology,[^s2] and Graver notes that one strategy in Book III for anger already in full flow sits awkwardly with Seneca's own theory.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

*De Ira* is philosophy and moral exhortation, not a study. Some of its practical advice nonetheless lines up with later experiments. In Bushman's experiment with 600 students, those who hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who insulted them felt angrier than those who did nothing, and were more aggressive.[^s5] A 2024 meta-analysis of 154 studies found that activities which lower arousal, such as slow breathing and time-outs, reduced anger, while venting found no support.[^s6] These findings fit Seneca's case for delay and calm, but they do not test his central claim that anger should be eliminated rather than moderated, which remains a philosophical dispute with the Aristotelian view.[^s2] Anger that persistently harms relationships or leads to aggression is a reason to seek professional help, not only a book.

## Connections

*De Ira* is **authored by** Seneca and sits within **Stoicism**.[^s2] It **contrasts with** Aristotle's **good temper**, the virtue of being angry at the right things and to the right degree, which Seneca rejects (I.9–10).[^s1][^s2] Its advice to delay and not to rehearse grievances is loosely **supported by** Bushman's **venting and rumination experiment** and by the **meta-analysis of arousal-lowering anger activities**.[^s5][^s6] It is the source of the Stoic version of the **evening review**, and it relates to **anger**, **rumination**, **self-distancing**, **forgiveness** and **patience**.[^s1]
