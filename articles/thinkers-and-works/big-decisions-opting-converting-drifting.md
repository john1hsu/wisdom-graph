# Big Decisions: Opting, Converting, Drifting

*Big Decisions: Opting, Converting, Drifting* is an essay by the philosopher Edna Ullmann-Margalit about the limits of decision theory when the choice in front of us could change who we are.[^s1] Its opening analogy is that Newtonian physics works for middle-sized objects but fails for the very small and the very large; in the same way, standard rational-choice theory may fit only middle-sized, ordinary decisions.[^s1] The very small and very big ones, she argues, need treatment of their own.[^s1]

## Origin

Ullmann-Margalit first set out the idea as a fellow at the Wissenschaftskolleg zu Berlin in 1984–85, in a report titled "Opting: The Case of 'Big' Decisions".[^s2] The full essay appeared in 2006 in a Royal Institute of Philosophy supplement published by Cambridge University Press, and was later collected in her book *Normal Rationality* (Oxford University Press, 2017), whose theme is how people proceed when they cannot simply act on reasons or project consequences.[^s1][^s6] The Stanford Encyclopedia lists the essay, with David Lewis's work on what experience teaches, as one of the two main antecedents of today's debate on transformative experience.[^s3]

## Key ideas

**Picking and choosing.** At the small end, when options are effectively identical, we cannot choose for a reason; we simply *pick*.[^s2]

**Opting.** A big decision, in the earlier report, has four marks. It transforms the person's beliefs and values with a sharp break, rather than through gradual change. It is irrevocable, a point of no return. The person sees it as a real choice between live options. And the rejected option lingers as a shadow against which the chosen life is later measured.[^s2] Ben-Menahem confirms that the 2006 version keeps the lingering shadow as the most important of these marks.[^s4] Ullmann-Margalit's examples range from an abdication for love to a young person torn between a concert career and science.[^s2]

**The old-self, new-self problem.** Opting changes what she calls the person's rationality base, the beliefs and desires by which actions are judged. So the decision must be made from inside the present self about a future self whose standards cannot yet be known.[^s2] Rational choice in the optimal sense, she concludes, does not fully apply.[^s2]

**Converting.** A convert is also transformed, but does not experience a genuine choice. Converts feel there is no other way, and often come to see their former life as wrong rather than merely different.[^s2]

**Drifting.** A drifter makes a big decision without seeing it as one, through a stepwise build-up of commitments, in marriages and careers alike. Where someone drifts through what is really an opting situation, she suggests, self-deception may be at work.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The essay helps people stop expecting the wrong thing from a deliberation. Ullmann-Margalit distinguishes being rational in the optimising sense from being reasonable, and holds that failing to optimise in a big decision does not make anyone irrational in the everyday sense.[^s2] Her practical proposal is to cut a big decision down to middle size: break it into reversible steps, such as living together before marrying or taking a visiting term before accepting a permanent post abroad.[^s2] The Stanford Encyclopedia summarises this as minimising the two burdens of a big decision, its discontinuity and its point of no return.[^s3]

In practice, readers can use the three categories as a diagnosis. At work, a promotion out of hands-on work, a move into management or a decision to leave a profession may be opting; a slow slide into a role nobody chose may be drifting.[^s2] In relationships, the same questions apply to moving in together, marriage and parenthood.[^s3]

## Common misreadings

The essay is sometimes read as saying that big decisions are irrational and should be left to gut feeling. Ullmann-Margalit rejects both the hope for a super-rational method and the retreat to pure instinct, and asks instead how reason can better steer a life.[^s2] Nor are opting situations pathological; she treats them as rare but normal limiting cases of ordinary choice.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

This is a philosophical analysis, not a study. On how people actually decide, Ullmann-Margalit left the empirical question largely open, noting only some evidence that people can be more slapdash with big choices than with small ones.[^s2] Her stronger claim, that the deepest commitments are made without appeal to reasons, has been challenged. Ben-Menahem argues that beliefs are not a tidy deductive system, so people can criticise and revise even foundational commitments from within.[^s4] L. A. Paul, building on the essay, argues against Ullmann-Margalit that decision theory can sometimes show a transformative choice to be rational, while raising a separate problem of authenticity.[^s5] Persistent distress over a life choice is a reason to talk with a counsellor or other professional, not only to keep deliberating.

## Connections

The essay is **authored by** Edna Ullmann-Margalit and **studied in** decision theory, whose limits it maps.[^s1] It **introduces** the problem of **major life decisions** as a distinct topic for decision theory, and it is an acknowledged precursor of L. A. Paul's **transformative experience**.[^s3][^s5] Its strategy of turning big choices into reversible steps **complements** the principle of **reversible and irreversible decisions**.[^s2] Its account of drifting links to **self-deception**, and its lingering shadow of the road not taken links to **regret**.[^s2][^s4] Its preference for reasonable over optimal choice echoes **satisficing**, and its worry about an unknowable future self echoes the **end-of-history illusion**.[^s2]
