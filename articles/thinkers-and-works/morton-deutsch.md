# Morton Deutsch

Morton Deutsch (1920–2017) was an American social psychologist at Teachers College, Columbia University, widely regarded as a founder of the scientific study of conflict resolution.[^s1][^s2] His central question was not how to end conflict but what decides whether a conflict turns out constructive, leaving the parties feeling they gained, or destructive, leaving them feeling they lost.[^s3] For anyone trying to handle a quarrel with a partner, a sibling or a colleague more wisely, his answer is still useful: the way people frame their goals, and treat each other along the way, tends to set the course of the whole dispute.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

Deutsch was born in the Bronx in 1920, entered the City College of New York at fifteen, and served as an air navigator in the Second World War.[^s1] Kurt Lewin then recruited him to the Research Center for Group Dynamics at MIT, where his doctoral research set out a theory of cooperation and competition.[^s1][^s2] He later worked at Bell Laboratories, where he and Robert Krauss devised a bargaining experiment known as the trucking game, and then at New York University and Teachers College, where in 1986 he founded the International Center for Cooperation and Conflict Resolution; he also practised as a psychoanalyst.[^s1] His 1973 book *The Resolution of Conflict: Constructive and Destructive Processes* drew his research together.[^s2][^s3]

## Key ideas

- **Cooperation and competition.** When people's goals are linked so that they sink or swim together, they tend to cooperate; when one side's success means the other's failure, they tend to compete.[^s4] Deutsch's early work linked cooperation with openness, helpfulness and trust, and competition with poorer communication, coercion and suspicion.[^s2]
- **The crude law of social relations.** The processes typical of a relationship tend to call up more of the same; as he put it, "cooperation breeds cooperation, while competition breeds competition."[^s3] Coleman describes this as what would now be called a reinforcing feedback loop.[^s2]
- **Conflict is not the enemy.** Deutsch held that conflict is neither good nor bad in itself, and that it can prevent stagnation and bring problems into the open; the aim is to make it productive.[^s2][^s3]
- **Why disputes escalate.** Destructive conflicts tend to widen through competitive tactics, misperception and the urge to stay consistent; people read the other side's acts as hostile while seeing their own similar acts as benign. Trust also breaks more easily than it is rebuilt.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Deutsch gives a mechanism for an intuition many wisdom traditions share: how you enter a conflict shapes how it ends. His work suggests that resolution goes better when the parties recast the dispute as a joint problem, and when they keep to cooperative norms such as honesty, respect, owning one's part, forgiveness and seeking common ground.[^s4] At work this might mean two managers fighting over a shared budget agreeing first on what both teams need to deliver; at home, two partners treating a recurring argument as a puzzle to solve together rather than a contest.[^s4] Shared values such as reciprocity, human fallibility and nonviolence can supply common ground even between sharply opposed sides.[^s4] Because the crude law runs both ways, one person's first move, a suspicious accusation or a candid acknowledgement, can tilt the exchange that follows.[^s3]

## Common misreadings

- **"Always cooperate."** Deutsch studied mixed-motive situations in which cooperative and competitive interests coexist, and he discussed how low-power groups build their own power before genuine cooperation becomes possible.[^s3] Cooperation is not capitulation.
- **"A law of nature."** He called the law crude: it describes tendencies in relationships, not certainties.[^s2][^s3]
- **Avoiding conflict as the wise course.** His point was to steer conflict toward productive ends, not to suppress it.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The cooperation side of Deutsch's theory is among the better-supported ideas in social psychology. Reviewers of his contribution to teamwork report that hundreds of studies, summarised in meta-analyses, link cooperative goals with better communication, open-minded discussion of opposing views, productivity and relationships.[^s5] A later meta-analysis of experiments with college students found cooperative structures ahead of competitive ones for achievement and, by a larger margin, for peer relationships.[^s6] Much of this evidence comes from classrooms, mostly with North American undergraduates, and the meta-analysis author flagged gaps in study quality and unexplained variation, so its fit with marriages, families or workplaces is an extrapolation.[^s6] Deutsch's claims about escalation, trust and misperception were built from theory and earlier experiments, and no large recent replication of them was found when this article was written.[^s3] As a person entry, his evidence status is historical. None of this is a guide for relationships involving abuse, coercion or danger, where seeking safety and professional help comes first.

## Connections

Deutsch **introduced** the modern study of how conflicts between people become constructive or destructive, the question behind this graph's **interpersonal conflict** entry; his work spans interpersonal and **intergroup conflict** alike.[^s3] He **introduced** the **theory of cooperation and competition**[^s2] and the **crude law of social relations**, and **authored** *The Resolution of Conflict*.[^s3] He was **influenced by** **Kurt Lewin**, his mentor at MIT.[^s1] His cooperative norms overlap with **honesty**, **forgiveness** and **perspective-taking**, his account of misperception with **egocentric bias**, and his advice to seek arrangements both sides can accept with the compromise-seeking scored in **wise reasoning**.[^s3][^s4] His communication and negotiation work is covered more fully in the sibling communication graph.
