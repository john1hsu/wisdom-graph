# Leading others

Leading others is the life situation of being answerable for other people's work: running a team, supervising a shift, coaching a squad, heading a project or a classroom. What sets it apart is an asymmetry. Your decisions shape the days, prospects and pay of people who have less say than you, and the authority that makes you responsible can also make it harder to hear them.[^s1][^s4] This article is about that situation and what wisdom asks of someone in it; the quality of leading well is treated under **wise leadership**.[^s6]

## Origin

The situation is as old as households, armies and workshops, but modern research looks at it from two directions. Management scholars such as Linda Hill have followed people through the move from individual contributor to manager and documented how much of what they expected turned out to be wrong.[^s4] Social psychologists, meanwhile, have run experiments in which people are put in a high-power or low-power frame of mind (often by writing about a time they held power over someone) to see how power changes judgment.[^s1][^s2] Sternberg's WICS model adds a theory of what leaders need: creativity to generate ideas, analytical intelligence to test them, practical intelligence to carry them out and persuade others, and wisdom to weigh the interests of everyone affected so that the result serves a common good.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Having power over others seems to change how much you let them change your mind. In four studies, including a field survey of incoming business students rated by their co-workers and randomised experiments, See and colleagues found that people who held or felt more power were more confident in their own judgment and gave less weight to advice; confidence largely explained the link.[^s1] In their final experiment, high-power participants ended up less accurate than a simple average of their own estimate and the adviser's would have been.[^s1] The authors' summary is stark: the most powerful decision makers can also be the least accurate.[^s1] Since a leader's errors spread to everyone downstream, this discounting of advice is costly.

The people being led also decide how much they will tell you. In Edmondson's field study of 51 teams in a manufacturing company, teams whose members felt it was safe to take interpersonal risks did more of the asking, admitting errors and seeking feedback that let them learn, and that learning was linked to better performance.[^s5] Team leader coaching was among the conditions associated with that sense of safety, and members of one struggling team described a leader who did not want to hear when things were going badly.[^s5]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Treat authority as a last resort, not a method.** Hill and Lineback found that new managers often assume the title will produce results, yet formal authority rarely secures real commitment and leaning on it amounts to managing through fear; influence comes from trust, relationships and drawing on what the team knows.[^s4]
- **Make the psychological shift.** Strong individual performers promoted into management often keep doing the work and neglect the people; the job has changed from being good at the task to helping others become good at it.[^s4]
- **Deliberately give advice more weight than feels natural.** Because power inflates confidence, a leader who wants to stay accurate has to correct for it, for example by asking for estimates before revealing their own and by comparing their view with an average of others'.[^s1]
- **Do not read silence as agreement.** Ask directly, check understanding, and ask what someone would add.[^s4][^s5]
- **Weigh everyone the decision touches.** WICS places wisdom in balancing the interests of all stakeholders over the short and long term, not only the metric the leader is judged on.[^s6]
- **Manage upward and outward as well.** Hill stresses that a team's success depends on the context around it, so building relationships with peers and one's own boss is part of leading, not a distraction from it.[^s4]

## Pitfalls

The commonest mistake is the one the excerpt from *Being the Boss* names directly: falling back on being the boss, which tends to signal that influence has already been lost.[^s4] A second is hearing only from the people who agree; a third is hoarding the work one is best at, which keeps the leader busy and the team dependent.[^s4][^s5] A fourth is over-learning from popular psychology. The widely repeated claim that a brief taste of power stops people from seeing others' points of view rests partly on a study whose experiment on taking others' knowledge into account did not replicate: across about 3,000 participants in Many Labs 3, the effect was close to zero (d = 0.03), against d = 0.77 in the original group of 42 students.[^s2][^s3] The advice-taking evidence is more consistent, but the safest conclusion is about habits and structures that keep a leader listening, not a law that power corrupts.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The studies here are mostly short experiments with students, single-company field studies and practitioner research based on interviews, so effect sizes in real organisations are uncertain.[^s1][^s4][^s5] Edmondson notes that her data did not pin down specific leader behaviours precisely.[^s5] WICS is a theoretical model rather than a tested intervention.[^s6] The findings describe tendencies, not destinies.

## Connections

The hazards of the situation are **demonstrated by** See and colleagues' study of power, confidence and advice taking[^s1] and by Edmondson's study of psychological safety and team learning,[^s5] and the situation is **studied in organizational behaviour**.[^s1] What leaders need is **explained by** Sternberg's **WICS model**, which gives wisdom the job of balancing interests toward a common good.[^s6] The practices that apply here include **seeking counsel**, which counters **egocentric discounting**; **perspective-taking**; and the **after-action review**, for learning together from what happened. **Wise leadership**, **humble leadership** and **servant leadership** describe ways of carrying authority well, while **hubris** names how it goes wrong. **Giving advice** and **intellectual humility** round out the skills a leader draws on.
