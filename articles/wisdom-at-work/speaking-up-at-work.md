# Speaking up at work

Speaking up at work is the situation of knowing something that people above you ought to hear — a mistake in a forecast, a safety risk, an unfair practice, a colleague who is struggling, a better way to do the job — and having to decide whether to say it. Researchers call raising it **voice** and withholding it **silence**.[^s1][^s2] Wisdom here is not a rule to always speak or always keep quiet, but the judgment to tell an important concern from a passing grievance, to weigh who bears the cost of silence, and to find a time and form in which the truth can be heard.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Organisational researchers began studying silence systematically around 2000. In an early interview study, Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin asked 40 full-time employees from consulting, finance, media, pharmaceuticals and advertising about times they had held back a concern from someone above them.[^s1] Detert and Edmondson then examined "latent voice episodes", the moments when someone considers raising an issue and may or may not do so, and found that whether people speak depends heavily on the specific situation and the leader in front of them.[^s4] The dilemma itself is much older. Classical Chinese thought treats **remonstrance** (*jian*) — a minister's or a child's duty to correct a superior who has gone wrong — as a mark of loyalty to principle, carried out with courtesy and often at serious personal risk.[^s7] Asked how to serve a ruler, Confucius in the *Analects* tells his disciple not to deceive him but to "withstand him to his face".[^s6] (Book XIV, section 23 in Legge's numbering; 14.22 in some editions.)

## Why it matters

Silence is common and rarely idle. Thirty-four of the 40 people Milliken and colleagues interviewed (85%) recalled an important concern they had felt unable to raise; the most frequent topics were a colleague's or boss's performance, problems with processes and suggestions for improvement, and pay.[^s1] Their leading reasons were fear of being labelled a troublemaker, fear of damaging relationships, a sense that speaking would change nothing, and fear of retaliation.[^s1] Most believed colleagues shared their unease: silence tends to be collective.[^s1] Detert and Edmondson describe two beliefs that must both be in place before people speak: that it will not bring serious personal risk, and that it will not be a waste of time.[^s4] Feeling powerless relative to others also predicts silence, and that link is weaker when people see their supervisor as open to input.[^s3] The price of silence can fall on others: the researchers cite the *Columbia* shuttle loss and the GM ignition-switch failure, where problems were known and not raised.[^s3]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Separate prudent caution from a reflex.** Milliken's interviewees often weighed real risks sensibly, but fear can also inflate the danger, and people learn what is "unsayable" partly by watching colleagues rather than by testing it themselves.[^s1][^s4] Ask whether your silence is a judgment about this issue or a habit — and whether you are the right person to speak, now.[^s5]
- **Weigh importance and impact, not only safety.** One practical framing asks two questions: is it safe, and is it worth it — will saying it make a useful difference?[^s5] When the cost of silence falls on others and is high, the balance shifts even when speaking is uncomfortable.[^s5]
- **Check your facts and your motives.** Remonstrance in the Confucian ideal served shared principles, not self-interest, and was argued in moral terms with examples from the past rather than as a bare rebuke.[^s7]
- **Choose time, place and form.** Classical advice favoured courteous, repeated reminders of principles the superior already holds, and withdrawal only as a last resort.[^s7] *Analects* XIV.4 likewise distinguishes how boldly one should speak in a well-governed state from the reserve suited to a badly governed one.[^s6]
- **If you lead, make voice both safe and worthwhile.** Detert and Edmondson advise leaders to seek input personally and informally, act on it or explain why not, and reward those who raise problems; a suggestion box rarely does the job.[^s4] (See **leading others**.)

## Pitfalls

One error is waiting for perfect safety: Milliken and colleagues note that fear can lead people to overestimate the danger of speaking, so a concern that matters may never be raised.[^s1] The opposite error is treating every disagreement as a matter of conscience, or delivering a concern as a bare rebuke; the remonstrance ideal joined candour with decorum and deference.[^s5][^s7] Speaking up also carries real costs that wisdom should not wish away. In a meta-analysis, Chamberlin, Newton and LePine found that **promotive** voice (suggesting improvements) was positively associated with job performance ratings while **prohibitive** voice (raising problems and objections) showed the opposite pattern.[^s2] Valuable warnings can still be unwelcome, so raise them carefully and look for allies and formal channels.[^s1][^s2] Concerns about harassment, safety or law-breaking have formal reporting routes for good reason; this article is not legal advice.

## Evidence and limits

The research is mainly correlational: interviews, surveys and a meta-analysis of field studies, plus some laboratory experiments.[^s1][^s2][^s3] The Milliken study is small, relied on recall, and drew on relatively young MBA students.[^s1] Much of the practical advice, including the "safe and worth it" framing, is informed by research but not tested as an intervention.[^s4][^s5] Accounts of remonstrance describe a classical ideal; schools differed — Mencius allowed more drastic steps against a persistently wrong ruler than other texts — and historical practice was often brutal for those who tried it.[^s7]

## Connections

The situation is **demonstrated by** Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin's **employee silence interview study**[^s1], by Chamberlin and colleagues' **voice meta-analysis**[^s2] and by Morrison, See and Pan's study linking **powerlessness and silence**[^s3], and it is **studied in organisational behaviour**.[^s2] It calls above all on **courage** and **honesty**, and on judgment of timing (**kairos**). **Remonstrance** in **Confucianism** is its classical counterpart.[^s6][^s7] **Right speech** asks whether words are true, timely and helpful, and **self-distancing** can help separate the facts from the fear. **Leading others**, **humble leadership** and **wise leadership** concern inviting dissent, and **seeking counsel** is what voice offers a decision maker; Edmondson's **psychological safety** study describes the team climate in which people speak.
