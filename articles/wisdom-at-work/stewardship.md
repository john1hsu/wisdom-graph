# Stewardship

Stewardship is the habit of holding what you are responsible for (a team, a budget, a client's money, an institution, a piece of land) in trust for others rather than as your own possession. The management researcher Morela Hernandez defines it as attitudes and behaviour that put a group's long-term interests ahead of personal goals, shown when leaders take personal responsibility for what the organisation does and use its power for the welfare of everyone it affects, not only its owners.[^s2] Read as a virtue, it combines care, responsibility and a long time horizon: the steward asks not "what can I get from this?" but "what state will I leave it in?"[^s2]

## Origin

The steward is an ancient figure. A 2023 review traces the role back to ancient law codes such as Hammurabi's, the high stewards of Egypt, and the Greek *oikonomos* (overseer of a household) and *epitropos* (trustee or guardian).[^s5] In Jewish and Christian scripture the earth is said to belong to God (Psalm 24:1; Deuteronomy 10:14), so human beings hold it as caretakers; the review also draws on Gospel passages such as the parable of the fig tree and its caretaker (Luke 13:6–9).[^s5] In environmental ethics, the historian Lynn White argued that mainstream Christian thought, by setting humans above the rest of nature, encouraged its exploitation, while the philosopher John Passmore argued that the same tradition also contains a minority view of humans as stewards of creation, which reform could build on.[^s6]

In management the idea was revived in the 1990s. Peter Block's *Stewardship: Choosing Service over Self-Interest* urged leaders to choose service over personal gain, share rewards and purpose, and favour long-term joint benefit.[^s5] Davis, Schoorman and Donaldson set out a "stewardship theory" of management as a counterweight to agency theory: where agency theory pictures managers as self-interested and opportunistic, needing monitoring and incentives, stewardship theory pictures many of them as pro-organisational and trustworthy, and asks which personal and situational conditions bring out each kind of behaviour.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Many of the worst decisions at work are not dishonest; they simply discount the people who come later. The next owner of a system, the next generation of staff, the community living with a factory's waste all lack a voice in today's meeting. As one research team notes, people usually give fewer resources to future recipients than to present ones, a pattern they trace to psychological distance in time and between people.[^s3] Stewardship is the trait that corrects for that distance. It is also where wisdom and power meet: Hernandez notes that stewards tend to lead through personal power (expertise and the trust others place in them) rather than through position, coercion or rewards.[^s2]

## What good looks like

- **Ownership without possession.** The steward feels that the work is "ours" (psychological ownership) without treating it as private property.[^s2]
- **A wide circle.** The interests of every stakeholder the work affects are weighed, not only those of shareholders, owners or one's own team.[^s2]
- **Accountability that is kept.** In the secular literature, stewards delegate power but keep accountability for the result.[^s5]
- **A long horizon.** Long-term and shared goals count for more than short-term personal gain.[^s5]
- **Moral courage.** In Hernandez's studies, leaders' support for their teams was linked to stewardship mainly through moral courage, the willingness to accept personal risk to do the right thing.[^s2]

## How to practise it

Before a significant decision, deliberately bring to mind who will inherit its consequences once you have moved on, and what they would ask of you; in experiments, prompting people to think about their own legacy shifted choices towards future recipients.[^s3] Keep a short handover note for every role, as if you were leaving tomorrow; it shows what you are holding in trust. Share credit and rewards in ways that build the group rather than your own position.[^s5] Treat the people you lead as future stewards too: Hernandez proposes that followers tend to treat others the way their own leaders treated them, so stewardship can be passed on, or lost, across generations of a team.[^s2]

## Common misreadings

Stewardship is not control. In the steward-leadership literature the steward delegates power while keeping accountability, so a leader who hoards decisions "to protect" a team is guarding the role, not the people it serves.[^s5] Nor is it naive trust: Donaldson himself later conceded that stewardship theory is one-sided and needs combining with sensible checks, and others argue that the same manager can act as a steward in one setting and a self-interested agent in another.[^s5] In religious usage, stewardship carries a theological claim about who ultimately owns the world; secular versions keep the idea of trust but drop that claim, and the two should not be confused.[^s5][^s6]

## Evidence and limits

Stewardship is a normative ideal, but parts of it have been studied. In two small experiments (54 students and 71 online participants), people reminded of death gave more to future-oriented causes and to other units than controls did, which the authors attribute to a motive to leave a legacy.[^s3][^s4] The second study used role-play rather than real leaders, and the samples were small, so these results are suggestive.[^s4] Hernandez's survey of 354 executive students (with ratings from supervisors, peers and reports) and a follow-up experiment found that relational and motivational support predicted stewardship through moral courage, but the designs were partly correlational and stewardship and moral courage did not separate cleanly as measures.[^s2] A 2023 review adds that steward leadership is rarely measured on its own and is hard to separate from servant leadership.[^s5]

## Connections

Stewardship is a **moral virtue** and a facet of **wise leadership**, **applied in leading others**.[^s2] It is **explained by stewardship theory** in management research, which sets it against agency theory.[^s1] It **complements servant leadership**, with which, the 2023 review argues, it is so intertwined that the two are hard to separate in practice.[^s5] It is **rooted in Christianity** and the Hebrew scriptures it draws on.[^s5] It is **supported by** the **death, legacies and intergenerational decisions study**, which suggests a legacy motive can counter **intergenerational discounting**, the tendency to give less to people in the future.[^s3] Its reliance on moral courage links it to **courage**;[^s2] its turn from self-interest towards a wide circle of stakeholders sets it against **hubris** and beside **humble leadership**, and its weighing of many interests over the long term echoes the **balance theory of wisdom** and **practical wisdom**.[^s2]
