  - Write down your goals for the next few years and the kinds of help you will need — skill-building, sponsorship, honest feedback, emotional support, a view of how decisions get made.
  - List everyone who currently takes an active interest in your development, note which part of your life each comes from (employer, school, profession, community, family) and how close the tie is.
  - Look for gaps and redundancy — needs nobody covers, or several advisers who all see the world from the same vantage point.
  - Approach one or two new people to fill the biggest gap, asking for something specific and thinking about what they might gain or learn from you in return.

# Building a developmental network

Building a developmental network means deliberately cultivating a set of people — not one mentor — who each take an active interest in your growth and act to advance it. Organisational researchers Monica Higgins and Kathy Kram defined a developmental network as the group of people a person names as doing exactly that at a given time, and called its members "developers".[^s1] The practice turns an old piece of wisdom — seek counsel from the experienced — into a habit of asking *whose* counsel, from *where*, and whether it still fits the life you are living.[^s5]

## Origin

The idea grew out of mentoring research. Mentoring research had long pictured a single relationship between a senior and a junior person. In a 2001 *Academy of Management Review* paper, Higgins and Kram argued that this picture no longer matched working life: job security had weakened, careers crossed organisational boundaries, fast technological change made continuous learning essential, flatter and networked organisations made a single internal mentor less dependable, and a more diverse workforce often drew support from several sources at once.[^s1] They proposed describing a person's developers along two dimensions. *Range* (diversity) is how many different social systems — employer, school, profession, community, family — the developers come from, so that information is less redundant. *Strength* is how emotionally close, reciprocal and frequent each relationship is.[^s1] Crossing the two gives four ideal types: *entrepreneurial* (wide range, strong ties), *opportunistic* (wide range, weak ties), *traditional* (narrow range, strong ties, often centred on one primary developer) and *receptive* (narrow range, weak ties).[^s1]

## Why it matters

The framework yields testable predictions. Higgins and Kram proposed that entrepreneurial networks would be most likely to support career change and personal learning, that traditional networks would foster the most commitment to one's organisation, and that receptive networks would bring the least work satisfaction.[^s1] An earlier longitudinal study of lawyers by Higgins and David Thomas found that the quality of a person's primary mentor best predicted short-term outcomes such as satisfaction and intentions to stay, but that the composition and quality of the whole constellation of developers better predicted longer-term retention and promotion.[^s2] A later review of the field reported that developers from outside one's own organisation and a larger number of developers were associated with higher satisfaction, performance and advancement, and that a broader range of developers was associated with more job offers and career changes.[^s3] For wisdom, the point is practical: a single adviser, however good, sees your situation from one vantage point, and judgment improves when it can draw on several.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Kram and Higgins frame the practice as a cycle rather than a one-off networking push.[^s5] First, know yourself — your goals, strengths, weaknesses and how comfortable you are asking for help — and reassess as circumstances change.[^s5] Second, understand your career context by auditing how promotions, hiring or a change of field actually work in the place you want to go, and who could open doors there.[^s5] Third, enlist developers beyond your boss: senior sponsors, peers who can explain the politics, people outside your organisation and sometimes family.[^s5] Fourth, approach people with mutual benefit in mind, since juniors often bring knowledge seniors lack and developers gain from the relationship too.[^s5] Finally, tend and revise the network: as careers move on, former developers often become friends or more distant allies, and new needs call for new people.[^s5] The authors also suggest developing others as you develop yourself, through peer-coaching groups or by mentoring juniors.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Mistaking contacts for developers.** Kram and Higgins distinguish high-quality mentoring — mutual learning and a wish for more connection — from superficial networking.[^s5] An opportunistic network of many weak ties was predicted to produce *less* personal learning than the other types.[^s1]
- **Assuming bigger is always better.** The review notes that researchers question a simple "more developers is better" view and argue that the right network depends on what the person needs; some evidence suggests a single strong supportive tie can be enough for satisfaction.[^s3]
- **Building an echo chamber.** In a three-wave study of MBA graduates over five years, people whose developers were more interconnected (a denser network) later reported a *less* clear sense of their professional identity.[^s4]
- **Only taking.** Treating developers as resources to be used ignores the reciprocity that makes strong ties strong.[^s1][^s5]
- **Asking a career network to do a clinician's job.** Developers can offer support, but persistent distress is a reason to seek qualified professional help.

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is promising but limited, so the status here is *mixed*. Most studies survey only the protégé, rely on self-reported and subjective outcomes, and draw on narrow samples such as lawyers, academics and MBA alumni; few are longitudinal or test what links network structure to outcomes.[^s3] The single-school MBA sample behind the identity finding limits how far it generalises.[^s4] The lawyer study is longitudinal but also confined to one profession.[^s2] The four network types were offered as propositions to be tested, not established findings.[^s1]

## Connections

This practice sits under **seeking counsel**, which it extends from a single decision to a standing set of relationships, and it **complements** it: Kram and Higgins's advice to look beyond your boss for advisers from varied settings is seeking counsel made routine.[^s5] It is **applied in mentorship**, which Higgins and Kram reconceived as a network rather than a pair,[^s1] in **major life decisions** such as a change of field, where insiders in the new setting make the move realistic,[^s5] and, more tentatively, in **learning from experience**, since the framework predicts that diverse, strong networks support personal learning.[^s1] The perspective was **introduced by Monica Higgins and Kathy Kram**,[^s1] and its value is **demonstrated by the constellations-and-careers study** of lawyers[^s2] and the **developmental-networks-and-professional-identity study** of MBA graduates.[^s4] It requires some **self-reflection**, because mapping a network starts with knowing what you need.[^s5] Related topics include **giving advice**, **leading others**, **workplace feedback** and **generativity**, since helping others develop is part of the practice.[^s5]
