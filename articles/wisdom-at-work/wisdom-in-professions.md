# Wisdom in the professions

Every job that serves people meets cases its rules did not anticipate: the patient whose circumstances make the standard plan unworkable, or the student the scripted lesson leaves behind. The psychologist Barry Schwartz argues that institutions usually respond to poor performance with two tools, tighter rules and smarter incentives, and that neither can replace what such cases actually need: practical wisdom in the person doing the work.[^s1] Wisdom in the professions is the situation of exercising that judgment inside an organisation, with protocols, targets and pay attached.

## Origin

The problem is ancient. In Book V of the *Nicomachean Ethics* Aristotle observes that law is always stated in general terms, so it will sometimes get a particular case wrong; equity (*epieikeia*) is the correction of law where it fails because it is universal, deciding as the lawgiver would have if he had foreseen the case (*NE* V.10, 1137b).[^s2] Schwartz, writing with the political scientist Kenneth Sharpe, brought this Aristotelian idea into modern debates about medicine, law, teaching and management. In his 2011 review he splits practical wisdom into **moral will**, the desire to pursue the proper aim of a practice (health in medicine, learning in teaching), and **moral skill**, the ability to perceive a situation, read people's feelings, foresee consequences and balance competing aims.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Schwartz's central claim is that over-reliance on rules wears down moral skill, and over-reliance on incentives wears down moral will.[^s1] Detailed scripts leave practitioners no room to practise judgment, so the capacity is never built; he cites teachers bound to scripted curricula and test targets, and wildland firefighters whose four simple survival guidelines were replaced by lists of more than forty-eight items, which, drawing on Karl Weick's research, he links to falling survival rates and less improvisation.[^s1] Incentives, he argues, change the question a worker asks from "is this right?" to "is this worth it to me?"[^s1] Much valuable work, such as a hospital custodian's compassion toward a grieving father, appears in no job description and cannot be fully specified or paid for, which is why contracts and metrics always leave a gap that only character fills.[^s1]

The best-known evidence for the incentive side comes from economics. In a field experiment at ten day-care centres in Haifa, a small fine for parents who collected their children late roughly doubled late pickups at the six centres that introduced it, and lateness stayed high after the fine was withdrawn; the control centres showed no similar rise.[^s3] The authors suggest the fine turned a social obligation into a service with a price.[^s3]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Keep the purpose in view.** Ask what the practice is for and whom it serves, and let that guide how you use the protocol.[^s1]
- **Treat rules as aids to judgment, not substitutes for it.** Well-designed rules carry hard-won lessons; the wise practitioner follows them by default and departs only when the case clearly falls outside what they were written for, as Aristotle's equitable judge does.[^s2]
- **Deliberate with others on hard cases.** In interviews with 131 UK medical students and doctors, practitioners described practical wisdom in terms of perceiving the patient's circumstances, respecting their values, recognising the limits of treatment, seeking guidance from colleagues and having the courage to act.[^s5]
- **Find the motivation to follow through.** The same study found that motivation was not a final step but what started and sustained wise decisions throughout.[^s5]
- **Help fix the system, not only the case.** Schwartz argues that institutions get the moral will and skill they ask for, so those who design rules and incentives should arrange them to support judgment and the common good; practitioners can help by reporting where rules misfire.[^s1]
- **Learn from mentors and varied experience.** Schwartz calls practical wisdom a craft learned through the right experiences: varied cases with feedback, mentors who use scripts as training wheels rather than permanent constraints, and the room to plan, act, see results and re-evaluate.[^s1]

## Pitfalls

The commonest misreading is that rules are the enemy of wisdom. Simple, well-designed rules can save lives: when eight hospitals in eight countries introduced the WHO's 19-item surgical safety checklist, deaths fell from 1.5% to 0.8% of operations and complications from 11.0% to 7.0%.[^s6] The authors credit the checklist partly to better team communication, through introductions, briefings and debriefs, which is a support for judgment rather than a replacement.[^s6] A second pitfall is self-serving discretion: "I know better than the protocol" is also what the overconfident and the careless say, so departures should be rare, explainable and open to review.[^s2][^s5] A third is assuming every incentive corrupts. Gneezy and colleagues' review finds that incentives often work, especially when they are large enough and target concrete, measurable tasks; they backfire mainly when they signal distrust or that the behaviour is unimportant, or when they are small.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is mixed. Crowding-out effects are real but depend on incentive size, framing and context.[^s4] The day-care study was small, and its authors could not separate an information explanation from a social-norm one.[^s3] The checklist study used a before-and-after design and acknowledged that being observed may have helped.[^s6] Schwartz presents his account as a plausibility argument needing further research,[^s1] and the doctors' accounts are self-reported narratives from one health system.[^s5]

## Connections

The idea of practical wisdom as the antidote to rules and incentives was **introduced by** Barry Schwartz, and it is **studied in organizational behaviour**.[^s1] Why incentives can backfire is **explained by** **motivation crowding-out**, as reviewed by Gneezy, Meier and Rey-Biel.[^s4] The risk is **demonstrated by** the **"A fine is a price"** day-care study,[^s3] while the **surgical safety checklist study** **demonstrates** that a rule designed to support team judgment can improve outcomes.[^s6] In medicine, the situation is **studied in medical ethics** through research on clinical phronesis.[^s5] It is a workplace case of **practical wisdom**; **stewardship**, **courage** and **seeking counsel** describe what it often asks of people, and **leading others** and **wise leadership** concern those who design the rules and incentives that others work under.
