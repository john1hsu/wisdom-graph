# Moral awareness

Moral awareness is the ability to see that a decision in front of you has ethical stakes: that someone could be harmed, treated unfairly or misled, or that a duty, promise or norm is on the line.[^s5][^s1] Organisational researchers call it the first step in ethical decision-making, because a person who never registers the moral side of a choice never gets as far as reasoning about it.[^s2] Without it, someone may do the right thing by luck, or the wrong thing without realising it.[^s5] Psychologists also speak of *moral sensitivity* or *ethical sensitivity* for much the same capacity.

## Origin

The idea comes from the psychologist James Rest's four-component account of moral behaviour, in which acting well requires noticing the moral issue, judging what is right, intending to do it and following through; teaching materials from the University of Texas at Austin present moral awareness as the first of those four steps.[^s5] In 1991 Thomas Jones argued that features of the issue itself, its *moral intensity*, shape every stage of Rest's sequence, including whether the issue is noticed at all. Jones listed six features, such as the size of the consequences, social consensus that an act is wrong and the closeness of those affected; later factor analysis suggests they collapse into three: probable magnitude of harm, proximity and social consensus.[^s4] Field and survey research by Kenneth Butterfield, Linda Treviño, Gary Weaver and Scott Reynolds then made awareness an object of study in its own right.[^s2][^s1][^s3]

A parallel thread runs through moral philosophy. Iris Murdoch argued that much of moral life is a matter of *seeing* rather than choosing, and that it goes on continually, not only at moments of explicit decision. She held that what we attend to, and how justly, shapes what we do when choices arise.[^s6] Her ideal was a "just and loving gaze" on the reality of other people.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Ethics educators argue that much workplace wrongdoing comes not from weighing the ethics and choosing badly but from never seeing the ethical question at all, largely a problem of attention: a focus on pleasing a boss, fitting in with a team or hitting a target crowds the moral dimension out of view, the process known as ethical fading.[^s5] Moral awareness is the capacity that brings that dimension back. For wisdom it is the perceptual half of good judgement: before you can weigh competing goods, you have to see that goods are in competition.[^s6][^s2]

## What good looks like

People with strong moral awareness spot ethical issues early, in ordinary tasks as well as dramatic dilemmas.[^s3] Several things predict it.

- **The issue's features.** In a field experiment with 291 competitive-intelligence practitioners, awareness depended on the magnitude of consequences and on whether the issue was framed in moral language, as well as on the competitive context and on whether peers were seen to regard the practice as ethically problematic.[^s2]
- **What you count as a moral cue.** Reynolds found that managers' ethical predispositions shaped what they noticed. Those with a more formalist, rule-and-duty outlook recognised both harm and the breaking of a norm as signs of a moral issue, while those with a more utilitarian outlook responded mainly to harm.[^s1]
- **Habitual attention.** Reynolds later measured *moral attentiveness*, a stable tendency to notice and reflect on morality in everyday experience, and found it related to recalling moral behaviour, to moral awareness and to moral behaviour itself.[^s3]

Good awareness is also accurate rather than anxious. Murdoch's account warns that a sharp-eyed but self-serving observer can read people precisely and still fail to see them justly, and that fantasy and self-flattery distort what we take ourselves to see.[^s6]

## How to practise it

- **Ask the harm question every time.** Before acting on a routine request, ask who could be affected and how; probable magnitude of harm and closeness to those affected are central features of an issue's moral intensity.[^s4][^s2]
- **Translate jargon into plain moral words.** Rename "optimisation", "aggressive accounting" or "pulling a deal forward" as what it does to people; moral framing raises awareness.[^s2]
- **Check for rules and promises, not only consequences.** Ask whether a norm, duty or commitment is at stake, the cue that utilitarian-minded people tend to miss.[^s1]
- **Use the outsider test.** Ask whether the people affected, or most colleagues, would see an ethical issue here; perceived consensus is a strong cue.[^s4][^s2]
- **Take unease seriously.** Pause on a sense of guilt, shame or discomfort and ask what it is pointing at; gut feelings are not always right, but they are worth examining.[^s5]
- **Rehearse your commitment.** Remind yourself regularly, even daily, that you intend to act ethically, so ethics stays in your frame of reference under pressure.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The core findings rest on survey, scenario and field-experimental studies with managers and professionals, and they hang together: issue features, framing, social context and individual dispositions all predict whether people recognise moral issues.[^s2][^s1][^s3] The evidence is **mixed** rather than robust, however. Much of it uses hypothetical scenarios and self-reports, the moral intensity construct has not held its originally proposed six-part structure,[^s4] and claims that training reliably raises awareness in practice rest on thinner, mostly self-reported evaluations. Awareness is also only the first step: noticing an issue does not guarantee sound judgement or the courage to act.[^s5]

## Connections

Moral awareness **addresses ethical fading**, the attention failure that lets the moral side of a choice disappear,[^s5] and is **part of** Rest's **four-component model** of moral behaviour.[^s5] It is **demonstrated by** Butterfield, Treviño and Weaver's **field study of moral awareness in business organizations**[^s2] and **supported by** Reynolds's **study of ethical predispositions**.[^s1] It is **explained by** Jones's **moral intensity** of the issue[^s4] and by the trait of **moral attentiveness**.[^s3] It sits under **practical wisdom** as the perceptive side of judgement, and links to **Iris Murdoch**'s ethics of attention.[^s6] It is related to **self-deception** and **bounded ethicality**, which dim it; to **moral case deliberation** and **speaking up at work**, which put awareness into practice; to **perspective-taking**, which helps reveal who is affected; and to **wise leadership**.[^s5][^s2]
