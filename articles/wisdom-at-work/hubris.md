# Hubris

Hubris, as the word is used today, is the overconfidence that success and power can breed: leaders come to rate their own judgment far above other people's, make overambitious bets, and stop listening to criticism or advice.[^s2] It differs from ordinary confidence less in degree than in its deafness. The confident leader still updates on bad news; the hubristic one explains it away.[^s2][^s6]

## Origin

The Greek *hybris* meant something narrower and uglier than the modern word. In the *Rhetoric* (Book II, Part 2), Aristotle treats it as a form of slighting: doing or saying things that shame another person, not for any gain or in revenge, but for the pleasure of feeling superior. He adds that the young and the rich are prone to it for that reason.[^s1] Earlier still, Solon of Athens, counted among the Seven Sages, warned in his poem on good order that the people's leaders, lured by wealth and unable to keep excess in check, would bring much grief through their great hubris.[^s7] The modern sense keeps the core of that picture, a self-image swollen by advantage, but shifts the emphasis from insulting others to misjudging oneself.[^s1][^s2]

In leadership research the idea took two paths. Management scholars study hubris as a pattern of exaggerated self-confidence that can be measured indirectly and linked to company decisions.[^s2][^s4] In 2009 the physician and former politician David Owen and the psychiatrist Jonathan Davidson proposed a "hubris syndrome": 14 features, among them speaking of oneself in the royal "we", identifying oneself with the nation or organisation, restless recklessness, and letting a broad vision override practical detail, said to appear in some people only after they have held substantial power for a while.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Hubris turns a leader's strengths into a hazard. The track record that earned authority becomes the reason to discount warnings, so mistakes grow larger before anyone can stop them.[^s2] The best-known evidence comes from corporate acquisitions. In a study of 106 large deals, Hayward and Hambrick found that indicators of the acquiring CEO's hubris (the firm's recent performance, recent media praise of the CEO, and the CEO's self-importance) were strongly associated with how much more than the market price the company paid, and that bigger premiums and greater hubris went with bigger losses for the acquirer's shareholders.[^s4] The link was stronger where the board was weak, with many insiders on it or the CEO also chairing it.[^s4] The same pattern appears in smaller settings, wherever one person's say-so can override a team's information.[^s6]

## How to spot it

Watch for behaviour rather than labels. Warning signs in yourself or others include:[^s2][^s3][^s6]

- Treating recent wins and praise as proof that your judgment no longer needs checking.[^s2][^s4]
- Dismissing a concern because of who raised it, or punishing people for raising one.[^s2]
- Speaking as if you and the organisation were the same thing.[^s3]
- Growing impatience with detail, evidence and process when they get in the way of a big idea.[^s3]
- A shrinking circle of advisers who mostly agree with you.[^s6]

## How to fix it

Hubris is hard to see from the inside, so the remedies are mostly structural: arrange your situation so that other people can still reach you.[^s6]

1. **Build channels for honest feedback** and protect the people who use them; ask for the case against your plan before you commit.[^s6]
2. **Keep reminders that success is temporary**, and spend time in other people's jobs to keep their view in sight.[^s6]
3. **Strengthen independent checks.** In Hayward and Hambrick's data, hubris mattered most when boards were dominated by insiders or the CEO chaired the board, which suggests that real oversight blunts its effects.[^s4]
4. **Make decisions interdependent**, so no single person can push a large bet through alone.[^s6]
5. **Cultivate humility deliberately**, as a counterweight that power does not supply on its own.[^s6]

## Evidence and limits

Hubris is real enough to show up in company decisions, but hard to measure. Researchers cannot examine executives directly, so they rely on proxies such as press coverage, pay and language, and a review of the field notes that hubris overlaps with overconfidence, narcissism, pride and very high core self-evaluation and must be distinguished from them.[^s2][^s4] Hubris syndrome is not a recognised diagnosis in DSM or ICD; a 2023 editorial arguing for its inclusion concedes that no systematic follow-up studies exist and that ruling out other conditions is the hardest step.[^s3] This article describes a pattern of judgment and conduct, not a clinical condition, and is no basis for diagnosing anyone.[^s3]

The popular claim that power by itself erodes people's ability to see others' perspectives also outruns its evidence. In the Many Labs 3 project, a replication of one well-known experiment on power and perspective-taking with nearly 3,000 participants found no effect (d = 0.03), against d = 0.77 in the original sample of 42.[^s5] Hubris may depend more on long tenure, repeated success and weak checks than on a brief taste of power.[^s3][^s4][^s5]

## Connections

As a warning against the excess of the powerful, it is **rooted in** the archaic Greek tradition of the **Seven Sages**, through Solon's poetry.[^s7] Hubris **undermines wise leadership** by cutting a leader off from criticism and advice, and **undermines leading others** by turning attention from the people led toward oneself.[^s2][^s6] It **contrasts with humility**, the counterweight that management writers recommend cultivating.[^s6] Its effects in business are **demonstrated by** Hayward and Hambrick's study of acquisition premiums, and the broader idea that power quickly destroys perspective-taking is **challenged by** the failed replication in Many Labs 3.[^s4][^s5] It is related to **humble leadership**, the practice most directly aimed against it; to **intellectual humility** and **know thyself**, ancient and modern reminders of one's limits; to **seeking counsel**, which keeps outside views in play; and to **intellectual arrogance** and **escalation of commitment**, its cousins in belief and in decision-making.[^s1][^s2][^s6] The cognitive bias of overconfidence, treated in the sibling critical-thinking graph, is one ingredient of hubris but not the whole of it.[^s2]
