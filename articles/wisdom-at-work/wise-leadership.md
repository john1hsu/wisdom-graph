# Wise leadership

Wise leadership is leading with practical wisdom rather than with intelligence, charisma or technical skill alone. In Robert Sternberg's WICS model, creativity generates ideas, analytical intelligence tests them and practical intelligence carries them out and persuades others; wisdom is the element that balances the interests of all stakeholders and keeps the leader's actions aimed at a common good.[^s1] Ikujiro Nonaka and Hirotaka Takeuchi describe the same quality as phronesis: serving the common good while making sound judgment calls and acting in the present.[^s6]

## Origin

The idea is old. In Book VI of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle says people credit Pericles and others like him with practical wisdom because they can see what is good for themselves and for people in general, and he counts those who manage households and cities well among the wise.[^s2] He treats political wisdom and practical wisdom as the same state of mind seen from different angles, and notes that it depends on experience of particular cases, which is why young people are rarely practically wise.[^s2] Modern psychology took the theme up through Sternberg's WICS model, published in *American Psychologist* in 2007,[^s1] while management scholars such as Nonaka and Takeuchi built it into accounts of how long-lived companies renew themselves.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Leaders decide for other people, so their errors spread. Wisdom is what separates a leader who is clever from one whose cleverness serves more than themselves: in the WICS account, intelligence and creativity without wisdom can be put to narrow or harmful ends.[^s1] Studies of leaders also suggest that wisdom shows up in the relationships people experience at work, not only in big strategic calls (see Evidence and limits below).[^s3][^s5]

## What it looks like in practice

Drawing on their study of long-lived firms, Nonaka and Takeuchi list six habits of wise leaders: judging what is good for society as well as the company; grasping the essence of people and situations; creating shared spaces where people make meaning together; conveying the essence through stories and metaphors; bringing people with conflicting goals together and moving them to act; and developing practical wisdom in others through apprenticeship and staying close to frontline work.[^s6] Psychological research adds two more concrete marks. Leaders who score higher on wisdom tend to give individual followers more personal attention, which predicts better leader–follower relationships,[^s3] and recognising and managing uncertainty was the wisdom dimension most clearly linked to transformational leadership in a multi-source study.[^s4]

In everyday terms, that means naming who a decision affects before deciding, saying what you don't know, treating people as individuals rather than headcount, and explaining your reasoning so others can learn to judge for themselves.[^s1][^s3][^s4][^s6]

## Common misreadings

- **Wise means soft or indecisive.** Aristotle's practically wise person is defined by acting well in particular cases, not by weighing forever,[^s2] and Nonaka and Takeuchi include using power to move people to act.[^s6]
- **Wise means "anything goes."** In one study, the wisdom dimension of relativism of values was negatively related to rated transformational leadership, while managing uncertainty was positively related.[^s4] Tolerance of other views is not the same as having no view.
- **Smart, successful leaders are wise.** WICS treats intelligence and wisdom as distinct resources a leader has to combine.[^s1] In the multi-source study, narcissism predicted weaker transformational leadership even after mental ability and emotional intelligence were controlled.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The empirical base is real but thin. Among 75 religious leaders rated by 158 followers, leaders' personal wisdom predicted higher-quality leader–follower relationships, indirectly through individualized consideration, after controlling for the Big Five, emotional intelligence and narcissism.[^s3] A study of 77 school employees, each also rated by colleagues, linked Berlin-paradigm wisdom dimensions to transformational leadership, but only one dimension positively and only in a cross-sectional design the authors call small and specialised.[^s4] In a two-wave survey of 821 employees in nine organisations, perceived wise leadership, measured as fair and supportive supervision, linked organisational practices to later job satisfaction and well-being.[^s5] That measure is close to "good supervision" in general, and the case material behind the six habits comes largely from Japanese firms and founder anecdotes.[^s5][^s6] So the evidence status here is **mixed**: wisdom-related traits go with better leadership relationships, but definitions vary, samples are small, and no study yet shows that training leaders in wisdom changes outcomes.

## Connections

Wise leadership is **applied in leading others** and **explained by the WICS model**, in which wisdom balances stakeholders' interests toward a common good.[^s1] It is **rooted in Aristotelian virtue ethics**, where practical and political wisdom are one state.[^s2] It is **supported by** the leader-wisdom and LMX study,[^s3] the wisdom-and-narcissism study,[^s4] and, more weakly, the wise-organisations study,[^s5] and is **studied in the psychology of wisdom**, which supplied the Berlin-paradigm measures.[^s4] Its narrower form is humble leadership, and its characteristic failure is hubris.
