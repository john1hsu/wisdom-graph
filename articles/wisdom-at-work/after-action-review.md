  - Agree at the start that the purpose is learning, not grading or blame, and that what is said will not be used to punish anyone.
  - Establish what was supposed to happen, using the original plan, goals or standards.
  - Reconstruct what actually happened from several viewpoints and from any objective records, before anyone explains or defends.
  - Ask why the gap opened, looking for specific causes you could influence, including your own decisions.

# After-action review

An after-action review (AAR), called an after-event review in the research literature, is a short, structured conversation held soon after a task, in which the people who did it work out what happened, why it happened, and how to keep their strengths and fix their weaknesses next time.[^s1] It is a disciplined form of reflection: instead of being told what they did wrong, participants are helped to "discover for themselves what happened, why it happened" and what to change.[^s1] Researchers define a good debrief by four features: active self-learning rather than passive feedback, a developmental and non-punitive aim, a focus on specific events, and input from more than one source.[^s2]

## Origin

The method comes from the U.S. Army. A review by Morrison and Meliza for the Army Research Institute traces it to the mid-1970s, when it replaced the traditional critique, in which umpires delivered subjective judgments in a one-way lecture that soldiers often resented.[^s1] The AAR turned that lecture into a facilitated discussion, grounded in objective records of what happened, in which unit members reach their own conclusions; it was later adapted for the Army's National Training Center.[^s1] A model for it was S. L. A. Marshall's group interviews of soldiers immediately after combat in the Second World War.[^s1] Other military services and civilian organisations have since adopted the method,[^s1] where it is often reduced to a one-off project "postmortem".[^s6]

## Why it matters

Wisdom researchers argue that life's challenges can make people wiser, but only for those who bring certain resources to them, among them a sense of mastery, openness, *reflectivity* and emotion regulation with empathy.[^s5] In this view, what decides whether a hard experience yields wisdom is not the experience itself but how a person appraises it, copes with it and works it into their life story.[^s5] The AAR builds that reflection into ordinary work, so that learning does not depend on whether someone happens to brood productively over a setback.[^s4][^s5]

The practical payoff is well documented. A meta-analysis of 46 samples (2,136 people) found that debriefed teams and individuals outperformed comparison groups by roughly 20 to 25 per cent on average.[^s2] In a nine-month study of 173 people going through four developmental experiences, structured after-event reviews increased leadership development.[^s4] Because the discussion leader facilitates rather than lectures, and the atmosphere is non-punitive, the method also moves a group from judging people towards learning together.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Keep it close to the event and short; during intense or long projects, review after each phase rather than waiting for the end.[^s6] State at the outset that the aim is learning, not evaluation.[^s1][^s2] Then work through three questions: what was supposed to happen, what actually happened, and why the two differed, before turning to what to sustain and what to change.[^s1] Gather several viewpoints and any objective data before interpreting, since debriefs that draw on multiple sources work better.[^s2] Review successes as well as failures: in a quasi-field experiment with soldiers on navigation exercises, those who reviewed both improved significantly more than those who reviewed only their failures.[^s3] Finally, close the loop: write down a few specific lessons, assign each to someone, and review them before the next similar task, a step practitioners call a before-action review.[^s6]

The same structure works for one person. Debriefs aimed at individuals were about as effective as team debriefs, and the effect is largest when the review focuses on the level at which performance is judged.[^s2] After a hard conversation or a missed deadline, you can run the four questions on yourself in a notebook.[^s2][^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Turning it into a trial.** If the review is used to assign blame or grades, people defend themselves instead of reporting what happened; the original method was designed to remove exactly that dynamic.[^s1][^s2]
- **Only reviewing failures.** Successes hold lessons too, and failure-only reviews teach less.[^s3]
- **Lessons noted but not applied.** A common corporate failure is treating the review as a one-off report and then "discovering" the same mistakes on the next project; learning only counts once behaviour changes.[^s6]
- **No facilitation or structure.** The few unfacilitated and unstructured debriefs in the meta-analysis showed much smaller effects, though there were too few to be conclusive.[^s2]
- **Assuming it works for everyone equally.** Benefits for leadership development were larger for people who were conscientious, open to experience and emotionally stable, and for those with richer prior experience.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is **mixed** rather than robust: effects are consistently positive and often large, but most studies are quasi-experimental, few unpublished studies were found, and studies with subjective outcome measures reported bigger effects than those with objective ones, so the true benefit may be smaller than the average suggests.[^s2] The effect also depends on how the review is run and on who takes part.[^s2][^s4] The link to wisdom itself is theoretical: no study has yet tested whether regular reviews make people wiser, as opposed to better at a task.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

The AAR **develops self-reflection**, giving it a fixed time, a structure and an endpoint so it becomes learning rather than replay.[^s4] It is **applied in learning from experience**, where reviewing both success and failure matters, as the **study of after-event reviews with soldiers demonstrates**.[^s3] It is **applied in leading others**, and the **leadership-development study** shows its effect on growing leaders.[^s4] It is **supported by** Tannenbaum and Cerasoli's **meta-analysis of team and individual debriefs**.[^s2] It relates to the **MORE life experience model**, which explains why reflecting on challenges helps some people grow wiser,[^s5] and to **humble leadership** and **wise leadership**, which depend on admitting and learning from mistakes.[^s1]
