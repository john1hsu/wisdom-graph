steps:
  - Choose a real, current case from your own work that leaves you or your team unsure what is right, and ask someone trained and not involved in the decision to facilitate.
  - State the moral question as a concrete choice between two options, and name the harm each option would do.
  - Ask factual questions until everyone can stand in the case presenter's shoes, without judging how they handled it.
  - List everyone with a stake in the case and the values and norms that pull each of them towards one option or the other.
  - Look for alternatives beyond the original two options.
  - Each write down privately what you would do, the value behind it, what you would sacrifice, how you would limit that damage and what you would need to act on it.
  - Compare the answers in dialogue, exploring the reasons behind disagreements rather than voting or trying to win.
  - Close by asking what has changed in each person's view, who will act on what, and what to improve in the next session.

# Moral case deliberation

Moral case deliberation (MCD) is a structured meeting in which professionals reflect together, with a trained facilitator, on a moral question raised by a concrete case from their own work. Sessions run from about 45 minutes to a full day, and the facilitator guides the conversation without telling the group what is right.[^s1] It turns the advice to "talk hard cases through with colleagues" into a repeatable practice.

## Origin

MCD grew up mainly in Dutch health care, where ethicists such as Bert Molewijk and Guy Widdershoven have developed it and studied it in settings such as psychiatric care.[^s1] Its authors describe its roots as **dialogical ethics**, the view that moral insight comes from shared experience and dialogue rather than theory alone; Aristotle's **practical wisdom**, which starts from the particulars of a case and moves back and forth to general principles; and **pragmatic hermeneutics**, in which meaning is interpreted in context through stories. Several conversation methods are used, including a Socratic method aimed mainly at building participants' moral competence.[^s1] The most widely used is the **dilemma method**, a ten-step format for 8 to 12 participants that usually takes 60 to 90 minutes.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Professional work keeps producing cases where rules give no clear answer and colleagues disagree: whether to override a patient's refusal, how far to go with treatment, what to do when a policy fits a client badly. MCD's developers give it three aims: to improve what happens in the case itself, to strengthen professionals' moral competence and reflection on what it means to be a good professional, and to bring institutional problems into view.[^s1] Its secondary aims include helping to prevent moral distress and building team cohesion.[^s2]

It differs from asking an expert. An ethics consultant typically takes the expert's role and says what is right or who should decide, whereas an MCD facilitator guards a process through which the group reaches its own answers.[^s1] It also differs from shared decision-making with a patient: MCD usually takes place among caregivers, aims at mutual understanding rather than persuasion or consensus, and leaves responsibility for the decision with the treating professional.[^s3]

## How to apply it

In the dilemma method, the facilitator introduces the purpose and ground rules, a professional presents a case, and the group frames it as a concrete choice between two options, naming the harm each would cause. Participants ask factual questions to put themselves in the presenter's position, map the stakeholders and their values, and look for alternatives. Each then privately writes down which option they would choose, the value behind it, what they would sacrifice, how to limit that damage and what they would need. The group compares these answers in dialogue, the facilitator asks what has changed in people's views, and the session closes with an evaluation.[^s2] The facilitator stays neutral, keeps the exchange open and equal, and stresses that the aim is understanding rather than winning; consensus is not required, and a majority view is not thereby justified.[^s2]

The practice is not confined to hospitals: experienced Dutch facilitators report using it in settings from elderly care to prisons and business.[^s4] Any team whose rules run out on a hard case can use the same moves (state the dilemma, take the actor's position, map the values, decide individually, then inquire together) to structure its discussion.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a debate or a vote.** The point is to understand the reasons behind positions, not to tally them or win.[^s2]
- **Bringing an abstract issue instead of a real case.** The method depends on the particulars of a case someone is actually responsible for.[^s1]
- **Expecting the facilitator to give the answer.** That turns deliberation into consultation and weakens the learning it is meant to produce.[^s1]
- **Stopping at insight.** Professionals in one study were disappointed when sessions lacked follow-up, and the researchers added a step for translating conclusions into practice.[^s5]
- **Ignoring the costs.** Experienced participants listed disappointment with outcomes and a sense of wasted time among possible results of MCD.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

Most of the evidence is qualitative and self-reported. In a concept-mapping study, twelve experienced Dutch participants, mostly facilitators, grouped possible outcomes into eight clusters, from organisation and policy to team development, personal and professional growth, and concrete action.[^s4] A multiple-case study of eight sessions in a Dutch care organisation for people with intellectual disabilities found that professionals reported greater moral awareness and better collaboration with colleagues and relatives, and changed actions after half of the sessions; but quality of care was hard to see, one session left lasting stress, and the study relied on staff perceptions in a single organisation that funded it.[^s5] A Cochrane review of ethical case interventions for adult patients found only four randomised trials, all of ethics consultation in intensive care, none of MCD, and judged the certainty of evidence very low throughout.[^s6] MCD is best treated as a well-developed practitioner method whose effects have not been tested in controlled trials.

## Connections

MCD is **applied in** the situation of **wisdom in the professions**, where rules do not settle a case,[^s1] and its developers present it as a way of **developing practical wisdom** and **moral competence**, drawing on Aristotle's account of judgment that starts from particular cases.[^s1] It is **influenced by** **philosophical hermeneutics** and dialogical ethics, and **studied in** **medical ethics**.[^s1] It **contrasts with** **clinical ethics consultation**, where an expert advises,[^s1] and with **shared decision-making**, which involves the patient and aims at a joint decision.[^s3] Its developers hope it **addresses** **moral distress**, a hope the evidence has not yet tested.[^s2] The impact study **supports** its value for awareness and collaboration, with the limits noted above.[^s5] It is a group form of **deliberation** and **seeking counsel**, and it relies on **perspective-taking** and the **intellectual humility** to let a colleague's reasons change one's mind; like the **after-action review**, it turns team experience into shared learning.
