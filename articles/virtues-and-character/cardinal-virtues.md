# Cardinal virtues

The cardinal virtues are the four excellences of character that much of Western ethics treated as the hinges of a good life: practical wisdom or prudence, justice, courage or fortitude, and temperance or moderation. The name comes later than the list. Later Latin writers called them "cardinal" from the Latin *cardo*, a hinge, because the other virtues were thought to turn on them.[^s4] The idea is that a good person must see what is right, give others their due, hold firm against fear and keep desire in proportion, all at once.[^s3]

## Origin

The list first appears as a set in Plato's *Republic*. In Book IV, Socrates argues that a city rightly ordered will be wise, courageous, temperate and just. Wisdom is good counsel in the rulers; courage is the preservation of right belief about what is truly to be feared; temperance is agreement among the parts about which should rule; and justice is each part doing its own work.[^s1] He then finds the same structure in the individual soul, where reason should rule with spirit as its ally and appetite kept in order.[^s1]

The Stoics kept the four as the main kinds of virtue, usually named wisdom, justice, courage and moderation, each with subordinate virtues such as endurance under courage and self-control under moderation.[^s5] They also held that the virtues are inseparable: to have one fully is to have them all, as one person can be poet, orator and general.[^s5] Cicero's *De Officiis* passed this scheme to the Latin world, tracing all moral rightness to four sources: prudence, the social instinct expressed in justice, courage and temperance (I.152).[^s2] Christian writers then adopted it. Aquinas cites Ambrose and Gregory the Great for the four, and argues in *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 61 that every other moral virtue reduces to them.[^s3] In that tradition they sit alongside the theological virtues of faith, hope and charity, which Aquinas treats as given by grace rather than acquired.[^s4]

## Why it matters

The framework is a compact checklist for character. Aquinas explains why there are four: reason's good must show up in thinking itself (prudence), in dealings with others (justice), in restraining desires that pull us away from reason (temperance) and in standing firm against fears that push us away from it (fortitude).[^s3] Most failures of judgment can be placed under one of these headings: a misread situation, an unfair act, a loss of nerve or a loss of proportion. Cicero adds a point that matters in practice: because all four are sources of right action, they can pull against each other, and a hard choice may mean weighing one against another.[^s2] He warns, for example, that courage cut off from the bonds of human society turns into brutality.[^s2]

## How it works and how to use it

- **Prudence first.** Aquinas treats prudence as principal without qualification, since it is right reason about what to do and guides the other three.[^s3][^s4] Before acting, ask what the situation actually is and what would serve the good end you are pursuing.
- **Justice for what is owed.** Justice concerns relations with others: giving each their due, in exchanges and in how shared goods and burdens are distributed.[^s4] Ask who is affected and what you owe them.
- **Courage for what must be faced.** Fortitude moderates both fear and reckless daring.[^s4] Ask whether fear is bending your judgment, or bravado is replacing it.
- **Temperance for the right measure.** Temperance moderates pleasures and appetites.[^s4] Ask whether desire, comfort or the wish to be liked is setting the amount.
- **Use them together.** The Stoic and Thomist traditions both see the virtues as connected; Aquinas notes that passions can cloud judgment, so prudence itself depends on well-ordered desires.[^s5][^s4]

## Common misreadings

The four are not a complete list of the virtues. Aquinas says each cardinal virtue gathers related virtues under it, such as patience and perseverance under courage, or gratitude and truthfulness under justice.[^s4] Nor are they rules: they describe dispositions that need judgment in each case.[^s3] "Prudence" in this sense is not caution or self-protection but practical wisdom about acting well.[^s4] Finally, the traditions disagree on details: the Stoics treat the virtues as forms of perfected reason that stand or fall together, while Aquinas distinguishes them as separate habits and places them beneath charity, which in his account gives the moral virtues their final direction.[^s5][^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The cardinal virtues are a normative framework, judged by argument and long use rather than experiment. Psychology has tested a related claim: that a small set of virtues is valued everywhere. Reviewing texts from Confucianism, Taoism, Buddhism, Hinduism, Athenian philosophy, Judaism, Christianity and Islam, Dahlsgaard, Peterson and Seligman reported six recurring core virtues, four of which correspond to the classical list, plus humanity and transcendence.[^s6] Data from over a million respondents did not recover those six as factors, however; McGrath found that the 24 VIA character strengths group best into three broader dimensions, caring, inquisitiveness and self-control.[^s7] Lomas, analysing over 200 virtue-related words from many languages that lack direct English equivalents, argues that virtue research has been Western-centric and that other cultures carve the space differently.[^s8] These findings do not refute the classical framework as ethics, but they caution against presenting it as a universal or empirically confirmed structure of human character.[^s7][^s8]

## Connections

The framework was **introduced by** Plato **in** the *Republic*, and is **rooted in** Stoicism, which systematised its four kinds of virtue, and in Christianity, whose moral theology named and extended it.[^s1][^s5][^s3] Its claim to cross-cultural reach is **supported by** the shared-virtue study and **challenged by** McGrath's factor-analytic study and Lomas's lexical analysis.[^s6][^s7][^s8] It sits within **virtue ethics**, which you need first, and its members include **practical wisdom**, **courage**, **temperance** and **justice as a virtue**.[^s3] It relates to the **theological virtues**, which Aquinas sets beside it, and to the modern **VIA character strengths**, which reworked its categories.[^s4][^s6] It is built by **moral habituation** and echoes the **doctrine of the mean**: fortitude moderates both fear and rash daring.[^s4]
