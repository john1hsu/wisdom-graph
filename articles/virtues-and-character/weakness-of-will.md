# Weakness of will

Weakness of will, or *akrasia*, is acting freely and intentionally against your own judgment of what is better overall: you could do otherwise, you believe the other course is better, and you do this one anyway.[^s1] "Better judgment" here means your own all-things-considered verdict, not a superior verdict handed down by someone else.[^s1] Many philosophers now also count a second, everyday pattern: giving up a resolution you had good reason to keep, as when a plan to run before work dissolves once the alarm goes off.[^s1]

## Origin

The puzzle begins with Plato. In the *Protagoras*, Socrates argues that no one who knows or believes another course is better will keep to a worse one, so apparent akrasia must really be a kind of ignorance (*Protagoras* 358b–c).[^s1] Aristotle took the question up in *Nicomachean Ethics* Book VII. He grants that the Socratic view clashes with how things plainly seem, then concludes that Socrates was partly right: at the moment of action the akratic person's knowledge is present only in a way, like that of a sleeper, a drunk or an actor reciting lines.[^s2][^s3]

Aristotle distinguished two forms. In **impetuosity** (*propeteia*) a person acts on passion without deliberating at all and regrets it later; in **weakness** (*astheneia*) a person deliberates, reaches a choice, and is then carried away from it by appetite or anger.[^s3] He also placed akrasia carefully among other states of character. The self-controlled or continent person (*enkratēs*) feels the same pull but follows reason; the akratic person shares the conflict and yields; the vicious person feels no conflict because he has stopped valuing the good, so the akratic person tends to repent while the vicious one does not.[^s2][^s3] Modern philosophers such as Donald Davidson defended the possibility of akrasia against those who deny it, and Richard Holton recast ordinary weakness of will as the unreasonable abandonment of resolutions.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Wisdom is not only knowing what is good but acting on it. Aristotle held that the same person cannot be both practically wise and akratic, because practical wisdom includes the settled character that carries judgment into action.[^s2] He compared the akratic person to a city with good laws that "makes no use of them".[^s2] Most people will recognise the pattern: the apology you meant to make, the hard conversation you postponed again, the late-night message you knew you would regret. Weakness of will also explains why advice that is correct can still be useless if it never reaches the moment of choice.

## Evidence

Psychology confirms that a sincere intention is not enough. A meta-analysis of 47 experiments that changed people's intentions found that a medium-to-large shift in intention (d = 0.66) produced only a small-to-medium shift in behaviour (d = 0.36).[^s4] One influential explanation is that rewards which are near at hand feel disproportionately strong; George Ainslie's work on hyperbolic delay discounting is the best-known version, and philosophers increasingly draw on it.[^s1]

A popular explanation has fared worse. The "ego depletion" idea holds that willpower is a fuel that runs down with use, so lapses follow earlier effort. A preregistered replication across 23 laboratories and 2,141 participants found an effect close to zero (d = 0.04, with a confidence interval including zero).[^s6] Philosophers reviewing this literature note that the depletion model is increasingly questioned and that planning ahead and arranging one's surroundings often beat effortful resistance in the moment.[^s1] The phenomenon is real; claims about its mechanism and fixes are moderate in size and still debated.

## How to spot it

Aristotle's markers still work: you feel divided before acting, and afterwards you regret it rather than defend it.[^s2] Watch especially for the impetuous form if you are quick and excitable, since such people act on the first impression before reasoning has a chance to speak.[^s2] The resolution form often hides behind reasons that appear only when keeping the resolution becomes costly. Ask whether the new reason would have convinced you when you made the plan.[^s1]

## How to fix it

Aristotle observed that people who see a temptation coming and rouse their reasoning beforehand are not overcome, just as those who tickle first are not tickled themselves.[^s2] Modern research agrees. Turn a goal into an if–then plan that names when, where and how you will act; across 94 tests such implementation intentions had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d = 0.65), apparently by making the right cue easier to notice and the response more automatic.[^s5] Change the situation rather than relying on resistance alone: remove the cue, add a delay, or ask someone to hold you to account.[^s1] Review lapses afterwards without self-contempt, since Aristotle thought akrasia acquired through habit more curable than vice.[^s2] Finally, keep some humility about your verdict: philosophers note that occasionally the "better judgment" an akratic act defies is itself mistaken.[^s1]

## Connections

Weakness of will **undermines practical wisdom**, which for Aristotle is incompatible with akrasia,[^s2] and it **contrasts with self-control** (*enkrateia*), the state of feeling the same pull and following reason anyway.[^s3] It also **contrasts with temperance**, the virtue in which desire and judgment no longer conflict.[^s2] It is **explained in part by hyperbolic discounting**,[^s1] **demonstrated by the intention–behaviour gap meta-analysis**,[^s4] and the popular willpower-as-fuel explanation is **challenged by the multilab ego-depletion replication**.[^s6] It is **studied in the philosophy of action**.[^s1] Its impetuous form is close to **impetuosity**, which **deliberation** counters; **implementation intentions** are a practical remedy;[^s5] and the **evening review** and attention to **regret** help a person notice the pattern before it hardens.
