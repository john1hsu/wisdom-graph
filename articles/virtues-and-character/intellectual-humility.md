# Intellectual humility

Intellectual humility is the virtue of being properly attentive to the limits of your own knowledge and thinking, and of *owning* those limits rather than hiding, excusing or obsessing over them.[^s1] Philosophers Dennis Whitcomb, Heather Battaly, Jason Baehr and Daniel Howard-Snyder spell out what owning a limitation involves: believing you have it, admitting it to yourself and, when appropriate, to others, taking it seriously, and feeling some regret about it but not hostility.[^s1] It counts as a virtue, rather than mere self-doubt, when it flows from caring about truth and understanding.[^s1] Psychologists use a close cousin of this definition: recognising that a belief you hold might be wrong, and staying aware of the limits of your evidence and of your skill at judging it.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The ideal is old. In the *Analects*, Confucius asks his disciple Zilu (Yu) whether he should teach him what knowledge is, then says that holding that you know what you know, and allowing that you do not know what you do not, *is* knowledge (Book II.17).[^s5] Greek philosophy has a parallel in Socrates' teaching, covered in this graph as Socratic ignorance.

As a named topic of research, intellectual humility is recent. In 2015 four virtue epistemologists proposed the "limitations-owning" account, which places it between two failings: arrogance, which over-owns strengths and under-owns limitations, and servility or intellectual diffidence, which does the reverse.[^s1] Psychologists built questionnaires for it in the same decade, notably a six-item scale by Mark Leary and colleagues at Duke.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Wise judgment depends on knowing where your knowledge runs out. In four studies, Leary's team found that more intellectually humble people judged someone's character less harshly simply for disagreeing with them, told strong arguments from weak ones more accurately, and were less likely to condemn someone who changed position after new evidence.[^s2] Leary pointed to everyday stakes: quarrels with friends, partners and co-workers often turn on trivial points each side is certain about, and a boss low in the trait may ignore the team's ideas.[^s2] Reviews link intellectual humility to more tolerance of opponents, closer scrutiny of dubious claims, persistence after failure, and being seen as a more trustworthy leader.[^s3]

## What good looks like

The virtue is a mean, not a minimum. The intellectually humble person:

- **Owns limitations without hostility.** She admits she doesn't know a book or author rather than bluffing, and when a budget overruns, attributes it to her own weak estimating rather than to her staff or to the recession.[^s1]
- **Keeps strengths in proportion.** Arrogance is not the only failing; over-owning limitations, fixating on them or underrating real abilities, is the opposite vice.[^s1]
- **Stays open in disagreement.** People higher in the trait spend more of their reading time on views that oppose their own, and see opponents as having a perspective worth understanding rather than as unintelligent.[^s6]
- **Keeps conviction where it is earned.** A recent review warns that too much humility can slide into self-deprecation or gullibility, and that intellectual courage sometimes has to take precedence; practical wisdom judges which is called for.[^s4]

## How to practise it

- **Sort what you know from what you feel sure of**, in the spirit of *Analects* II.17, before you commit to a claim that matters.[^s5]
- **Name what would change your mind**, then go and read the strongest version of the other side.[^s6]
- **Treat ability as growable.** In one experiment, reading that intelligence can grow raised people's intellectual humility and their openness to opposing views, at least in the short term.[^s6]
- **Step back from the issue.** Reasoning about a personal or political question from a distanced, observer's vantage point has also increased humble reasoning in lab studies.[^s3]
- **Lower the threat.** Feeling threatened tends to close minds, so raise disagreements privately and calmly, and thank people who catch your errors.[^s3][^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is promising but thinner than popular coverage suggests. Most findings are correlational, so it is unclear how far intellectual humility causes better learning or relationships.[^s4] Measures rest heavily on self-report, which invites a "humility paradox": humble people may rate themselves modestly and arrogant people generously, and self-reported humility tracks well-calibrated confidence poorly.[^s3][^s4] Only a handful of interventions have raised the trait, mostly briefly and in adults, and definitions still vary across studies.[^s3][^s4] Researchers also caution that training people to doubt themselves could help the overconfident while harming groups already prone to underconfidence, and that cultures may express the virtue differently.[^s4] Its status here is therefore **mixed**.

## Connections

Intellectual humility **addresses intellectual arrogance**, the vice of over-owning strengths and under-owning limitations,[^s1] and works against **dogmatism**, with which humility scores are negatively related.[^s2] It is **rooted in Confucianism** through *Analects* II.17,[^s5] and is cultivated by **Socratic ignorance** and by **seeking counsel**. It is **applied in interpersonal conflict**, where it predicts warmer views of opponents and greater willingness to listen to them.[^s3] It **complements intellectual courage**, since practical wisdom must sometimes favour standing firm.[^s4] It is **studied in virtue epistemology**,[^s1] and sits beside **open-mindedness**, a related but distinct trait,[^s3] **self-distancing**, which strengthens it,[^s3] and **wise reasoning**, of which it is a core part.
