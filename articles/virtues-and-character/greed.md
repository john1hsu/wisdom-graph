# Greed

Greed is the desire for more that does not switch off once a need is met. Psychologists who have studied how ordinary people use the word find that two features sit at its centre: being self-interested and never being satisfied; ambition, by contrast, is only a peripheral feature, which is why people can tell a driven colleague from a greedy one.[^s2] Researchers now describe greed as an insatiable, acquisitive drive that can attach to money, possessions, status or power, and treat it as a stable disposition that people have in different degrees rather than as a rare moral monster.[^s3]

## Origin

Most wisdom traditions name greed as a central failing. In the Buddhist discourse on the roots (AN 3.69), greed (*lobha*) is the first of three roots of unskilful action, alongside aversion and delusion; the text says a person overcome by it harms others through seizure, blame and coercion, speaks falsely, and lives in distress, while non-greed is a root of skilful action.[^s5] Aristotle approaches the same ground through his doctrine of the mean: generosity (liberality) is the right disposition towards giving and taking wealth, and its deficient vice, meanness, falls short in giving and exceeds in taking. He groups lenders at ruinous rates, gamblers and highway robbers together as people who take more than they ought and from the wrong sources, out of a sordid love of gain.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Greed distorts judgment about what is enough and whose interests count. In laboratory economic games, people higher in dispositional greed keep more money for themselves when dividing it with a stranger and take more from a shared pool that a group depends on.[^s1] Reviews of the wider research link the trait to less helping, more willingness to take bribes (especially where corruption is common), and more hoarding during the COVID-19 pandemic; among chief executives, greedier leaders have been associated with lower shareholder returns and less investment in social responsibility.[^s3] The cost is not only to others: greed is reliably associated with lower life satisfaction, and its link to dissatisfaction runs partly through envy.[^s3][^s1]

## How to spot it

The clearest sign is the moving finish line: a goal is reached and immediately replaced, with no felt satisfaction in between.[^s2] Other markers include treating a gain as worth having regardless of who bears its cost, comparing one's holdings with those of others rather than with one's needs, and feeling envious when someone else does well.[^s3][^s1] The Buddhist text adds a telling sign in speech: under the pull of wanting, a person tends to deny facts that do not suit them and stops examining claims that are convenient.[^s5] At work, greed often looks respectable, as aggressive target-chasing or deal-making, which is why the useful question is not "how ambitious is this?" but "does this ever reach enough, and who pays for it?"[^s2][^s3]

## How to fix it

Practical moves that follow from these accounts:

- **Define "enough" before the opportunity arises.** Because insatiability is the core of greed, a pre-committed stopping point, such as a target, a budget or a number of hours, supplies the satisfaction signal that greed suppresses.[^s2]
- **Count the other side's losses.** Greed shows up as taking more from shared resources and from strangers, so make those losses visible: ask who bears the cost, and how you would justify the split to them.[^s1]
- **Practise the opposite habit.** In Aristotle's scheme the mean between meanness and prodigality is generosity: giving to the right people, in the right amounts and at the right time, and taking only from proper sources.[^s4] The Buddhist text likewise frames non-greed as a root of skilful action to be cultivated, not merely the absence of a fault.[^s5]
- **Change the norms around you.** Greed predicts corruption more strongly where corruption is common, which suggests that teams and organisations can weaken its effects by making fair dealing the visible norm.[^s3]

Two cautions. Aristotle thought meanness harder to cure than prodigality, because it is more deeply rooted in people, so expect slow progress.[^s4] And the evidence for specific interventions is thin: reviewers report only preliminary findings (for example, on mindful parenting and adolescent greed) and call for more research.[^s3]

## Evidence

The modern research programme rests on a seven-item Dispositional Greed Scale, validated across four samples from two countries (over 6,000 people); greed correlated with materialism, envy, self-interest and impulsiveness, negatively with self-control and life satisfaction, and predicted greedy choices in dictator, ultimatum and resource-dilemma games.[^s1] Greed is not all bad for the greedy: greedier people tend to work harder and in some samples report higher household income, though also more debt and less saving among adolescents.[^s3]

The limits are real. Much of the evidence is correlational and drawn from Dutch samples; the popular claim that greed caused the 2008 financial crisis rests mostly on anecdote; and the economists' argument that greed creates wealth for everyone is, the reviewers note, not well documented.[^s3] One prominent experiment also failed to hold up: a four-study replication of a famous finding that a "greed is good" prime erased the link between social class and cheating found that the prime raised favourable attitudes to greed and modestly increased cheating in most samples, but did not reproduce the class-by-greed interaction.[^s6] The trait is real and measurable, but its causes and cures remain poorly understood.

## Connections

In Aristotle's scheme greed sits on the deficient side of **generosity**, as the disposition that gives too little and takes too much.[^s4] In early Buddhism it is one of the **three unwholesome roots**, with aversion and delusion.[^s5] The trait was established in the **dispositional greed** studies,[^s1] and it is a strong predictor of **envy** and of lower **well-being**.[^s3] Its defining insatiability sets it against **contentment**, the settled sense that what one has is enough.[^s2] Elsewhere in this graph, Epicurus's **classification of desires**, **simplicity** and **memento mori** are presented as remedies for it, and **hedonic adaptation** helps explain why each new gain soon stops feeling like enough.
