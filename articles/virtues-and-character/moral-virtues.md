# Moral virtues

Moral virtues, also called virtues of character or ethical virtues, are the stable dispositions that make someone good at feeling and acting well: courage, temperance, generosity, justice, truthfulness and their kin. In Aristotle's account they are not feelings or inborn capacities but states of character, which is why we are praised or blamed for them.[^s1] Each one concerns choice and lies in a mean relative to the person and situation, a mean fixed by reason as a person of practical wisdom would fix it.[^s1]

## Origin

The term comes from Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*. He divides the soul into a part that reasons and a part that cannot reason itself but can listen to reason, and assigns a different kind of excellence to each: intellectual virtues such as theoretical and practical wisdom to the first, ethical or character virtues to the second.[^s3] The two kinds are also acquired differently. Intellectual virtue grows mostly through teaching and needs time and experience, while moral virtue comes from habit; Aristotle notes that the Greek word *ēthikē* is itself derived from *ethos*, habit (*NE* II.1).[^s1] We are fitted by nature to receive the moral virtues, but do not have them by nature: we become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave ones.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The idea shifts the ethical question from "what rule applies?" to "what kind of person am I becoming?". A virtue in this sense is deep: it shapes what a person notices, values, feels and chooses, not only what they do.[^s5] An honest person avoids deceit because they see dishonesty as a reason in itself, not because they fear being caught, and that same trait shows in the friends they choose and the deceptions they disapprove of.[^s5] This is why one act rarely reveals character, and why moral virtue is built over years of practice rather than switched on by resolution.[^s5][^s1]

## Key features

- **Feeling as well as action.** Virtue means having fear, confidence, anger or pity at the right times, toward the right people, for the right reasons and in the right way (*NE* II.6).[^s1]
- **Pleasure as a sign.** Aristotle treats the pleasure or pain that follows an act as an indicator of character: the temperate person enjoys going without excess, while someone who resents it is not yet temperate (*NE* II.3).[^s1]
- **Virtue versus continence.** The fully virtuous person does the right thing without inner struggle; the continent person does it while fighting contrary desires, which is admirable but falls short.[^s3][^s5]
- **Three conditions.** A just act does not make its doer just unless they act knowingly, choose the act for its own sake and act from a firm character (*NE* II.4).[^s1]

## Moral virtue and practical wisdom

Aristotle insists that moral virtue and practical wisdom need each other. Virtue makes the goal right, while practical wisdom finds the right way to it; cleverness alone can reach any goal, good or bad (*NE* VI.12).[^s2] Children and good-natured adults may have "natural virtue", kindly or brave dispositions that can still go wrong without judgment to steer them.[^s2][^s5] Strict virtue appears only when practical wisdom is added, and once it is present the virtues come together, so they cannot be fully separated (*NE* VI.13).[^s2] Socrates, Aristotle says, was right that the virtues involve reason but wrong to think they are simply forms of knowledge.[^s2] Scholars still debate how strong this "unity of the virtues" thesis is meant to be.[^s3]

## Common misreadings

Moral virtue is not niceness or rule-following; a generous person who gives at the wrong time or to the wrong person has missed the mean.[^s1] It is not the same as the four cardinal virtues: Aristotle's list is longer and includes generosity, magnificence and greatness of soul.[^s3] And it is not reserved for saints. Aristotle presents it as an ideal approached by degrees, starting from the low-grade virtue that good upbringing provides.[^s3] Virtue ethics in this mould also has non-Western counterparts, notably in Confucius and Mencius, and in recent work on Buddhist and Hindu ethics.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

As a philosophical account, moral virtue is judged by argument rather than experiment. But it assumes people can have stable, cross-situational character traits, and that assumption has been tested. Philosophers such as Gilbert Harman and John Doris argued that social psychology, including bystander and obedience studies, shows most people lack such global traits, which would undermine Aristotelian virtue ethics.[^s4] Some of the evidence is shakier than once thought: the classic "dime in the phone booth" helping study has had replication problems, and the Stanford prison experiment has drawn serious criticism.[^s4] Defenders reply that full virtue was always meant to be rare, that consistency is relative to the agent's own view of situations, and that most people have mixed traits rather than none.[^s4][^s5] Experience-sampling research adds that people's moral behaviour varies a great deal from moment to moment yet their average levels stay strikingly stable over time, and virtue measures predict outcomes beyond broad personality traits.[^s6] How fully personality science can capture practical wisdom remains an open question.[^s6]

## Connections

Moral virtues were **introduced by** Aristotle **in** the *Nicomachean Ethics* and are **rooted in** Aristotelian virtue ethics, where they **contrast with** the **intellectual virtues**.[^s1][^s3] They **complement** **practical wisdom**, without which they remain only natural virtue.[^s2] The concept is **challenged by** **situationism** and partly **supported by** **whole trait theory** and related work on the stability of virtue.[^s4][^s6] It sits within **virtue ethics** and is related to the **cardinal virtues**, to specific virtues such as **courage**, **temperance**, **honesty** and **compassion**, to the **doctrine of the mean** that defines each virtue, to **moral habituation**, by which they are acquired, and to **weakness of will**, which shows what falls short of them.[^s1][^s3][^s5]
