# Intellectual virtues

Intellectual virtues are excellences of the mind: the stable states and traits that make a person good at reaching truth and understanding, and at thinking well about what to do. A widely used minimal description calls them traits that make for intellectual flourishing, or "which make for an excellent cognizer".[^s2] The idea has two main homes: Aristotle's ethics, where they stand beside the virtues of character, and contemporary virtue epistemology, which studies the traits of a good inquirer.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

In Book VI of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle asks by which states the soul grasps truth, and names five.[^s1] Scientific knowledge (*epistēmē*) demonstrates conclusions about what cannot be otherwise (VI.3). Craft or art (*technē*) is a reasoned capacity to make things (VI.4). Practical wisdom (*phronēsis*) is a reasoned capacity to act well about human goods, to deliberate well about what is good for oneself and for people generally (VI.5). Intuitive reason (*nous*) grasps the first principles the others start from (VI.6), and theoretical or philosophic wisdom (*sophia*) joins intuitive reason to scientific knowledge about the highest things (VI.7).[^s1] He adds supporting excellences: good deliberation, which reaches the right conclusion in the right way and at the right time (VI.9); understanding, which judges what others say about practical matters (VI.10); and considerate judgement about what is fair (VI.11).[^s1] Cleverness, the knack of hitting whatever target one sets, is not a virtue: it serves good and bad ends alike, and practical wisdom needs it without being reducible to it (VI.12).[^s1]

In the late twentieth century philosophers revived the term. Virtue reliabilists such as Ernest Sosa and John Greco treat intellectual virtues as reliable cognitive faculties like perception and memory; virtue responsibilists such as Lorraine Code, James Montmarquet and Linda Zagzebski treat them as character traits of a responsible thinker, such as conscientiousness and open-mindedness.[^s2] Baehr's survey lists inquisitiveness, fair-mindedness, open-mindedness, carefulness, thoroughness, tenacity, attentiveness, intellectual courage, honesty, integrity and reflectiveness.[^s3] Zagzebski models these traits on Aristotle's moral virtues: each has a motivation component, ultimately a desire for truth and understanding, and a success component, reliably reaching what it aims at.[^s3] Both Greco and Baehr argue that the reliabilist and responsibilist camps are less far apart than they look and that a full account needs both.[^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

Intellectual virtues are where wisdom and good thinking meet. Practical and theoretical wisdom are themselves intellectual virtues in Aristotle's scheme, which is why this graph places them under this heading.[^s1] And Aristotle's remark that the young can master mathematics but rarely practical wisdom, because it depends on experience of particulars, explains why intelligence alone does not make someone wise (VI.8).[^s1] The responsibilist traits matter because they govern how a person treats evidence and other people's views when the answer is not given in advance: whether they keep looking, notice their own blind spots and hear out someone who disagrees.[^s3][^s2] Recent work also stresses a social side, such as the virtue of giving speakers fair credibility and the vice of discounting testimony because of who is speaking, which Miranda Fricker calls epistemic injustice.[^s2]

## Cultivating them

Aristotle says scientific knowledge can be taught, starting from what the learner already knows (VI.3), while practical wisdom needs long experience of particular cases (VI.8).[^s1] Some virtue epistemologists take a similarly practical, educative line, aiming to diagnose characteristic thinking failures, improve training and reform intellectual culture rather than only define knowledge.[^s2] At work, this can mean asking in a review not only whether a forecast was right but whether it was reached carefully, whether dissent was heard and whether the evidence that could have changed it was sought. The best-supported short exercise for one such trait is self-distanced reflection, which in several studies increased intellectual humility.[^s4]

## Common misreadings

Intellectual virtue is not intelligence, expertise or debating skill; a very clever person can be closed-minded or careless, and Aristotle denies that cleverness is a virtue at all.[^s1][^s3] Nor is it a list of thinking techniques. On the responsibilist view the motive matters: open-mindedness practised only to win an argument is not the virtue.[^s3] Finally, intellectual and moral virtues are not separate compartments. For Aristotle practical wisdom and moral virtue cannot exist fully without each other, so poor character distorts judgement and poor judgement misdirects good intentions (VI.12-13).[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The concept itself is philosophical, so its status here is normative. Empirical research has examined some intellectual virtues, intellectual humility most of all. A 2022 review co-authored by Igor Grossmann links it to less myside bias, more openness to opposing views, better discrimination between strong and weak arguments and more scrutiny of misinformation.[^s4] The authors stress the limits: most studies are cross-sectional and self-reported, samples are mostly Western, there is no validated behavioural task yet, most intervention effects are small and short-lived, and classroom programmes have shown limited and inconclusive results.[^s4] A deeper challenge is epistemic situationism. Mark Alfano argues that studies showing people's reasoning swayed by trivial factors such as mood and social cues imply few people have robust intellectual virtues. Replies include aiming first to avoid intellectual vices and relying on good environments and communities to scaffold good thinking.[^s2]

## Connections

Intellectual virtues were **introduced by** Aristotle **in** the *Nicomachean Ethics*, where they contrast with the **moral virtues**; practical wisdom and theoretical wisdom are two of them, and **cleverness** is the non-virtuous capacity practical wisdom relies on.[^s1] Responsibilists model them on Aristotle's character virtues, so they are **rooted in** Aristotelian virtue ethics.[^s3] They are **studied in** **virtue epistemology**, which counts **intellectual humility**, **intellectual courage** and **open-mindedness** among them, and they are **challenged by** **situationism**.[^s2] The review of **intellectual humility research** partly **supports** the claim that such traits affect how people reason and relate, and points to **self-distancing** as a practical route.[^s4] They connect to **deliberation**, **wise reasoning** and **teaching for wisdom**, and they oppose **dogmatism**.[^s1][^s3][^s4]
