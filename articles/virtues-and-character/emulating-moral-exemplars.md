steps:
  - Pick two or three people whose judgment you trust in a domain that matters to you now (a manager who handles conflict well, a relative who stays fair under pressure), and prefer people close to your own situation over distant heroes.
  - Watch for a specific moment where they acted well, and write down what they noticed, what they did and what they chose not to do.
  - Ask them, or work out, the reasons behind it, so that you learn the ideal the action served rather than only the gesture.
  - When you feel the sting of falling short, name the specific good you lack and one small step toward it this week, instead of looking for faults in them.
  - Try the step in a comparable situation of your own, adapted to your circumstances, then compare the result with what they did and why.
  - Widen and update your set of exemplars over time, and drop any you find yourself copying blindly or defending against all criticism.

# Emulating moral exemplars

Emulating moral exemplars means learning good judgment and character from people who already have them: studying what a wiser person does in a concrete situation, grasping why, and letting the discomfort of falling short push you toward acting that way yourself.[^s3][^s4] It is one of the oldest answers to a puzzle about practical wisdom: if good judgment cannot be reduced to rules, how does anyone acquire it? Aristotle's suggestion is that we first understand practical wisdom by looking at the people we credit with it.[^s2]

## Origin

In *Rhetoric* II.11 Aristotle describes emulation (*zēlos*) as a pain we feel when people whose nature is like our own have goods we could have but lack, such as courage, wisdom or honour. What separates it from envy is where the pain points: the emulous person tries to get the good for themselves, while the envious person wants the other to lose it. Aristotle calls emulation "a good feeling felt by good persons", and envy its bad counterpart.[^s1] In *Nicomachean Ethics* VI.5 he approaches practical wisdom itself by studying the persons to whom we attribute it.[^s2]

Modern virtue-ethical educators revived the idea. Kristján Kristjánsson argued that role-modelling in character education faced unanswered questions about how to inspire learners and what to teach them, and that Aristotle's account of emulation helps answer both.[^s3] Emerald Henderson has since argued that emulation is best seen as a virtue that includes action, not only a feeling, and that it develops in stages.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Practical wisdom is a skill of seeing what matters in particular situations, which is exactly what rules and lectures convey poorly. Henderson calls the result "entangled phronesis": a learner who lacks practical wisdom can, for a time, lean on an exemplar's judgment, at first by following their lead and later by reasoning alongside them, with the learner relying on the exemplar less as their own judgment matures.[^s4] This addresses a real paradox: the people who most need guidance are the ones who cannot yet tell good guidance from bad on their own.[^s4]

The same logic applies wherever judgment is learned on the job or at home: a newer colleague watching how a senior one handles a hard conversation, or a young person seeing how a parent stays fair under strain. Kristjánsson's point is that such modelling only educates if learners are moved to emulate and know what they are emulating, which is why the method needs an account of emulation and not just a supply of good examples.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Choose exemplars whose situations resemble yours, and look at specific moments rather than reputations. Ask what they noticed and why they acted, so that you take away the ideal behind the action, not the surface style. Henderson stresses that role models represent virtues rather than embody them perfectly, so the aim is to pursue the ideal, not to become the person.[^s4] When you feel the pang of comparison, convert it into one achievable step; her account treats the judgment that an ideal is both worthwhile and attainable as what keeps admiration from collapsing into paralysis.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Blind imitation or hero-worship.** Copying a person's mannerisms, or defending everything they do, is the standard objection to role-modelling; emulation aims at the ideals they stand for.[^s4]
- **Choosing exemplars too far above you.** Research reviewed by Han and colleagues suggests that very extreme moral figures can feel threatening and provoke resentment or self-defence rather than effort.[^s5]
- **Letting comparison turn into envy.** Aristotle's line between the two is whether you try to rise or want the other brought down.[^s1]
- **Treating admiration as enough.** Feeling moved by a story is not the same as acting differently; the measured effects on behaviour are smaller than the effects on feeling.[^s6]

## Evidence

The empirical work tests a narrower question than practical wisdom: whether exemplar stories increase prosocial behaviour. In a small lab study and an eight-week school study in Korea, stories of attainable, relatable peers produced more moral elevation and more voluntary service than stories of extraordinary historical figures; the authors note small samples, self-report, non-random class assignment and confounded conditions.[^s5] A later set of studies (one preregistered, the others with larger online and student samples) found that relatable recent graduates inspired more than famous moral heroes, but story type had no significant effect on donation or volunteering intentions, and relatability rather than attainability seemed to matter.[^s6] Whether emulation builds practical wisdom itself has not been tested directly; Henderson acknowledges that her model's psychological realism is untested.[^s4] The practice therefore rests on a long philosophical tradition plus mixed, small-scale evidence.

## Connections

The practice **develops** practical wisdom, on Henderson's account of entangled phronesis,[^s4] and is **rooted in** Aristotelian virtue ethics, as Kristjánsson's revival makes explicit.[^s3] It was **introduced in** Aristotle's *Rhetoric* and **contrasts with** envy, the vice that wants the other person's good destroyed rather than matched.[^s1] It is **supported by** Han and colleagues' 2017 study of attainable exemplars[^s5] and **challenged by** their 2022 studies, which found null effects on prosocial intentions,[^s6] work **studied in** moral psychology.[^s6] It sits close to seeking counsel and teaching for wisdom, which also learn from more experienced people, and to self-reflection, which turns what you have observed into change.[^s3][^s4]
