# Temperance

Temperance is the virtue of wanting and enjoying bodily pleasures well: in the right amount, at the right time and in the right way. Aristotle defines it as observing the mean with regard to pleasures, and he narrows its field to the pleasures of touch and taste that humans share with other animals, such as eating, drinking and sex; delight in music, pictures, learning or honour is not what temperance governs.[^s1] Its vice of excess is self-indulgence (Ross's "profligacy"); its vice of deficiency, caring too little for pleasure, he calls insensibility and thinks rare.[^s2] Modern usage is broader. The VIA classification of character strengths uses "temperance" for a family of strengths, including self-regulation, that help people manage habits and guard against excess.[^s9]

## Origin

The Greek word is *sōphrosynē*, often translated "moderation" or "sound-mindedness". Plato's *Charmides* tries to define it and fails instructively: it is proposed in turn as quietness, as modesty, as doing one's own business, as self-knowledge in the spirit of the Delphic "know thyself", and finally as a knowledge of knowledge, before Socrates leaves the question open.[^s4] Scholars read the dialogue as pairing two elements, a calm temper and self-knowledge, that a full account would need to unite.[^s5] In the *Republic*, moderation becomes one of four central virtues alongside wisdom, courage and justice, and is pictured as a harmony in which every part of the soul agrees about which part should rule.[^s5] This list later became the cardinal virtues. Aristotle made the idea more concrete and more modest: temperance is a settled disposition about bodily appetite, a mean found by right reason rather than a rule.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Appetite is constant, so the chances to get it wrong recur every day. Aristotle notes that self-indulgence is more voluntary than cowardice, since we are drawn by pleasure rather than driven by fear, and that for the same reason it is easier to train oneself out of: the occasions are frequent and the practice carries no danger.[^s3] He also warns that indulged appetite tends to grow until it crowds out reasoning, which is why he wants appetite to listen to reason the way a child listens to a tutor.[^s3] Judgment that cannot govern desire stays theoretical: a person can know what is good for them and still lose evenings to habits they would not choose on reflection.

## What good looks like

The temperate person is not someone fighting cravings with gritted teeth. Aristotle describes someone who desires *what he ought, and as he ought, and when he ought*, whose appetites agree with reason.[^s3] Such a person is not pained when a pleasure is absent, and does not delight in what the self-indulgent person craves.[^s2] Nor is temperance asceticism: the temperate person welcomes pleasures that support health and good condition, and others besides, as long as they do no harm, are not ignoble and are within their means.[^s2] That marks the difference from people who merely restrain themselves: on Plato's and Aristotle's picture, the virtue lies in the agreement of the parts of the self, not in one part constantly overpowering another.[^s5][^s3]

## How to practise it

- Start with the recurring, low-stakes cases. Because temptations of appetite come back daily and carry little risk, they are good training ground, as Aristotle himself observes.[^s3]
- Shape situations before you are in them. Everyday-life data suggest that people high in self-control experience weaker desires and less conflict, and resist less often rather than more successfully, which points to arranging life to meet fewer temptations.[^s8]
- Build automatic routines. Trait self-control is more strongly linked to automatic, habitual behaviour than to deliberate, effortful acts, so steady routines may serve better than heroic willpower.[^s7]
- Check the reason behind the pleasure. Ask whether this amount supports health, your commitments and your means, the tests Aristotle sets for the pleasures a temperate person accepts.[^s2]
- Watch for joylessness as well as excess. Insensibility is also a failing, and refusing every pleasure is not the virtue.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Temperance is a normative ideal, judged by argument and long practice, but its modern cousin, trait self-control, has been studied extensively. In the Dunedin cohort of about 1,000 children followed to age 32, lower childhood self-control predicted poorer health, substance dependence, money problems and criminal convictions in a graded way across the whole range, even after accounting for IQ and social class; among about 500 sibling pairs, the sibling with lower self-control fared worse.[^s6] A meta-analysis of 102 studies (N = 32,648) found a small-to-medium link between trait self-control and behaviour, stronger for automatic than for controlled behaviour.[^s7] In an experience-sampling study of 205 adults reporting 7,827 desires, about half of desires conflicted at least somewhat with other goals, and people high in self-control reported weaker desires and less conflict, not better resistance.[^s8] These findings fit Aristotle's view that the temperate person's desires are already in tune with judgment, but they are largely correlational and measure self-control, not the full virtue.[^s6][^s8] Compulsive or addictive patterns are a reason to seek professional support, not just to try harder at moderation.

## Connections

Temperance is **rooted in** Aristotelian virtue ethics, which analyses it in *Nicomachean Ethics* III.10–12, and it belongs among the cardinal and moral virtues that Plato's *Republic* first grouped.[^s1][^s5] It **addresses** intemperance, or self-indulgence, its vice of excess, and insensibility, its rarer vice of deficiency, which makes it a central case of the **doctrine of the mean**.[^s2] It **requires** practical wisdom, since right reason fixes the mean in each case.[^s2] Its modern counterpart is **supported by** the Dunedin self-control gradient study, the trait self-control meta-analysis and the everyday-temptations study,[^s6][^s7][^s8] and it is **studied in** positive psychology through the VIA temperance strengths.[^s9] The *Charmides* links it to **know thyself**.[^s4] It contrasts with **weakness of will** and with mere **self-control**, where desire and judgment still conflict, and it is built by **moral habituation** and supported by tools such as **implementation intentions**.
