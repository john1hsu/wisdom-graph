# Patience

Patience is the virtue of bearing what is slow, irritating, painful or provoking without letting it take over one's judgment. Thomas Aquinas, following Augustine, describes it as the disposition by which a person bears evil *with an equal mind*, so that sorrow does not make them abandon the goods they are pursuing.[^s1] Modern psychology uses a close cousin of that definition: Sarah Schnitker defines patience as the propensity to wait calmly in the face of frustration or adversity.[^s2] Patience is not the same as passivity. It governs how one reacts, not whether one acts, and on Aquinas's account it does not forbid resisting a wrongdoer when that is necessary.[^s1]

## Origin

In the Christian tradition, patience is praised in the New Testament and given a full treatment by Augustine, whose treatise *De Patientia* Aquinas quotes throughout *Summa Theologiae* II-II, question 136.[^s1] Aquinas argues that patience is a moral virtue because sorrow, more than most passions, can push reason off course; he classes it as a secondary virtue attached to fortitude, since fortitude faces the gravest dangers while patience faces any evil, especially those inflicted by others.[^s1] He also separates it from longanimity, or long-suffering, which looks to a distant good and is bound up with hope and the sorrow of delay.[^s1]

Buddhist traditions treat patience (*kṣānti* in Sanskrit, *khanti* in Pali) as one of the perfections a practitioner develops. In the Pali texts collected in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's study guide, patient endurance is called the foremost austerity (Dhammapada 184), and the simile of the saw (Majjhima Nikāya 21) asks monks to keep their minds free of hatred even under extreme abuse.[^s8] The eighth-century Mahāyāna poet Śāntideva devotes the sixth chapter of his *Bodhicaryāvatāra* to patience, opening with the claim that no evil equals anger and no austerity equals patience (6.2), and distinguishing patience with one's own suffering, patience in gaining certainty about the teachings, and patience with harm from others.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Delay and friction are constant in work and family life, so the chance to react badly recurs every day. Śāntideva's best-known argument is a test anyone can use: if a problem can be fixed, there is no need for dejection, and if it cannot, dejection does no good (6.10).[^s6] Stephen Harris reads the chapter as arguing that anger always harms the person who has it, and that it feeds on discontent; remove the discontent and the anger loses its fuel.[^s7] In everyday terms, patience keeps a person able to think in exactly the moments when thinking is hardest.

## What good looks like

The patient person still notices the delay or the insult and still cares about it. What differs is that the feeling does not dictate the response. Psychologist Kate Sweeny proposes treating impatience as a distinct emotion that signals a goal being blocked, and patience as the skilful regulation of that emotion, rather than its absence.[^s4] Schnitker's research separates three forms: patience with other people, with long-running life hardships, and with daily hassles such as queues and traffic, which relate somewhat differently to well-being and personality.[^s2] A person can be patient with a toddler and impatient in traffic, so the virtue is built domain by domain.

## How to practise it

- Ask Śāntideva's two questions when frustration rises: can this be remedied, and if not, what will agitation add?[^s6]
- Reframe the wait. The Greater Good Science Center suggests treating, for example, fifteen minutes lost to a late colleague as time to read or prepare.[^s5]
- Name the feeling and its trigger, then regulate it. Schnitker's two-week training, as described by Greater Good, combined identifying feelings and triggers, regulating emotions, empathising with difficult people and meditation.[^s5]
- Practise on small provocations. Buddhist teachers treat the person who wrongs you as an occasion for practising patience; Śāntideva calls enemies the causes of one's patience.[^s6]
- Check that patience is not avoidance. Aquinas's point that patience does not rule out resisting wrongdoing is a useful test for whether you are being patient or merely conflict-averse.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Patience is a normative ideal, but trait patience has a growing empirical literature. Schnitker's 2012 paper reported three studies: a new three-factor patience scale, a semester-long study in which goal pursuit and achievement partly explained the link between patience and well-being, and a training study in which an intervention raised trait patience and lowered depression relative to a control.[^s2] Greater Good reports the survey sample as nearly 400 undergraduates and the training sample as 71.[^s5] In a 2026 paper, Sweeny and Schnitker found in one exploratory study (N = 799) and three preregistered conceptual replications that more patient people worried less and felt more positive emotion during stressful waits; the associations were consistent but modest, with correlations around .1 to .3.[^s3] Most of this evidence is correlational and drawn from students, and the training effect comes from a single small study, so claims that patience reliably makes people healthier or happier outrun the data.[^s2][^s3] Sweeny's review frames patience research as a young field still building its theory.[^s4] Patience is also not a remedy for every hardship: prolonged pain, grief or distress is a reason to seek professional support rather than simply to endure.

## Connections

Patience is **rooted in** Christianity, through Augustine and Aquinas, and in **Buddhism**, as the perfection of *khanti* or *kṣānti* taught in the Pali texts and by **Śāntideva**.[^s1][^s8][^s6] Aquinas makes it **part of** fortitude, or **courage**, and contrasts its domain with **temperance**, which concerns the sorrow of going without pleasures rather than evils inflicted by others.[^s1] It **addresses** **anger**, the central target of Śāntideva's chapter, and **impatience**, which Sweeny models as the emotion that patience regulates, so it is **explained by** **emotion regulation**.[^s7][^s4] Its modern study is **supported by** Schnitker's patience and well-being study and Sweeny and Schnitker's waiting-period studies, and it is **studied in** **positive psychology**.[^s2][^s3][^s5] Interpersonal patience is **applied in** **interpersonal conflict**.[^s5] It sits close to **equanimity**, **acceptance** and **cognitive reappraisal**, to Seneca's *De Ira*, and to **kairos** and **knowing when to quit**, which mark where waiting stops being wise.
