# Honesty

Honesty is usually reduced to "not lying", but philosophers who study it treat it as a broader trait of character. Christian B. Miller, whose 2021 book calls honesty a neglected virtue, describes its core as reliably not intentionally distorting the facts as you take them to be.[^s1] On that view honesty stands against a whole family of failings: lying, cheating, stealing, breaking promises, misleading, hypocrisy, bullshitting and even deceiving yourself.[^s1] As a virtue rather than a habit or a policy, it also involves the right motives: someone who tells the truth only because lying would not pay is careful, not honest.[^s1]

## Origin

Aristotle discusses a narrower cousin of the virtue in *Nicomachean Ethics* IV.7. His truthful person is a mean between the boaster, who claims more than he has, and the self-deprecator, who disowns what he has; such a person presents himself as he is, and if anything leans towards understatement.[^s3] Aristotle stresses that this is not honesty in contracts or in court, which belongs to justice, but truthfulness in ordinary talk about oneself, and argues that someone *truthful where nothing depends upon it* will be all the more truthful when much does.[^s3] The modern study of honesty as a virtue revived only recently; Miller's work at Wake Forest brings philosophy together with psychological research on lying and cheating, and with applied work on feedback, negotiation and public communication.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Philosophers distinguish lying, which needs a statement you believe false, from deception, which is any intentional causing of a false belief, including by gesture, silence or a true but misleading remark.[^s4] That distinction matters in practice, because much everyday dishonesty takes the second form: the selective fact, the technically true answer, the strategic omission.[^s4] Honesty as a virtue covers both, which is why it asks more than a rule against lying.[^s1] It also turns inward: refusing to distort the facts includes not telling yourself a flattering story about your own conduct, which ties honesty to self-knowledge and to the clear sight that wise judgment needs.[^s1]

## What good looks like

An honest person is reliable across situations, not just when watched, and shows it in what they say, do and imply.[^s1][^s2] In Aristotle's picture they neither inflate nor play down their own record.[^s3] Wise honesty is also not the same as saying every true thing at once. Choosing *what* to raise, *when* and *how gently* is a matter of practical judgment; misleading someone about the substance is not.

## How to practise it

- Audit implication, not just words: before answering a hard question, ask what the listener will come away believing, and whether that belief is true.[^s4]
- Separate tact from evasion. Delay or soften delivery if that helps the other person, but do not swap the facts for a comfortable story.[^s5]
- Pair hard truths with care and a next step, so honesty reads as goodwill rather than point-scoring.[^s5]
- Watch the small cases. Aristotle's point that truthfulness in low-stakes talk shapes behaviour in high-stakes talk suggests practising on minor exaggerations and self-promotion.[^s3]
- Do not rely on gimmicks. One widely adopted nudge, signing an honesty pledge at the top of a form, did not reduce dishonesty in large replications.[^s6]

## Evidence and limits

Honesty as a virtue is a normative ideal, judged by argument and long practice, but empirical work bears on how far people live up to it. Reviewing studies of lying and cheating, Miller concludes that most people are neither honest nor dishonest in the full sense: they have a mixed character, with a considerable gap between how they act and how an honest person would.[^s1][^s2] Diary research points the same way from another angle. In a study by Timothy Levine, Kim Serota and Tony Docan-Morgan, more than 630 people logged their lies for three months; about three-quarters told between zero and two a day, while roughly 6 per cent told more than six a day and accounted for a large share of all lies, and most people lied more on some days than others.[^s7]

Situational tricks for boosting honesty have a weaker record than once claimed. A 2012 *PNAS* finding that signing at the beginning of a form made reports more truthful was adopted by government agencies, but a 2020 team including the original authors found no effect across five conceptual replications (4,559 participants) and a preregistered direct replication (1,235 participants).[^s6] In 2021 the original authors asked the journal to retract the 2012 paper after evidence emerged that data in one of its field studies had been fabricated.[^s8]

Honesty can also pull against kindness. In six experiments, Emma Levine and Maurice Schweitzer found that people trusted partners who told lies to help them more than partners who told self-serving truths, because benevolence drove that kind of trust; yet when people had to rely on what a partner *said*, honest partners were relied on more.[^s5] The authors note the stakes were small and the games artificial.[^s5] A wise reading is not that kind lies are good, but that people want both goodwill and accuracy, and honesty serves best when it carries both.

## Connections

Honesty is **rooted in** Aristotelian virtue ethics through Aristotle's account of truthfulness as a mean between boastfulness and self-deprecation, a link to the **doctrine of the mean**.[^s3] Because Miller counts self-deception among the failings honesty opposes, it **addresses** self-deception, and it sits close to **know thyself**, **Socratic ignorance** and **intellectual humility**, the truth-loving virtues of the mind.[^s1] It **contrasts with** prosocial lying and **complements** compassion: research on trust suggests people value both.[^s5] It is **studied in** moral psychology, and is **applied in** giving feedback at work, one of the settings the Honesty Project examines.[^s2] Saying an unwelcome truth often takes **intellectual courage**.
