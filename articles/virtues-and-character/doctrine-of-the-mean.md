# Aristotle's doctrine of the mean

The doctrine of the mean is Aristotle's account of what a virtue of character is. Courage, temperance, generosity and good temper are each a settled disposition to feel and act at the intermediate point between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency: courage sits between rashness and cowardice, temperance between self-indulgence and a rare insensibility to pleasure.[^s1][^s2] The intermediate point is not a fixed midpoint. It is "the mean relative to us", set by the situation and the person, and found by the kind of judgment a practically wise person exercises.[^s1] Read well, it is a habit of asking whether a feeling or action is too much, too little or about right here.

## Origin

Aristotle sets out the doctrine in Book II of the *Nicomachean Ethics*. He defines virtue as a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean relative to us and fixed by reason as the person of practical wisdom would fix it (II.6).[^s1] His illustration is food: if ten pounds is too much and two too little, a trainer will not simply order six, which would be too little for the wrestler Milo and too much for a beginner.[^s1] Chapter 7 tabulates virtues and their flanking vices, such as truthfulness between boastfulness and mock modesty.[^s1]

The idea had precedents. Britannica points to the Delphic maxim "Nothing in excess" and to the myth of Icarus, and notes that a similar ideal of balance appears in the Confucian text known in English as the *Doctrine of the Mean* (*Zhongyong*), attributed to Confucius's grandson Zisi, and in the Buddha's middle way between luxury and severe asceticism.[^s3] These are distinct teachings with their own aims, not versions of Aristotle.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The doctrine gives a structure to self-correction. Aristotle says virtue concerns feelings as well as actions: fear, anger and pity can all be felt too much or too little, and virtue means feeling them at the right times, about the right things, towards the right people, for the right reason and in the right way.[^s1] That means strong emotion is sometimes exactly right. Kraut stresses that the appropriate amount of a feeling is proportionate to the seriousness of the situation, so the doctrine is not a counsel of mildness.[^s2]

It also explains why good intentions go wrong. Admired qualities such as confidence, candour or generosity become failings when overdone.[^s1][^s4]

## How to apply it

Aristotle admits that hitting the mean is hard, like finding the centre of a circle; anyone can get angry, but getting angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time is rare (II.9).[^s1] He offers practical rules. First, steer away from whichever extreme is worse, choosing the lesser of two evils when the mean itself is out of reach. Second, notice the errors you personally drift towards, which your pleasures and pains reveal, and pull yourself towards the opposite side, as people straighten a bent stick. Third, be especially wary of pleasure, which bribes judgment.[^s1]

He adds that small deviations are not blameworthy and that how far is too far cannot be settled by formula; it rests with perception of the particular case.[^s1] Virtue itself is built by practice: people become just by repeatedly acting justly, and the same holds for the other virtues (II.1).[^s1]

## Common misreadings

- **Mean as mediocrity or compromise.** Aristotle says that, with regard to what is best and right, virtue is an extreme, not a lukewarm middle (II.6).[^s1]
- **A mean for everything.** Aristotle says some actions and feelings, such as spite, envy, theft and murder, are bad in themselves and admit no right amount (II.6).[^s1]
- **A decision procedure.** Kraut notes that the doctrine does not tell you what to do mechanically; it points to the judgment of a practically wise person, and critics object that it is unilluminating for choices between competing obligations.[^s2]
- **Every "golden mean" saying is Aristotle's.** The popular label is used for ideals of balance in several traditions, from the Delphic maxims to Confucian and Buddhist teaching, so a saying about balance should not be credited to Aristotle unless his text confirms it.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

As a teaching about character, the doctrine is judged by argument and long practice rather than experiment, so it is `normative`.[^s2] Its sharpest philosophical rival is Stoicism: Kraut notes that where Aristotle wants emotions such as anger felt in the right measure, the Stoics answered by rejecting such passions, and Aristotle does not fully defend his side of that dispute.[^s2]

Psychologists have tested a modern cousin of the idea. Grant and Schwartz reviewed evidence that many positive traits and experiences show inverted-U effects, where benefits rise and then reverse, and proposed Aristotle's mean as a guide for research on happiness and success.[^s4] Pierce and Aguinis collected similar curvilinear findings in management, for example for leader assertiveness, conscientiousness and job experience, and stressed that the tipping point is context-specific.[^s5] Both evidence bases have limits: curvilinear effects are statistically hard to detect, and Simonsohn showed that the common quadratic-regression test can label a merely flattening relationship as U-shaped, proposing a stricter "two lines" test instead.[^s5][^s6] The empirical work fits the doctrine's spirit but cannot by itself say where the mean lies for you.

## Connections

The doctrine is **rooted in Aristotelian virtue ethics**, the tradition whose core it forms,[^s2] and was **introduced by Aristotle in the *Nicomachean Ethics***.[^s1] Its practical rules for counter-steering against pleasure are aimed especially at **temperance**, so it **develops** that virtue.[^s1] It **contrasts with Stoicism**, which sought to remove rather than measure passions such as anger.[^s2] Its modern empirical counterpart is the **too-much-of-a-good-thing effect**, which **supports** the idea that goods can be overdone.[^s4][^s5] It is related to **practical wisdom** and **deliberation**, which locate the mean in a particular case,[^s1][^s2] and to ideals of balance in the Delphic maxims, **Confucianism** and **Buddhism**.[^s3]
