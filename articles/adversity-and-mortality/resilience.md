# Resilience

In everyday speech, resilience means bouncing back. Researchers of loss and trauma use the word more precisely: a resilient person keeps a fairly steady, healthy level of functioning through a hard event, rather than falling into months of symptoms and slowly climbing out.[^s1] The American Psychological Association describes it more broadly as the process of adapting well to adversity, trauma, tragedy or serious stress, and stresses that it is built from thoughts and actions people can learn, not a trait reserved for a lucky few.[^s7] Neither view means the absence of pain: resilient people still grieve, yearn and have intrusive thoughts, which pass without disabling them.[^s1][^s7]

## Origin

For much of the twentieth century, clinical thinking assumed that a loss had to be "worked through" and that someone who showed little grief was either in denial or heading for a delayed collapse.[^s1] In a 2004 review, the psychologist George Bonanno argued that this picture came from studying people who sought treatment. Prospective studies of ordinary bereaved and trauma-exposed adults showed that low, stable distress was common: in one study of spousal loss, close to half the participants showed little depression before or after the death and few grief symptoms, while chronic distress affected a smaller group.[^s1] He also found no longitudinal study clearly showing delayed grief, and he separated two outcomes that had been lumped together: **recovery**, a period of real symptoms followed by a return to normal, and **resilience**, stable functioning throughout.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The finding changes how we treat ourselves and others in hard times. A friend who returns to work and laughs at the funeral reception is not necessarily suppressing anything, and pressing everyone to grieve in one prescribed way can do harm. Bonanno noted that grief-focused treatment shows modest effects overall, and that routine debriefing of everyone after a disaster has largely failed and may interfere with natural recovery.[^s1] Equally, someone who is struggling is not failing at something easy: outcomes vary, and many routes lead to adapting well.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence

The core evidence comes from tracking people over time. A 2018 statistical review by Isaac Galatzer-Levy, Sandy Huang and Bonanno pooled 54 studies across events such as injury, bereavement, combat, disaster and illness. On average about two-thirds of people followed a resilient trajectory, about a fifth a recovery path, about one in ten chronic distress, and fewer a delayed-onset path. Resilience was the most common pattern in every type of event examined.[^s2] The authors flagged limits: the studies differed enormously, most relied on self-report, and those without pre-event measures tended to underestimate resilience.[^s2]

The headline rate has been challenged. Frank Infurna and Suniya Luthar re-analysed the same longitudinal panel data behind earlier claims about spousal loss, divorce and unemployment. With the original modelling choices, resilience again came out most common; once they let people within a group differ in how fast they changed, the resilient group became the least common.[^s3] Their conclusion is that single "rates of resilience" are highly sensitive to statistical assumptions, and that such figures should not be used to argue against offering support.[^s3]

A second puzzle is prediction. Traits, coping styles and social resources linked to resilience each explain only a few per cent of who ends up resilient, and resilience questionnaires did not predict long-term trajectories in the studies reviewed. Bonanno and colleagues call this the resilience paradox: resilience is never guaranteed, only more or less likely.[^s4] Their proposed explanation is **regulatory flexibility**: reading what a situation demands, choosing a fitting coping strategy, and adjusting if it is not working. They warn against assuming any strategy, even reappraisal or seeking support, helps in every context.[^s4]

Status: **mixed**. Resilience is real and often common, but its rate depends on method and individual outcomes are hard to predict.[^s2][^s3][^s4]

## What wisdom adds

Wisdom research suggests resilience and wisdom feed each other. In a study of 994 older adults, Monika Ardelt and Dilip Jeste found that wiser people, especially on the reflective dimension of wisdom, reported higher well-being, and wisdom softened the link between recent adverse events and lower well-being.[^s5] A related analysis of the same sample linked wisdom to stronger resilience and mastery, and these to lower perceived stress; Ardelt noted that not everyone grows wiser with age and that these qualities can be cultivated.[^s6] These are correlational findings, so they show association rather than cause.[^s5]

The flexibility account fits what wise reasoning asks for generally: attending to the particulars rather than applying one rule everywhere.[^s4] In a hard season that suggests a few questions. What does this moment actually call for: action, rest, company or solitude? Is the way I am coping working, or only familiar? The APA adds the ordinary foundations that make such judgment easier: keeping connected to people you trust, looking after sleep and health, taking small steps on what you can influence, and accepting what you cannot.[^s7]

## Common misreadings

- **"Resilient people don't feel pain."** They do; it passes without lasting impairment.[^s1][^s7]
- **"Resilience is a personality trait you either have or lack."** Researchers mostly treat it as an outcome reached by many routes, and trait measures predict it poorly.[^s4][^s7]
- **"Most people are resilient, so support isn't needed."** Rates depend on modelling choices, and some people follow chronic or recovery paths.[^s2][^s3]
- **"Resilience is the same as growth."** Resilience is staying steady through adversity;[^s1] the APA treats personal growth as something adapting well can sometimes lead to, not the same thing.[^s7]

This article is general and reflective. If a loss or traumatic event leaves you unable to function or carry out daily life, that is a reason to seek professional or crisis support rather than to try harder at being resilient.[^s7]

## Connections

Resilience is **demonstrated by** the 2018 review of trajectories after potential trauma.[^s2] The claim that it is typical is **challenged by** Infurna and Luthar's re-analysis.[^s3] It is **explained by** regulatory flexibility.[^s4] It **contrasts with** recovery, and is **applied in** grief and loss, where Bonanno's bereavement studies showed how common it is.[^s1] It sits under **coping**. It relates to **post-traumatic growth**, a different outcome;[^s7] to **wisdom** and the **three-dimensional model of wisdom**, which buffers adversity in Ardelt and Jeste's data;[^s5] to **wise reasoning**, through its emphasis on context;[^s4] and to **equanimity** and the **dichotomy of control**, echoed in the APA's advice to act on what you can influence and accept what you cannot.[^s7]
