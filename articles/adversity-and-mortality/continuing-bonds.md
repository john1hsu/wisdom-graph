# Continuing bonds

Continuing bonds is the idea that the relationship with someone who has died does not simply end: many bereaved people keep an inner connection with the person, through memories, conversations in the mind, keepsakes, rituals or carrying on their values, and this can be part of healthy grieving rather than a failure to accept the death.[^s1] The phrase comes from a 1996 collection edited by Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman and Steven Nickman, which drew on studies of widows and widowers, bereaved parents, children and siblings, adoptees and mourners in Japan.[^s1]

## Origin

For much of the twentieth century, the dominant view, traced to Freud's account of mourning, held that grief exists to loosen the survivor's attachment so that love can be reinvested in new relationships; holding on to the dead was read as a sign of pathological grief.[^s1][^s5] Klass, Silverman and Nickman argued that this model reflected the cultural values of modernity more than what bereaved people actually do. In their data, survivors often found a place for the dead in their ongoing lives and communities, even when professionals did not validate it, and the bond was not denial: the dead could be a resource for living well in the present.[^s1]

The idea spread quickly through bereavement research. By 2005, Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut described how theory had moved from urging the bereaved to break bonds to asking whether, and for whom, continuing them helps.[^s2]

## Why it matters

How we think about the bond shapes how we treat grieving people, including ourselves. A widower who still talks to his wife on morning walks, a family that sets a place at a holiday table, or a daughter who asks what her mother would have done may be told, or may tell themselves, that they are "not letting go".[^s1][^s4] The continuing bonds view gives a more humane default: the question is not whether a bond exists but how it is working for this person, now.[^s4]

It also widens the cultural lens. In many traditions keeping a relationship with the dead is ordinary. Goss describes how Tibetan Buddhist mourners spend the 49 days after a death reading the *Bardo Thodol* to the deceased, making offerings and dedicating merit, and then continue the relationship through anniversary rites and objects such as a stupa holding a parent's ashes.[^s6] In that setting grief is not suppressed but turned into compassionate action for the dead, and this coexists with Buddhist teaching that clinging, including to loved ones, causes suffering; the bond is framed as devotion rather than grasping.[^s6] Eisma and Nguyen likewise note that in Japan and Pakistan keeping a connection to the dead is often seen as a natural part of present life.[^s5]

## Using the idea wisely

- **Ask what the bond does, not whether it exists.** Bonds that bring comfort, help a person make sense of the death or carry forward who they were with the deceased are, in the research, common and often helpful; bonds that bring fear, guilt or sharpened yearning deserve attention.[^s4]
- **Prefer inner bonds to searching.** Holding the person in memory, values and identity is generally linked with better outcomes than externalised forms, such as vivid sensations of their presence or repeated efforts to be physically near them, which are more often tied to distress and complicated grief.[^s4][^s5]
- **Mind the circumstances.** After a suicide, a violent or sudden death, the death of a child or a conflict-laden relationship, bonds are more likely to be painful, so extra support is wise.[^s4]
- **Let the bond change.** Researchers argue that bonds are better understood as transformed over time than as kept or cut, and that what helps early may differ from what helps later.[^s4]

## Evidence

The empirical picture is mixed. Reviewing the early studies, Stroebe and Schut concluded that the evidence did not show either continuing or relinquishing bonds to be generally helpful, and called for research on which kinds of bond help whom.[^s2] A prospective Dutch study of 56 bereaved people found that different bond types predicted different outcomes: keeping bonds through vivid memories strongly predicted later grief symptoms, while using the deceased's possessions weakly predicted both grief and depression.[^s3] A 2023 systematic review of 79 studies, covering more than 13,000 bereaved people, found that bonds often brought comfort, meaning and continuity of identity, and often brought distress too, sometimes alternately; internalised bonds tended to be adaptive and externalised ones less so.[^s4] It could not establish whether keeping bonds is helpful overall, because most studies were cross-sectional, measures varied and samples were mostly white, Christian women from Western countries.[^s4] A 2022 survey of 694 bereaved Dutch adults found that more deliberate behaviours aimed at feeling close to the deceased were associated with more prolonged grief and depression symptoms, though the design could not show causation.[^s5]

The theory is best read as a corrective to a rigid detachment model rather than as advice that everyone should maintain a bond.[^s1][^s2] This article is general and reflective, not clinical guidance: if grief stays intense for many months, makes daily life unmanageable or leaves you feeling unsafe, contact a doctor, a mental health professional or a crisis line.

## Connections

Continuing bonds was **introduced by** Dennis Klass with Phyllis Silverman and Steven Nickman in **Continuing Bonds: New Understandings of Grief**, and it **contrasts with** **grief work**, the Freudian view that mourning must sever attachment, which is why it is often set against **Mourning and Melancholia**.[^s1][^s5] It is **applied in** **grief and bereavement**, and is **supported by** the 2023 **systematic review of the impact of continuing bonds** in finding frequent comfort, meaning and identity benefits.[^s4] It is **challenged by** Stroebe and Schut's **review of continuing versus relinquishing bonds**, which found no general advantage, and by the **proximity-seeking behaviour scale study**, which linked seeking closeness to the dead with prolonged grief symptoms.[^s2][^s5] It sits among **theories of grief** alongside the **dual process model of bereavement**, and relates to **prolonged grief disorder** and **rumination**, the forms of being stuck that some bonds can feed.[^s4][^s5] It also relates to **meaning in life**, which bonds can help rebuild,[^s4] and to **Buddhism**, whose Tibetan mourning rites keep a compassionate bond with the dead while teaching non-clinging.[^s6]
