# Social support

Social support is the help and care people get, or believe they could get, from those around them. Researchers treat it not as one thing but as at least three: social integration, meaning how many kinds of relationships and roles a person takes part in; perceived support, the belief that others would help if asked; and enacted or received support, the help that is actually given.[^s6] These turn out to behave differently, and the differences matter for anyone trying to help, or be helped, wisely.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin

The modern study of social support grew out of epidemiology and health psychology in the 1970s and 1980s, when researchers noticed that people with more and better relationships tended to stay healthier.[^s1] Two explanations took shape. On the main-effect view, simply being woven into a network of roles and relationships benefits health whether or not life is going badly. On the stress-buffering view, support matters chiefly when people face stress, softening its effects; statistically, this shows up as an interaction between stress and support.[^s1] Sheldon Cohen's review concluded that both are real but run through different channels: social integration works mainly as a main effect, while it is perceived support that buffers stress.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The links with health are substantial. Reviews report that support from others can be protective, with socially less involved people more likely to suffer a first heart attack and less integrated heart patients more likely to be readmitted.[^s2] In a seven-year Swedish study cited by Cohen, men who felt they had emotional support were protected against the link between stressful events and death, whereas the sheer number of their ties did not buffer stress.[^s1] The crucial ingredient, Cohen argued, is the perception that appropriate help would come, even from one reliable person.[^s1]

For wisdom, the point is that the feeling of being backed and the act of helping are not the same. Help that is received is not automatically help that helps.[^s1][^s3]

## Giving and seeking support wisely

- **Make support available, not loud.** Help that is explicit and recognised as help can raise anxiety and lower confidence, because it draws attention to the problem and can signal that the person is not coping. Subtle, practical help that is not experienced as help often avoids those costs.[^s3]
- **Match the moment.** Visible comfort still has a place: it can soothe immediate distress and show that someone cares. In couples' daily diaries, visible support brought more relationship satisfaction but also more anxiety on the same day, while invisible support predicted greater satisfaction the next day.[^s3]
- **Reduce conflict, not just add kindness.** Cohen found that people with lasting conflicts in their relationships were more than twice as likely to develop a cold after exposure to a virus; negative interactions are a separate influence, not merely the absence of support.[^s1]
- **Give as well as receive.** In a five-year study of older married couples, giving practical help to friends, relatives and neighbours, and emotional support to a spouse, predicted lower mortality, while receiving support showed no effect once giving was accounted for.[^s4] Coverage of the study noted that whether giving actually causes better health remains open.[^s5]
- **Build the network before the crisis.** Because perceived support rests on a history of reliable relationships, the time to invest in it is when things are calm.[^s1][^s6]

## Common misreadings

One misreading is that more help is always better. Cohen reports that peer support groups for breast cancer patients harmed women who already had strong natural support, and that formal support interventions have been largely disappointing; he recommends working with people's existing networks instead.[^s1] Another is that counting contacts captures support. Integration and perceived support are distinct, and the mismatch between what people expect and what they receive can itself be associated with poorer outcomes.[^s1][^s2] A third is that support from friends and family can replace professional care. It cannot: if distress is persistent or overwhelming, the wise use of one's network includes letting it help you reach a doctor, counsellor or crisis service.

## Evidence and limits

The association between social ties, perceived support and health is well documented across many studies, but most of it is observational, so causation is hard to pin down, and the mechanisms are still being worked out.[^s1][^s2] The findings on received and visible support are more conditional: their effects depend on how help is given, by whom and when, which is why the overall status here is mixed rather than robust.[^s3] The giving-support finding comes from one older, married American sample and needs caution about cause and effect.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Social support is a part of the wider web of social relationships, its **broader** concept, and the reason relationships matter most in hard times.[^s1] It is **explained by** the stress-buffering hypothesis, which says perceived support softens the harm of stress, and it is **applied in** adversity of every kind.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** the daily-diary studies of invisible support in couples[^s3] and by the prospective study linking giving support to lower mortality.[^s4] It is **studied in** health psychology, which examines how relationships get under the skin.[^s2] It is **related** to friendship, compassion and empathy, the sources of good support; to generosity, its giving side;[^s4] to seeking counsel and giving advice, its more deliberate forms; to rumination and emotional suppression, which can wear supportive ties thin; to loneliness, its absence; and to resilience, grief and loss, and wisdom and well-being, where support shapes how people come through.[^s1][^s2]
