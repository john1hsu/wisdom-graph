# Grief and bereavement

Bereavement is losing someone close through death; grief is its response. Research on bereaved adults finds that grief is less a single feeling than a shifting mix: disbelief early on, a deep yearning or pining for the person, anger and low mood, and a growing acceptance that, after a natural death, is often present even in the first weeks.[^s3] There is no single correct way through it, and after a natural death the painful feelings typically peak and begin to ease within about six months.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

The most familiar picture of grief, the five stages of denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance, comes from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's 1969 book *On Death and Dying*. She built it from conversations with dying patients, not the bereaved, using case material rather than systematic study; it was later stretched to mourners and many other losses.[^s4]

Bereavement researchers also questioned an older clinical idea, the "grief work" hypothesis, which held that the bereaved must confront and work through their feelings and that avoiding them is harmful. Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut argued that it was vaguely defined, poorly supported and rarely tested across cultures.[^s1] In its place they proposed the **dual process model**: grieving people face two kinds of stress, the loss itself (loss-oriented) and the changes it forces on daily life, such as new roles, tasks and identity (restoration-oriented), and they cope by moving back and forth between the two, sometimes confronting and sometimes setting aside each one.[^s1] On this view, taking a break from grief is part of coping, not a failure of it.[^s1]

## Why it matters

What we believe about grief shapes how we treat ourselves and others. When a stage model is taught as fact, people whose grief does not fit may conclude something is wrong with them.[^s4] Grief also sits at the edge of serious mental health risk. In 2021 the American Psychiatric Association announced that **prolonged grief disorder** would enter its diagnostic manual (the DSM-5-TR, 2022) for grief that becomes incapacitating: in adults, at least a year after the death, with grief reactions most of the day, nearly every day, and symptoms such as identity disruption, intense emotional pain, emotional numbness, intense loneliness or a sense that life is meaningless, beyond what one's culture or faith would expect.[^s5] Telling ordinary grief, which needs room and company, from grief that needs treatment is a practical judgment most families eventually face.[^s3][^s5]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Expect variety, not a script.** Grief has typical ingredients but no fixed order.[^s3][^s4]
- **Oscillate on purpose.** Leave time to remember and mourn, and time to learn new tasks, see friends or simply rest from grief.[^s1]
- **Let the relationship change rather than end.** Many bereaved people keep a bond with the person who died, through memories, rituals or carrying on their values. A review of 79 studies found such bonds can bring comfort, help rebuild meaning and identity, and sometimes bring distress, so the question is how a bond works for you, not whether you should have one.[^s2]
- **See the loss as shared, without minimising it.** In a Buddhist commentary, a young mother named Kisa Gotami, mad with grief, carries her dead son from house to house seeking medicine. The Buddha asks her for a mustard seed from a home that has never known death. Finding none, she comes to see that death touches every family, and is able to lay her son to rest.[^s6] The story belongs to Buddhist teaching on impermanence, but its point that grief is part of every life can ease isolation for anyone.[^s6]
- **Know the warning signs.** Persistent, incapacitating yearning, numbness or meaninglessness, or distress that stays high long after the death, is a reason to seek evaluation from a mental health professional rather than to try harder alone.[^s3][^s5]

## Pitfalls

- **Treating the five stages as a map.** The stages began with the dying, not mourners, and later research gives them at best partial support.[^s3][^s4]
- **Assuming you must "work through" every feeling straight away.** Setting the loss aside for a while is part of healthy coping; the model sees confronting and avoiding as both needed.[^s1]
- **Hurrying people to "let go."** Ongoing bonds with the dead are common and can be adaptive.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The best-known test of the stage theory, a 2007 *JAMA* study of 233 bereaved people in Connecticut led by Paul Maciejewski, found that acceptance was common from the start and grew over time, and that yearning, not depression, was the most prominent painful feeling. Disbelief, yearning, anger and depression peaked in that order across about six months, but disbelief was not the dominant early response, so the stage model received only partial support.[^s3] Critics questioned its methods, and a 2002 study found that only a small minority followed the assumed "normal" grief path.[^s4] The dual process model is widely used but its authors present it as needing further testing,[^s1] and the review of continuing bonds could not settle whether keeping or relinquishing bonds is better overall.[^s2] Status: **mixed**.

This article is general and reflective, not medical advice. If grief leaves you unable to manage daily life, or you feel unsafe, contact a doctor, a mental health professional or a local crisis line.[^s5]

## Connections

Grief and bereavement is **explained by** the dual process model, which describes coping as oscillation between loss and restoration,[^s1] and by **continuing bonds**, which describes how the relationship with the dead is transformed rather than cut off.[^s2] Its typical course is **demonstrated by** the 2007 empirical test of the stage theory of grief, which also challenged the **five stages of grief** as a fixed sequence.[^s3][^s4] It **contrasts with** **prolonged grief disorder**, grief that becomes incapacitating.[^s5] It sits under **adversity** and relates to **resilience**, **rumination** (being stuck on the loss side), **meaning in life**, which loss can shake and bonds help rebuild,[^s2][^s5] and **impermanence**, **acceptance**, **compassion** and **Buddhism**, through the story of Kisa Gotami.[^s6]
