# Positivity effect in ageing

The positivity effect is an age difference in where the mind lingers. Set beside younger adults, older adults on average give relatively more of their attention and memory to pleasant material (smiling faces, good news, the upside of a choice) and relatively less to unpleasant material.[^s1] Researchers call it an *effect* rather than a *bias* because it is a comparison between age groups: younger adults often lean the other way, towards the negative.[^s1][^s2] It can come from processing positive things more, processing negative things less, or both.[^s1]

## Origin

The pattern was first reported in memory studies by Laura Carstensen, Mara Mather and Susan Charles in the early 2000s, and the label appeared in print soon after; since then well over a hundred peer-reviewed papers have examined it.[^s1] It grew out of socioemotional selectivity theory, which holds that as people sense their remaining time shrinking, their goals move from gathering information towards emotional meaning and well-being.[^s1] On that account the effect is not something that simply happens to ageing brains but something older people do, often without noticing, as their priorities change.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The effect helps explain one of the more hopeful puzzles of later life: despite losses in health, roles and people, many older adults report steady or improved emotional well-being.[^s1] For anyone thinking about wisdom, it is a reminder that what we attend to is partly a choice shaped by what we value, and that a shift towards savouring the good can be a sensible response to a sense of limited time rather than denial.[^s1][^s3]

It also has a practical edge. It shows up in decisions, with older adults paying more attention to the positive features of doctors, hospitals or cars when comparing them.[^s1] Researchers have asked whether that could make people overlook warning signs in health choices or in a too-good-to-be-true offer.[^s1][^s6] The honest answer so far is that the risk is plausible but not established.

## Evidence

The strongest evidence is a 2014 meta-analysis of 100 studies with 7,129 participants. It found a reliable but small age difference overall (d ≈ 0.26): older adults leaned slightly towards the positive, younger adults slightly towards the negative.[^s2] The effect was medium-sized (d ≈ 0.48) when people were free to look and remember as they liked, and small (d ≈ 0.13) when instructions or tasks dictated what they had to process; publication bias appeared minimal.[^s2] Older adults also show it most when they have the mental resources to steer attention. In one study it reversed when their attention was divided, and the shift towards pleasant faces emerges seconds after an image appears rather than instantly.[^s1] Both points fit a deliberate, goal-driven process better than simple decline.[^s1][^s2]

Experiments that change people's sense of time point the same way. When younger and older adults wrote about having only six months left rather than a very long life ahead, both groups later recalled a more positive mix of pictures, and the change was not explained by mood.[^s3] The rival "ageing brain" account, in which wear on emotion circuits blunts reactions to bad news, is not ruled out, but Reed and Carstensen argue it fits poorly, since age differences show up in reactions to positive material as well as negative.[^s1]

The limits are real. Nearly all the evidence compares age groups at one point in time, so generational differences can masquerade as ageing, and few studies test real, high-stakes choices.[^s2] Culture matters too, though how is disputed. A Hong Kong study found no age difference in where Chinese participants looked; instead, older Chinese adults who did look at positive pictures gained more in mood, which the authors read as a difference in how the effect works rather than whether it exists.[^s4] A later eye-tracking study that compared Chinese and British adults directly found a similar positivity effect in both.[^s5] And the feared link to fraud has not held up cleanly: in an online study of pandemic-era scams, older adults saw *fewer* benefits in the offers than younger adults did and were no more willing to respond.[^s6] Overall the effect is best rated **mixed**: real and replicated, but small, conditional and culturally variable.

## Living with it wisely

None of this is a reason to avoid bad news or to treat an older person's good cheer as naivety. A few modest applications follow.[^s1][^s2]

- **Keep the good, check the bad on big decisions.** Savouring what is going well is healthy, but for a large financial, medical or housing choice, write down the downsides deliberately; in one study, older adults who listed pros and cons ended up more satisfied with their choices than younger adults did.[^s1]
- **Respect a different focus.** An older colleague who dwells less on a project's risks may be weighing what matters differently, not missing it; ask what they see before assuming.[^s1]
- **Borrow the perspective.** Since a sense of limited time shifts attention at any age, younger people can ask what they would notice and keep if their time were short.[^s3]
- **Watch for pressure, not age.** Be wary of any offer that needs an answer now, at any age; the evidence does not support treating everyone over seventy as gullible.[^s6]

## Connections

The effect is **explained by socioemotional selectivity theory**, was **introduced by Laura Carstensen** and her collaborators, and is **studied in lifespan developmental psychology**.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by the 2014 meta-analysis**, which also shows it **contrasts with the negativity bias** typical of younger adults.[^s2] It is **supported by the limited-future recall study**, in which shortening people's time horizon made recall more positive at any age.[^s3] It is **challenged by the Hong Kong study of cultural differences**, which found no age difference in looking preferences among Chinese adults,[^s4] and **supported by the Chinese-and-UK eye-movement study**, which found the effect in both cultures.[^s5] Its supposed role in scam vulnerability is **challenged by the COVID-19 scam study**.[^s6] It sits under **emotion regulation** and **ageing**, and relates to **wisdom and age**, **savouring**, **death awareness** and **equanimity**, all about how attention and finitude shape emotional life.[^s1][^s3]
