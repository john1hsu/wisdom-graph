# Failure

Failure is the situation of falling short of something you set out to do and cared about: a rejected application, a product that doesn't sell, an exam you fail, a decision that costs your team. Unlike adversity in general, it involves your own aims and often your own choices, which is what makes it hard to think about clearly. Failure is often called the best teacher, but research suggests people learn less from their own failures than they assume, chiefly because failing threatens how they see themselves.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Epictetus, the Stoic teacher, opens his *Handbook* by dividing things into those that are up to us, such as our opinions, desires, aversions and actions, and those that are not, such as our body, property, reputation and offices. He warns that treating the second kind as our own leads to frustration and to blaming other people.[^s7] A modern reading for failure, not Epictetus's own words, is that the outcome is never fully yours, while your preparation, judgment and response are.[^s7]

Amy Edmondson's 2011 *Harvard Business Review* article argued that failure cannot be avoided in complex work and that learning from it is a mark of reliable organizations. She separated preventable failures in predictable work, often slips that checklists can catch, from unavoidable failures in complex systems, which should be reported, analysed and used to learn.[^s5]

## Why it matters

In five studies with 1,674 participants, Lauren Eskreis-Winkler and Ayelet Fishbach found that people learned less from failure feedback than from success feedback, even when both told them the right answer, when learning from failure took less effort, and when learning was rewarded.[^s1] Those told they had failed even remembered fewer of their own answers. The authors traced this to ego threat: failing feels bad, so people tune out. When the failures were someone else's, participants learned from them as well as from successes.[^s1]

A preregistered replication with 520 participants reproduced this "tune-out effect" under the original conditions, but it disappeared when the same incentive was framed as a loss for each wrong answer, or when people got corrective feedback.[^s2] The tendency is real, but many real failures that come with a clear correction may be hard to tune out.[^s2]

How you treat yourself afterwards also matters. In four experiments, people prompted to take a self-compassionate view of a weakness, a moral lapse or a failed test saw the weakness as more changeable, felt more motivated to make amends, and studied longer for a retest than people given a self-esteem boost or no intervention.[^s4] Accepting a failure kindly, rather than either excusing it or attacking yourself, seemed to make improvement more likely.[^s4]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Look before you look away.** Expect the urge to stop attending to bad news, and make yourself read the feedback in full.[^s1]
- **Borrow an outsider's view.** Because people learn well from others' failures, review your own as if it were a colleague's.[^s1]
- **Get the correction, not just the verdict.** Finding out what the right answer or better approach would have been helps counter tuning out.[^s2]
- **Sort what was yours.** Separate your own choices from the things that were never in your control, and spend your effort on the first.[^s7]
- **Name the kind of failure.** A slip in routine work calls for a fix such as a checklist; a failure in complex or experimental work calls for analysis and, often, another informed attempt.[^s5]
- **Be kind and exact at once.** Self-compassion is not letting yourself off: it pairs owning the failure with the belief that you can improve.[^s4]

At work, leaders shape whether failures get reported at all: a culture that punishes every failure hides the information people need to learn.[^s5]

## Pitfalls

- **Assuming you learn automatically.** The feeling that "I'll remember this" is not evidence that you will.[^s1]
- **Turning a failure into a verdict on yourself.** Harsh self-judgment invites tuning out; it does not sharpen attention.[^s1][^s4]
- **Blaming only circumstances.** The control distinction cuts both ways: what was yours to do is still yours to examine.[^s7]
- **Romanticizing failure.** Among junior scientists who just missed out on a major grant, those who stayed on later outperformed those who just scraped in, but the near miss also made it more likely, by an estimated 10% or more, that they left the funding system for good.[^s6] Failure can strengthen people who persist; it also drives some away.[^s6]
- **Getting stuck.** Replaying a failure without new information or a correction is not the kind of review that helped people learn.[^s2] If a failure leaves you persistently low or unable to function, that is a reason to talk with someone you trust or a professional.

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is **mixed**. The tune-out effect replicated in a preregistered study but has clear boundary conditions.[^s2] A later commentary argued that the main test used in the original studies is confounded: it scores consistent answers as correct after success but wrong after failure, so people who answer consistently without learning can look as if they failed to learn from failure.[^s3] The self-compassion findings come from short laboratory experiments.[^s4] The grant study is observational, although its design compares people just above and just below a funding line.[^s6] The popular claim that failure reliably makes people stronger or wiser goes beyond this evidence.

## Connections

Failure sits under **adversity** and is **demonstrated by** Eskreis-Winkler and Fishbach's study of not learning from failure,[^s1] by Keith and colleagues' replication of the tune-out effect,[^s2] by Breines and Chen's experiments on self-compassion,[^s4] and by Wang, Jones and Wang's study of early-career setbacks.[^s6] It is **challenged by** the answer-consistency critique.[^s3] It relates to **learning from experience** and the **after-action review**;[^s5] to **regret** and **rumination**;[^s1] to **self-compassion** and **self-distancing**, which ease ego threat;[^s1][^s4] to the **dichotomy of control**, **Epictetus** and **Stoicism**;[^s7] to **resilience** and **knowing when to quit**;[^s6] and to **speaking up at work** and **leading others**.[^s5]
