# Post-traumatic growth

Post-traumatic growth (PTG) is the name psychologists give to positive change that people describe after wrestling with a crisis: a bereavement, a serious illness, an accident, a divorce or a disaster. The people who coined the term define it as change that comes from the struggle with highly challenging circumstances, and that goes beyond bouncing back to how one was before.[^s2] Reports cluster in five areas: closer relationships with others, a sense of new possibilities, greater personal strength, spiritual or existential change, and a deeper appreciation of life.[^s1] The popular version, that what doesn't kill you makes you stronger, needs careful handling.[^s4]

## Origin

The idea that suffering can bring something good is very old. Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, who named the phenomenon, point to early Hebrew, Greek and Christian thought and to teachings in Hinduism, Buddhism and Islam, while stressing that the events themselves are never desirable.[^s2] Their contribution was to measure it. In 1996 they published the 21-item Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI). Students who had lived through a severe trauma reported more growth than those who had not, and women reported somewhat more than men.[^s1] In 2004 they set out a fuller model. Growth does not come from the trauma itself but from the struggle to rebuild beliefs about the world that the event has shattered. It is linked to deliberate reflection rather than intrusive, unwanted thoughts, and it is helped by being able to talk things through with supportive people.[^s2]

## Why it matters

For a reader trying to meet hardship wisely, the idea cuts two ways. It offers real hope: many people do come to value their relationships, their days and their own capacities differently after a loss.[^s2] Tedeschi and Calhoun even tied growth to the development of a broader life wisdom and to the reshaping of one's life story.[^s2] Wisdom research points the same way. Studies by Nic Weststrate and colleagues found that wisdom in midlife was linked to hard, non-normative experiences such as divorce, illness and job loss, but mainly when people reflected on them deeply and exploratively; adversity without that reflection did not predict wisdom.[^s5]

But the idea can also become a burden. If growth is expected, people still in pain may feel they are failing at suffering. Tedeschi and Calhoun themselves warned that growth often coexists with ongoing distress, is not inevitable, and should not be pushed on survivors by helpers.[^s2] A wise stance holds both: growth is possible, not required, and not a measure of how well someone is coping.

## Evidence

The main dispute is about measurement. Nearly all early studies asked people, after the event, how much they had changed. That relies on remembering one's former self accurately, and it invites reports shaped by hope, by a wish to make sense of the loss, and by a cultural script that says adversity should make us stronger.[^s4]

The most cited test of this concern was a prospective study by Patricia Frazier and colleagues. They measured students on the PTGI's domains, then again two months later. Among 122 students who had a traumatic event in between, PTGI scores were largely unrelated to actual measured change. Perceived growth went with more distress, while actual growth went with less, and perceived growth was linked to coping by positive reinterpretation.[^s3] The authors concluded that retrospective growth questionnaires may not capture real before-and-after change.[^s3]

A 2021 review by Eranda Jayawickreme, Frank Infurna and colleagues found the wider picture consistent with this. Reliable improvement after adversity appeared in only a minority of people in prospective data, roughly 5 to 25 per cent depending on the outcome in the Frazier study. Pooled analyses of large panel studies found positive personality change after adversity hard to detect, and long-term tracking suggested that decline followed by gradual recovery is a more common path than growth.[^s4] A meta-analysis they cite found some genuine growth but left unclear which events and personal characteristics produce it.[^s4] The reviewers do not dismiss growth but call for measures taken before the event.[^s4] They also note that wisdom-related strengths such as intellectual humility and open-mindedness are plausible areas of change, and that a self-distanced way of reflecting on adversity may support such growth while immersed rumination may hinder it.[^s4]

The status here is therefore **contested**: some people do change for the better after hardship, but the claim that growth is common or typical outruns the evidence, and self-reported growth is not good evidence of it.[^s3][^s4]

## Common misreadings

- **"Trauma makes people stronger."** Growth, where it happens, comes from the struggle afterwards, not from the event, and it is not the usual outcome.[^s2][^s4]
- **"If I haven't grown, I'm coping badly."** Growth and distress are largely separate; recovery to one's former self is a good outcome in its own right.[^s2][^s4]
- **"Growth is the same as resilience."** Tedeschi and Calhoun distinguish them: resilience is weathering adversity well, growth is a change beyond one's former functioning.[^s2]
- **"Saying I've grown proves I have."** Perceived and actual growth can diverge.[^s3]

This article is general and reflective. Persistent distress after a traumatic event is a reason to seek professional or crisis support, not to wait for growth; the theory's originators themselves warn helpers not to expect or push it.[^s2]

## Connections

Post-traumatic growth was **introduced by Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun**, who named and measured it.[^s1] It is **commonly confused with resilience**, which its originators treat as a different outcome.[^s2] It is **explained by** the idea that trauma shatters a person's basic assumptions about the world, which then have to be rebuilt.[^s2] The claim that it is common is **challenged by** Frazier and colleagues' prospective **study of perceived versus actual growth**.[^s3] It sits under the broader topic of **personal growth**, which reviewers frame as lasting positive personality change.[^s4] It relates to **rumination**, because intrusive brooding is not the deliberate reflection linked to growth;[^s2] to **self-reflection** and **wisdom**, through work linking exploratory reflection on hard experiences to wisdom;[^s5] to **self-distancing**, which may support wisdom-related growth;[^s4] and to **benefit-finding**, the positive reinterpretation that tracks perceived rather than actual growth.[^s3]
