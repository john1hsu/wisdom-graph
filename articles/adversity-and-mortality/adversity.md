# Adversity

Adversity is the experience of serious hardship: illness or injury, divorce, losing a job, a failure that matters, or a run of misfortune that cannot simply be avoided. Most people meet several such events over a lifetime; in one large US survey only about 8% of adults reported none at all.[^s1] Because it is so common, the question is less whether adversity will come than how to meet it, and whether it leaves a person diminished, steadier or wiser.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Wisdom traditions have long treated hardship as the place where character is tested. The Stoic teacher Epictetus opens one of his discourses by saying that it is difficulties that show what people are, and compares a difficulty to a trainer of wrestlers who matches a pupil with a rough young opponent (*Discourses* 1.24).[^s5] Early Buddhist teaching offers a different image. In the *Sallatha Sutta*, an untrained person struck by pain grieves and struggles against it, as if shot by one arrow and then by a second; a trained disciple, the Buddha says, "feels one pain: physical, but not mental" (SN 36.6).[^s6] The Stoic image stresses training through hardship; the Buddhist one stresses not multiplying it. Both locate much of what matters in how the mind responds, not in the event alone.[^s5][^s6]

Psychology took up the question empirically. Glück, Bluck and Weststrate's **MORE life experience model** proposes that life challenges are the main catalysts for developing personal wisdom, but only for people who bring certain resources to them.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The familiar adage that whatever does not kill us makes us stronger is only partly true. In a national US study of 2,398 adults followed over several years (2001–2004), more lifetime adversity generally predicted worse mental health, but people with a low, non-zero number of adverse events reported less distress, less impairment and higher life satisfaction than those with either none or many, and were least shaken by new setbacks.[^s1] The authors suggest some adversity may build coping skills, while heavy adversity can overwhelm coping and support networks.[^s1] So adversity is not good in itself, and how it is handled shapes what it leaves behind.

Studies of wisdom point the same way. Midlife adults link their own growth in wisdom to hard, emotionally negative, unexpected events such as divorce, serious illness or job loss. Wiser people reported moderate levels of adversity alongside many positive events, and the highest wisdom came from adversity combined with deep, exploratory self-reflection.[^s3]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Feel the first arrow; decline the second.** Acknowledge the real pain without piling on stories of catastrophe, blame or unfairness that add suffering of their own.[^s6]
- **Sort what is in your power.** The MORE model's first resource is a sense of mastery: accepting that much of life is uncertain and uncontrollable while trusting that you can act on the part that is not.[^s2]
- **Stay open and feel your way through.** Openness to other perspectives and emotional sensitivity and regulation, staying aware of feelings and tolerating mixed ones, are among the resources linked to wisdom.[^s2]
- **Reflect to understand, not just to close the book.** Researchers distinguish **exploratory** reflection, which looks for meaning, complexity and lessons, from **redemptive** reflection, which turns a bad experience into a good story for emotional closure. The first is linked to wisdom, the second mainly to feeling better.[^s2][^s3]
- **Do not go it alone.** Heavy, accumulating adversity can exhaust coping skills and support networks.[^s1] Persistent distress, or difficulty managing daily life, is a reason to reach out to others and to seek professional or crisis support, not a test to pass alone.[^s1]

## Pitfalls

- **Romanticising suffering.** The benefit in the 2010 study was for low, not high, adversity, and the data were correlational, counted events without weighing severity and relied on recall.[^s1] Seeking out hardship, or telling someone in pain that it will make them stronger, outruns the evidence.
- **Assuming more resources are always better.** The MORE model's authors note that extremes can backfire, for example too much self-sacrifice in the name of empathy.[^s2]
- **Mistaking a tidy moral for wisdom.** Hurrying to a redemptive lesson may ease feelings but skips the exploratory work linked to growth.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The U-shaped "steeling" pattern does not always appear. A one-year study of 238 Swiss adults aged 50 and over found no curvilinear link between early or chronic adversity and resilience, only linear associations, and reviewed a literature with both supporting and null findings; its sample's low adversity levels may have limited the test.[^s4] Evidence that adversity fosters wisdom comes mainly from cross-sectional and retrospective studies; the MORE model's own test, with 170 participants including people nominated as wise, cannot show that the resources cause wisdom.[^s2][^s3] Status: **mixed**.

This article is general and reflective, not medical or psychological advice. If hardship leaves you unable to cope, or you feel unsafe, contact a doctor, a mental health professional or a local crisis line.[^s1]

## Connections

Adversity's mixed relation to well-being is **demonstrated by** Seery and colleagues' study of cumulative lifetime adversity,[^s1] and the idea that moderate hardship toughens people is **challenged by** a later study that found no steeling effect.[^s4] How hardship can become wisdom is **explained by** the **MORE life experience model**,[^s2] and **demonstrated by** Weststrate's studies linking adversity and reflection to wisdom.[^s3] It sits under broader **life challenges** and is the setting for **grief and bereavement**. It relates to **resilience** and **post-traumatic growth**, outcomes that can follow it;[^s1][^s3] to **self-reflection** and **learning from experience**, through exploratory processing;[^s2][^s3] to **rumination**, the grieving and struggling that adds a second arrow;[^s6] to **Stoicism**, the **dichotomy of control** and **amor fati**, through Epictetus's view of hardship as training;[^s5] to **Buddhism** and **dukkha**, through the parable of the two arrows;[^s6] to **acceptance**, **cognitive reappraisal** and **Viktor Frankl**, as ways of meeting what cannot be changed;[^s2][^s6] and to **social support**, which heavy adversity can strain.[^s1]
