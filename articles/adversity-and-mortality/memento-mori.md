steps:
  - Pick a fixed cue for the reflection, such as waking up, a commute, or the last few minutes before sleep.
  - Bring to mind, concretely and in your own case, that your life will end and that you do not know when.
  - Look back over the last day or week and ask how you actually spent your time and attention.
  - Ask what you would regret leaving undone or unsaid, and whom you would think of first.
  - Choose one concrete action for today that reflects what matters, and let go of one petty grievance or craving.
  - Return your attention to the task in front of you; if the reflection leaves you more anxious than clear, shorten it or stop.
example:
  situation: You have spent a week stewing over a curt email from a colleague and are drafting a sharp reply late in the evening while your family is in the next room.
  before: "I'll send a reply that puts them in their place, then keep going through my inbox until midnight."
  after: "If my time were shorter than I assume, this exchange would not be worth another evening. I'll send a short, civil reply tomorrow and spend tonight with the people in the next room."
  why: Remembering that time is limited and uncertain shifts attention from winning a minor status contest to the relationships and values you would actually want to have served.

# Memento mori

*Memento mori* ("remember that you must die") names a practice found in several wisdom traditions: deliberately and regularly calling to mind that your life will end, and that the timing is unknown. The aim is not morbid brooding but clearer priorities. In the Stoic version, keeping death in view each day is meant to stop you from entertaining petty thoughts or craving things too eagerly.[^s2] In the early Buddhist version, mindfulness of death is praised as a practice of great benefit when it is done with real urgency rather than as a distant abstraction.[^s5]

## Origin

The Stoic teacher Epictetus told his students to keep exile and, above all, death before their eyes every day, so that they would not think base thoughts or desire anything excessively (*Handbook* 21).[^s2] Marcus Aurelius returns to the theme again and again in the notebook now called the *Meditations*. In Book II he tells himself to act and think as someone who could leave life at any moment (2.11), to do each act as if it were his last (2.5), and to treat death as a natural dissolution that is neither good nor evil (2.11–2.12, 2.17).[^s3] Scholars read the *Meditations* as writings to and for himself, meant to rehearse Stoic doctrines he wanted to live by; the imminence of death and acceptance of transience are among its most persistent strands.[^s4]

A parallel practice appears in the Pali discourses. In the *Maraṇassati Sutta* (AN 6.19), monks describe recollecting death over shrinking spans of time, from a day and a night down to a single meal. The Buddha says those who reflect only on the longer spans practise heedlessly, and praises those who hold death in mind across one mouthful or one breath.[^s5] The two traditions frame the goal differently: for the Stoics, living according to virtue and reason without fear;[^s3] for the sutta, heedfulness in service of liberation from the mental defilements.[^s5] They should not be collapsed into a single doctrine.

## Why it matters

Most people live as if their time were unlimited, which makes it easy to spend it on grudges, status contests and things they do not much value. Marcus argues that since a person can lose only the present moment, a long life and a short one differ less than we think; what matters is how the present is used.[^s3] Remembering death puts ordinary concerns in proportion, much as his habit of viewing each thing against the whole cosmos does.[^s4]

## How to apply it

The steps above turn the teaching into a short daily routine. Two features matter most. First, make it concrete: the sutta's point is that a vague "someday" breeds heedlessness, while a sense that time could end soon produces attention.[^s5] Second, connect it to a review of how you live. Marcus wrote his reflections down as reminders and rules for himself, in line with Epictetus's advice to write and rehearse one's principles in advance; a brief journal entry serves the same purpose.[^s4]

At work, the practice can deflate a feud over credit or a reply written in anger. In relationships, it tends to raise the question of what has gone unsaid. Across traditions it takes different forms, from Stoic morning and evening reflection[^s3] to the graded Buddhist recollection practised down to the length of a breath.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a licence for indulgence.** "Live each day as if it were your last" is often read as permission to grab pleasures. Epictetus gives the opposite reason for remembering death: it should curb excessive craving.[^s2]
- **Making it abstract or occasional.** The sutta criticises recollection held over long, comfortable spans as heedless.[^s5]
- **Dwelling on the frightening imagery.** In laboratory work, a brief abstract reminder of death and a reflection that included life review and thinking of others led to different responses; the reflective version was linked to less greed, the abstract one was not.[^s1]
- **Pushing through distress.** For someone who is grieving, anxious or unwell, contemplating death may do more harm than good. Shorten or set aside the practice, and treat persistent distress as a reason to seek professional or crisis support rather than as a test of resolve.

## Evidence and limits

Memento mori is a traditional teaching, judged by argument and long practice rather than by experiment, so its status here is normative. Modern research gives partial and cautious support to one version of it. In three small studies, Cozzolino and colleagues asked students to imagine dying in a fire and then review their lives and consider their families' reactions. Among students who strongly valued wealth, image and fame, this "death reflection" reduced greedy behaviour in a raffle task, whereas a standard brief mortality reminder did not; the reflective participants wrote more about regrets, their lives and other people.[^s1] The authors note real limits: small mostly undergraduate samples, one scenario and one behavioural measure.[^s1]

The wider research on death reminders is also unsettled. Terror management theory predicted that brief reminders of death make people defend their worldview more fiercely, but a 21-lab replication with more than 2,200 participants, including a protocol designed with the original authors, did not reproduce that effect.[^s6] Popular claims that remembering death reliably makes people kinder, calmer or more purposeful therefore outrun the evidence.

## Connections

Memento mori is rooted in **Stoicism**, where Epictetus prescribes daily remembrance of death,[^s2] and in **Buddhism**, where the *Maraṇassati Sutta* teaches mindfulness of death.[^s5] It aims to **develop equanimity**: Marcus repeatedly argues that death is natural and not to be feared.[^s3] It may help **address greed**, given the death-reflection findings, though the evidence is thin.[^s1] It **contrasts with mortality salience**, the brief, abstract death reminder used in terror management research, which aimed to provoke defensiveness rather than reflection.[^s1] It is a form of **death awareness**, and it sits close to **negative visualization**, **impermanence**, the **view from above**, which Marcus uses to put mortal things in proportion,[^s4] and **life review**, which death reflection deliberately includes.[^s1]
