  - Pick a fixed, short slot at the end of the day, such as the last ten minutes before bed, and keep it whether or not the day went well.
  - Go through the day in order, recalling what you said and did in the main conversations, decisions and tasks, as if replaying it for a fair observer.

# Evening review

The evening review is the habit of looking back over the day before going to sleep: recalling what you said and did, noticing where you acted well and where you fell short of your own standards, and settling what to do differently tomorrow.[^s1][^s2] Its classic form is a short set of questions asked at the same time every night. Seneca describes it as a daily appearance before a judge, but a lenient one: he pardons his faults and resolves to avoid them, rather than dwelling on them.[^s1]

## Origin

The oldest written version is in the Pythagorean *Golden Verses*, a short poem of moral maxims. Epictetus quotes its lines urging that sleep should not come before you have gone over each act of the day, asking what you did, where you went wrong and what duty you left undone.[^s2] The poem was traditionally credited to Pythagoras or his followers, but its date is disputed: one modern editor places it in the fourth century BCE, against more common views of a Hellenistic or early Roman date, and a reviewer judged that dating it is currently impossible.[^s5] So popular claims that Pythagoras himself prescribed the exercise go beyond the evidence.

Seneca gives the fullest ancient account, in *On Anger* (Book III, ch. 36). He credits the practice to the philosopher Sextius, who asked himself each night, in Stewart's translation, "What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day?"[^s1] Seneca adopted it: once the lamp was taken away, he examined the whole day's words and deeds and noted lapses such as having argued too combatively with people who would not listen, or having rebuked someone too harshly.[^s1] He observed that anger softens when it knows it must appear before this judge every day, and that the sleep after such an examination is sweet.[^s1] Epictetus says the verses should be kept ready and applied to one's conduct, not merely recited by rote.[^s2]

A parallel appears independently in the Confucian *Analects* (I.4), where Confucius's disciple Zengzi says he examines himself daily on three points: whether he has been faithful in acting for others, sincere with friends, and diligent in practising what he was taught.[^s3] Much later, Benjamin Franklin, citing the advice of the *Golden Verses* that daily examination is necessary, built an evening question into his daily schedule, asking what good he had done that day, and kept a small book with a page per virtue in which he marked each fault he found on examination.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Wisdom depends on learning from experience, and most experience slips by unexamined. The evening review creates a regular, low-stakes moment to compare conduct with intention while the day is still fresh.[^s1][^s4] The historian of philosophy John Sellars notes that Seneca was not trying to confess sins or uncover a hidden self: the review was a technique for living a more consistent, virtuous and happy life.[^s6] Franklin's experience shows the practice's corrective force for self-knowledge: he was surprised to find himself much fuller of faults than he had imagined, and saw them decrease as he kept at it.[^s4]

The review also cultivates emotional wisdom. Seneca presents it as a remedy for anger, because knowing that one's conduct will be reviewed tonight restrains the impulse during the day.[^s1] In relationships and at work, it gives you the chance to notice a sharp word or a broken promise before it hardens into a pattern, and to repair it the next morning.[^s1][^s3]

## How to apply it

Keep the review short and regular: Seneca made it a nightly habit, and Franklin tied it to a fixed evening slot in his daily routine.[^s1][^s4] Go through the day in order, as Seneca did, and ask the Pythagorean questions about what you did, where you went wrong and what you left undone.[^s2] Choose a few standards that matter to you, as Zengzi did with faithfulness, sincerity and practice, and Franklin with his list of virtues, so the review has a clear yardstick.[^s3][^s4] Close each shortfall with a concrete correction and a pardon, which is how Seneca ends his own examples.[^s1] Modern research suggests also writing down what went well and why: in the "three good things" exercise, people wrote each night about three things that went well and their causes.[^s7]

## Common mistakes

- **Turning review into rumination.** Seneca's version ends in a pardon and sleep, not in hours of replay; if the review leaves you more agitated, shorten it and focus on one fix.[^s1]
- **Only counting faults.** Franklin's black-spot ledger works for tracking habits, but a fault-only review can feel discouraging; adding what went well and why reflects how modern studies run the exercise.[^s4][^s7]
- **Vague verdicts.** "I was bad today" teaches nothing. Seneca names specific acts and specific corrections.[^s1]
- **Treating it as therapy.** The evening review is a reflective habit, not a treatment. If nightly reflection keeps turning into distress, or low mood persists, seek support from a qualified professional.[^s7][^s8]

## Evidence and limits

No study located for this article tests the ancient practice itself, which is judged by argument and long practice, as Sellars' account of its ethical aims suggests.[^s6] Modern positive-only versions have been studied, with **mixed** results. In an online trial of 577 adults, writing three good things each night for a week raised happiness and reduced depressive symptoms for up to six months, while gains in an early-memories placebo group faded within a week; people who kept doing it gained most.[^s7] A later replication found happiness benefits but no reduction in depressive symptoms beyond the placebo, and noted that another replication suggested such exercises may work through a common factor.[^s8] In a three-week field study of employees, a brief end-of-workday positive reflection reduced stress and improved health that evening.[^s9] These samples were largely self-selected and motivated, and outcomes were mostly self-reported.[^s7][^s8]

## Connections

The evening review is **rooted in Stoicism**, through Seneca and Epictetus,[^s1][^s2] and in **Pythagoreanism**, whose **Golden Verses** contain its earliest known formulation,[^s2][^s5] with a close parallel **rooted in Confucianism** in Zengzi's daily self-examination.[^s3] It **develops self-reflection** by giving it a fixed time and purpose,[^s6] and **addresses anger**, as Seneca argued.[^s1] It is **applied in learning from experience**, as Franklin's record of his faults shows.[^s4] Modern evidence comes from the **three-good-things study**, which supports a positive version,[^s7] and the **positive-reflection work-stress study**, which demonstrates an end-of-workday form,[^s9] while the **strength-based interventions replication** challenges claims that the exercise relieves depression.[^s8] It is a personal, daily cousin of the after-action review, a natural place to check whether an implementation intention fired, and a guard against weakness of will; done harshly or at length, it can slide into rumination.[^s1][^s4]
