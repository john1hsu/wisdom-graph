# Self-deception

Self-deception is the familiar but puzzling act of misleading yourself. At a minimum, philosophers describe it as acquiring and holding on to a belief that the evidence counts against, because some motive or emotion favours it, often while acting in ways that hint you know better.[^s1] It matters for wisdom because the person we find hardest to judge fairly is usually ourselves.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Moralists noticed self-deceit long before psychologists measured it. In the *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), Adam Smith argued that we judge our own conduct badly at both ends: before we act, passion distorts what we see, and afterwards we look away from facts that would support an unflattering verdict, because thinking ill of ourselves is painful.[^s5] He called this self-deceit "the source of half the disorders of human life" (Part III, chapter 4).[^s5] Joseph Butler's 1726 sermon on self-deceit made a related charge, that self-ignorance corrupts conscience itself.[^s1]

Philosophers have long debated how self-deception is even possible. If it mirrors lying to someone else, the self-deceiver must both believe and disbelieve the same thing and must intend to fool the very mind that holds the intention, two puzzles known as the static and dynamic paradoxes.[^s1] Some answer by dividing the self over time or into parts; deflationary accounts, of which Alfred Mele's is the best known, say no intention is needed: a desire simply biases how a person gathers and weighs evidence, so the belief forms through reasoning the person wrongly takes to be fair.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Self-deception damages the capacity that would otherwise correct it. Critics argue that it can enable harm to others and oneself, weakens our ability to scrutinise and change ourselves, and alienates us from our own principles; existentialists such as Sartre treated it as a basic failure of authenticity.[^s1] It also scales up. Groups can share a comforting belief and reinforce it socially, and organisations can become blind to the ethical side of their own decisions, a process researchers call ethical fading.[^s1]

The everyday version is the inflated self-view. People on average rate themselves above average and give optimistic estimates for when their projects will finish; self-ratings of skill track actual performance only weakly in many fields, and others sometimes predict our outcomes better than we do.[^s4] At work this makes honest feedback hard to give and hear at every level.[^s4]

## Evidence

Lab studies support the core idea that people convince themselves of flattering things. In four experiments, participants given a chance to cheat on a test scored higher and then credited the inflated score to their own intelligence; they expected to do just as well on a later test where cheating was impossible, even when that expectation proved costly.[^s3] People expected that they might cheat but did not foresee that they would fool themselves, and bigger rewards for cheating increased the self-deception.[^s3] These were short laboratory tasks, and we found no large independent replication; the authors' own lesson is that self-deception's short-term comfort and long-term cost should be weighed over time.[^s3]

Why the tendency exists is less settled. William von Hippel and Robert Trivers argue that self-deception evolved to help people deceive others, by hiding the cues that betray a lie, easing the mental load of lying and letting people show more confidence than their abilities warrant.[^s2] Critics reply that the supporting data are thin, that people are poor lie detectors anyway, and that the varied phenomena grouped as self-deception may not share one evolved mechanism; some treat it as a by-product of other mental machinery.[^s1] There is also a "vital lie" tradition that holds some self-deception protects people from truths too painful to live with.[^s1] Because the phenomenon is well documented but its mechanisms, benefits and even its definition remain disputed, its status here is *mixed*.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

## How to spot it

Look for an asymmetry in how you treat evidence: good news is accepted at once, bad news is audited, explained away or not sought.[^s1] Notice when a belief is one you would very much like to be true, or when being wrong would be costly to your pride or plans; that is where motivation does its work.[^s1] Smith's test still helps: be suspicious of verdicts on your own conduct reached while a strong feeling is running, and of a reluctance to look back at what you did once it has cooled.[^s5] A persistent gap between how you think you come across and what others tell you is another sign.[^s6]

## How to fix it

Self-deception cannot be switched off, but it can be resisted. Philosophers note that many sources of bias are within our control, especially on matters we take seriously, and point to cultivating impartiality, vigilance and conscientiousness, and to keeping company with people who will hold us to account.[^s1] Smith's remedy was to form general rules of conduct in calm moments, from watching how others' actions strike us, so that they can check us when self-love makes a choice look justified.[^s5] In the same spirit, you can decide in a calm moment, before the results arrive, what would count as failure.[^s5]

Outside views are the other main tool. In Simine Vazire's study of 165 people, friends judged intellect-related traits such as intelligence and creativity better than people judged themselves, while people knew their own anxiety better; she urges taking it seriously when others say you do not come across as you intend.[^s6] Self-assessment researchers likewise recommend explaining your decisions to peers and building in routine checks on your own estimates of skill and progress.[^s4] Where facing a hard truth brings grief or persistent distress, talking with someone you trust or a professional is wiser than forcing it alone.

## Connections

Self-deception **undermines self-knowledge**, the capacity for honest self-scrutiny, and works against the maxim **know thyself**.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** the cheating-and-self-assessment experiments of Chance and colleagues and **supported by** the **flawed self-assessment** review.[^s3][^s4] It is tentatively **explained by** von Hippel and Trivers's **evolutionary theory**, which is in turn **challenged by** the view that self-deception is a **by-product** rather than an adaptation.[^s1][^s2] Smith's **Theory of Moral Sentiments** and his **impartial spectator** are an early account of the problem and its remedy.[^s5] **Honesty** and **intellectual humility** are the virtues that counter it; **seeking counsel** and **self-reflection** are practical checks; and its self-flattering form shades into **hubris** and the inside-view blindness of **Solomon's paradox**.[^s1][^s4][^s6]
