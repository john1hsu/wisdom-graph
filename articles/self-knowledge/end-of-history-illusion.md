# End-of-history illusion

The end-of-history illusion is the gap between how much people say they have changed and how much they expect to change. Asked to look back ten years, people of every adult age describe real shifts in their personality, values and tastes; asked to look ahead ten years, they predict far less, as though growth stops at the present moment.[^s1] It matters for wisdom because many of life's largest choices are made on behalf of a future self who will not share all of today's priorities.[^s1]

## Origin

The term comes from a 2013 paper in *Science* by Jordi Quoidbach, Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson, drawing on more than 19,000 adults.[^s1] Some participants rated their personality as it was a decade earlier, others predicted it a decade later, and predicted change was consistently smaller than the change people of the same age reported having lived through. The same gap appeared for core values and for preferences such as favourite music, food and friends, and it persisted, though smaller, among people over fifty.[^s1] The authors summed up the pattern in one line: "History, it seems, is always ending today."[^s1]

## Why it matters

The illusion has a price. In one study, people said they would pay about 61 per cent more to see their current favourite band in ten years' time than others would pay today to see the band they had loved ten years before, in effect overpaying to indulge a taste that is likely to fade.[^s1] The authors argued that the same error leads people to make career, relationship and lifestyle commitments that their future selves come to regret.[^s1] Meanwhile, the longitudinal record shows that change is real: a meta-analysis of 92 studies found meaningful shifts in every major trait domain, with conscientiousness and emotional stability rising through young adulthood and midlife and change continuing into old age.[^s2]

## Evidence

The original paper checked its retrospective reports against the MIDUS longitudinal study, where personality measured a decade apart changed about as much as people recalled and more than predictors expected, suggesting the gap lies at least partly in forecasting rather than memory.[^s1] Using thirty years of MIDUS data, Harris and Busseri reported that most people predicted their life satisfaction accurately or overestimated how much it would change.[^s4] Quoidbach and colleagues reanalysed the same data within individuals and found that people did underestimate how much their satisfaction would change, mainly from the forties to the seventies.[^s3] Busseri and Harris replied that the two teams were measuring different things: when the direction of change is counted, younger adults expected too much improvement and older adults too much decline, and only about a third under-predicted absolute change.[^s4] A cross-cultural study found the illusion in both the United States and Japan but larger among Americans, partly explained by self-esteem and self-concept clarity; it measured perceived rather than actual change.[^s5] On balance the effect is well documented for personality, values and preferences, while its size depends on culture, age, the domain studied and how change is measured.[^s3][^s4][^s5]

## How to spot it

Watch for sentences that freeze the present: "I'll always want this," "I'm just not that kind of person," "this is who I am now." Suspicion is warranted when a hard-to-reverse commitment is justified mainly by a taste or value adopted recently.[^s1] One explanation the researchers offer is motivational: people tend to like who they are now and want to believe they know themselves, so imagined change feels like a threat.[^s1][^s5] Another is cognitive: picturing how you might change is harder than remembering how you did change, and difficulty can be mistaken for unlikelihood.[^s1]

## How to fix it

Start by looking backwards before you look forwards: list how your views, friendships and goals differed a decade ago, and assume a similar amount of movement ahead.[^s1][^s2] For decisions about how something will feel, ask people who have already lived it. In two experiments, forecasts based on another person's reported experience were markedly more accurate than forecasts built by imagining the event, yet most participants still believed imagination would serve them better.[^s6] Where you can, prefer choices that stay revisable, such as trials, rentals and staged commitments, so that a future self with different priorities is not trapped.[^s1] Treat confident claims about who you will always be with the same intellectual humility you would apply to any other forecast.[^s5]

## Connections

The illusion sits under **self-knowledge**, which it **undermines** by giving people a confident but incomplete picture of themselves,[^s5] and it **undermines** the **major life decision**, where commitments outlast the preferences that motivated them.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** the 2013 *Science* studies[^s1] and **supported by** the **meta-analysis of mean-level personality change**, which shows that adults keep changing.[^s2] It is partly **explained by** **self-enhancement**, the wish to see one's current self favourably.[^s5] It is **challenged by** the **MIDUS life-satisfaction study**, which found many people over- rather than under-predict change in that domain.[^s4] Related ideas include **transformative experience** and Ullmann-Margalit's essay on **big decisions**, both of which ask how to choose for a future self you cannot fully know; **regret**, which the illusion can set up; **hedonic adaptation**, a neighbouring forecasting error about feelings; **self-deception**; and **seeking counsel**, whose evidence base includes the finding that other people's experience predicts our own feelings better than imagination does.[^s6] Keeping big choices **reversible** and holding self-predictions with **intellectual humility** are the main practical responses.[^s1]
