# Self-knowledge

Self-knowledge is knowing what is actually true of yourself. Philosophers usually start with the narrow case, knowing your own current thoughts, feelings, beliefs and desires, and ask why that seems so much easier than knowing someone else's mind.[^s1] For living wisely, the broader case matters more: knowing your enduring character traits, abilities, values and typical reactions, which the philosopher Quassim Cassam calls *substantial* self-knowledge, as opposed to the *trivial* knowledge that you currently believe you are wearing socks.[^s2] Psychology adds a sobering finding: people's beliefs about their own personality match outside evidence only moderately, with predictable blind spots.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

The ideal is ancient and found in many traditions. In the *Analects* (1.4), Confucius's disciple Zengzi says he examines himself every day on three points: whether he has been loyal in acting for others, sincere with friends, and diligent in practising what he was taught.[^s7] In the Pali Canon, the Buddha tells his son Rahula to use his conduct as a mirror, reflecting before, during and after any act of body, speech or mind on whether it harms himself, others or both, and to disclose harmful acts to a teacher or wise companion.[^s6] Both treat self-knowledge as knowledge of one's conduct and character, gained by regular review, rather than as a single moment of discovery.

Modern philosophy took a narrower route. Descartes treated knowledge of one's own thoughts as uniquely certain, and Locke described introspection as a kind of inner sense, a model in which we observe our minds much as we observe the world.[^s1] Later accounts proposed other routes: interpreting our own thoughts and behaviour, or answering "Do I believe this?" by looking outward at the evidence for the thing itself, a "transparency" method associated with Gareth Evans.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most wise choices rest on some accurate self-knowledge: what you really value, what you are good and bad at, and how you tend to act under pressure. Monika Ardelt's three-dimensional model of wisdom builds this in: its reflective dimension, seeing events from several perspectives, is said to require self-examination, self-awareness and self-insight.[^s5] Erika Carlson's review notes that the blind spots in people's self-views carry fairly negative consequences, which is why the limits of self-knowledge are worth understanding rather than assuming away.[^s3]

## What self-knowledge involves

Research on personality points to an uneven map. Simine Vazire's self–other knowledge asymmetry model holds that people know their hard-to-observe inner states, such as anxiety, better than others do, while friends and family are as good or better judges of visible behaviour such as talkativeness, and clearly better judges of highly evaluative traits such as intelligence.[^s4] Vazire's summary is to trust a person about their thoughts and feelings, and their friends about their best and worst traits.[^s4] Carlson names two barriers behind the blind spots: missing information, since we cannot watch ourselves fidget or frown, and the motive to protect a flattering self-image.[^s3]

## How to build it

Treat substantial self-knowledge as something you infer from evidence. Cassam argues that we learn our traits and values much as we learn other people's, from past behaviour and from inner cues such as imagery and inner speech, not by a special inward glance.[^s2] In practice that means reviewing what you actually did, not only what you meant, as the Rahula teaching and Zengzi's daily review both do.[^s6][^s7] It means asking people who know you well about your visible habits and your most charged traits, where their view is most informative.[^s4] Carlson proposes that mindfulness, attending to present experience without judging it, could help with both barriers by letting people notice more and defend less, though she presents this as a hypothesis to be tested.[^s3]

At work this might mean asking a colleague how you come across in meetings; in relationships, hearing a partner's account of a recurring argument before giving yours. Across traditions the common thread is review of conduct plus an outside witness.[^s4][^s6]

## Common misreadings

The commonest mistake is to equate self-knowledge with introspection. Introspection is a good guide to some current states, but substantial self-knowledge is inferential and fallible, and people often misidentify what actually drove their choices.[^s2][^s1] A second is to assume first-person authority settles every question about you; philosophers now rarely defend infallibility, even about one's own states.[^s1] A third is to swing to the opposite extreme and treat others' views as always correct: Vazire's work shows that close others are biased too, though their judgements still track reality.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The philosophical debate about how self-knowledge works remains open, with acquaintance, inner-sense, interpretive, rationalist and agential accounts all defended.[^s1] Empirically, the self–other asymmetry is well supported for personality traits in Vazire's studies, but accuracy varies by trait and by who is judging, so the effects are real yet context-dependent.[^s4] Evidence that mindfulness improves self-knowledge is still preliminary.[^s3] Carruthers, reviewing Cassam, adds that general findings from cognitive science about human minds, such as implicit bias, are also a source of self-knowledge that individual self-observation misses.[^s2]

## Connections

Self-knowledge is a broader topic in the study of **wisdom**, and in Ardelt's framework it is **part of the three-dimensional wisdom model** through the self-examination its reflective dimension requires.[^s5] Its uneven accuracy is **explained by the self–other knowledge asymmetry model**, and it is **studied in personality psychology**.[^s4] Because others see what we miss, it **complements seeking counsel**.[^s4] It is often **confused with introspection**, which is only one of its sources.[^s2] The practice of daily self-examination roots it in **Confucianism** (*Analects* 1.4), and the Buddha's mirror teaching shows a parallel in early Buddhism.[^s7][^s6] It is the broader idea behind **know thyself** and **self-reflection**, is eroded by **self-deception**, and underlies **personal wisdom** and the self–other gap of **Solomon's paradox**.[^s3][^s5]
