# Know thyself

"Know thyself" (Greek *gnōthi seauton*) is probably the most famous piece of advice in Western thought. It was inscribed at the temple of Apollo at Delphi, and ever since, people have argued about what it asks of us.[^s1][^s3] Read one way, it is a warning: remember that you are a limited, fallible human being. Read another, it is an invitation: examine your own character, beliefs and ignorance before you try to run your life or anyone else's.[^s4] Modern psychology adds a twist the ancients partly anticipated: people do not know themselves well by looking inward alone.[^s5][^s6]

## Origin

The Greek travel writer Pausanias, describing Delphi, reports that the forecourt of the temple carried maxims useful for human life, which Greek tradition credited to the sages. He names Thales, Bias, Pittacus, Cleobulus, Solon, Chilon and Myson, and says they dedicated "Know thyself" and "Nothing in excess" to Apollo.[^s1] That is a report of tradition, not proof of authorship: ancient writers disagreed about which sage coined which saying, so the maxim is best treated as anonymous Delphic wisdom rather than a quotation from any one person.[^s1][^s4]

Its early sense seems to have been deflationary. On this reading it told visitors to the god to know what kind of being they were: mortal, limited and liable to error, not divine.[^s4] Christopher Moore's study of the precept argues that the Greeks did not regard its meaning as obvious; it needed interpreting before it could be obeyed, and Socrates inherited a culture that both revered and puzzled over it.[^s3] In Plato's *Phaedrus* (229e–230a), Socrates says he has no time to explain away myths because he must first know himself, as the Delphic inscription says, and he wonders whether he is a creature more tangled than the monster Typhon or a gentler, simpler one.[^s2] Plato's *Charmides* and *Alcibiades* return to the theme, and Moore argues that for Socrates self-knowledge was ethical and social: shaping oneself into a person whom knowledge can guide, largely through conversation with friends and critics.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Almost every wise decision depends on some self-knowledge: what you actually value, where your judgment is weak, what you are likely to do under pressure. The maxim's first sense guards against hubris, the overreach of someone who forgets their limits; its Socratic sense asks you to find out what you do not know before you act as if you did.[^s4][^s2]

The psychology of self-knowledge shows why this is hard. Timothy Wilson and Elizabeth Dunn's review concludes that much of mental life is simply not open to introspection, and that people also keep unwelcome thoughts and feelings out of view.[^s5] Introspection tends to construct a plausible story rather than read off the truth, and some kinds backfire: analysing the reasons for one's feelings can lower satisfaction with choices and the accuracy of predictions, and brooding while distressed deepens negative thinking.[^s5] Simine Vazire and Erika Carlson's review argues that self-perceptions are largely accurate but have blind spots, partly from missing information and partly from motivated distortion, so close others sometimes see traits we cannot, especially highly desirable or undesirable ones.[^s6]

## How to apply it

The research suggests three routes that work better than solitary soul-searching. First, watch what you do, not only what you feel: self-perception research suggests people infer their attitudes from their behaviour, and deliberately reviewing your actions may make those inferences better.[^s5] Second, ask people who know you well, and treat what they say as data, since peers often agree with each other about us and sometimes predict our behaviour better than we can.[^s5][^s6] Third, make sense of hard experiences by building a coherent account of them, as in expressive writing, rather than circling them.[^s5] This echoes Moore's reading of Socrates, for whom self-knowledge was pursued in dialogue rather than alone.[^s3]

At work, this looks like asking a trusted colleague how you come across in meetings; in relationships, like hearing a partner's account of a recurring argument before defending your own. In both, the Delphic warning applies too: no amount of reflection will make you a perfect judge of yourself.[^s4][^s6]

## Common misreadings

The popular modern version treats "know thyself" as pure introspection or as a quest to find a fixed "true self". The ancient sense was closer to knowing your place and limits, and the Socratic sense to recognising your ignorance and improving your character.[^s4][^s3] A second misreading is to assume you are always the final authority on yourself. Others' views are not always right either, so the aim is to combine perspectives: you have access to your inner states, and they see your behaviour and the traits you are motivated to misjudge.[^s6] Finally, more self-scrutiny is not always better: Wilson and Dunn note that mild positive illusions can be beneficial and that some kinds of self-analysis make judgment worse.[^s5]

## Evidence

As a maxim, "know thyself" is a philosophical teaching judged by argument and long practice rather than experiment.[^s3] The empirical work bears on how to follow it: reviews find that introspection has real limits and that combining self-views with observers' views gives a fuller picture of personality.[^s5][^s6] Those findings concern people's knowledge of their traits and motives, and do not settle what kind of self-knowledge matters most for living well, which remains a philosophical question.[^s3][^s5]

## Connections

"Know thyself" is **part of the Delphic maxims**, the sayings Pausanias describes in the temple's forecourt beside "Nothing in excess".[^s1] It sits under the broader topic of **self-knowledge** and is **rooted in Socratic philosophy**, which made the precept central to its method.[^s3] Read as a reminder of human limits, it **addresses hubris** and **develops intellectual humility**.[^s4] Because others can see traits we misjudge, it **complements seeking counsel**.[^s6] It is closely related to **Socratic ignorance**, its best-known interpretation; to **self-reflection** and **self-distancing**, which offer ways of looking inward without brooding; and to the **introspection illusion**, the limits of looking inward that Wilson and Dunn describe.[^s2][^s5]
