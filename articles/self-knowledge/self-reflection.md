# Self-reflection

Self-reflection is the deliberate act of examining your own feelings, motives and behaviour so that you understand them better and can change course. Psychologists treat it as one half of a cycle: reflection is the monitoring step, in which you look at yourself, and **insight** is the understanding that makes change possible.[^s2] The distinction matters because the two can come apart. People can reflect a great deal and still learn little.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

The idea that a good life requires examining oneself is ancient. In Plato's *Apology*, Socrates tells the jury that talking daily about virtue and examining himself and others is the greatest good for a human being, and adds that "the unexamined life is not worth living" (*Apology* 38a).[^s1] For Socrates, self-examination and the questioning of others were one practice, aimed at finding out how to live rather than at private self-absorption.[^s1]

Modern psychology took up the topic through research on *self-consciousness*, the tendency to attend to one's inner states. Trapnell and Campbell addressed a puzzle, the "self-absorption paradox": people who attend closely to themselves tend to have more extensive and accurate self-knowledge, yet also more psychological distress.[^s3] Their explanation was that the older measure lumped together two unrelated motives, a reflective and a ruminative one.[^s3] **Reflection** is self-exploration pursued out of curiosity, a love of understanding oneself; **rumination** is a repetitive, anxious return to one's past actions and failings.[^s3][^s4] Anthony Grant and colleagues later built a scale that separates engaging in self-reflection from actually gaining insight, noting that not every act of reflection yields useful understanding.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Wisdom researchers give reflection a central place. Monika Ardelt's three-dimensional model treats the reflective dimension, the willingness to look at events from many perspectives and to examine oneself, as the one that reduces self-centeredness, blame and projection, and so makes clearer understanding and compassion possible.[^s5][^s8] Ardelt and Dilip Jeste call it the cornerstone of wisdom.[^s5] In a sample of 994 adults aged 51 to 99, reflection was the only wisdom dimension that independently predicted well-being, and people high in reflection showed a much weaker link between adverse life events and lower well-being.[^s5]

At work, reflection is how experience turns into learning. In a field study at a call centre in Bangalore, trainees who spent the last 15 minutes of each day writing about what they had learned scored about 23 per cent higher on a final test than colleagues who used the time to keep working; laboratory follow-ups pointed to increased self-efficacy as one mechanism.[^s6] An expanded, still unpublished set of ten studies suggests people tend to prefer more practice over reflecting on practice, and that reflection helps most early in learning.[^s7]

## What good looks like

Good self-reflection is curious rather than defensive, specific rather than global, and it ends somewhere. Trapnell and Campbell's work implies that the motive behind the attention matters as much as the amount: the reflective motive goes with self-knowledge, the ruminative one with distress.[^s3] Grant and colleagues suggest that deliberate reflection, such as keeping a journal, is more likely to be useful than automatic self-scrutiny.[^s2] And the payoff lies in insight. In one study of 121 adults, insight predicted every dimension of psychological well-being and life satisfaction, whereas the amount of self-reflection showed little link to well-being beyond personal growth, and rumination predicted lower well-being.[^s4]

Reflection in Ardelt's sense also looks outward: seeing events from others' viewpoints and accepting one's own part, rather than settling on blame.[^s5][^s8]

## How to practise it

- **Set a time and a limit.** Reflect on purpose, for example in a short written review at the end of the day or after a project, rather than letting thoughts loop whenever they will.[^s2][^s6]
- **Start from curiosity.** Ask what happened, what you expected and where the gap opened, as an investigator would, not as a judge.[^s3][^s4]
- **Write it down.** Deliberate, written reflection is what Grant and colleagues recommend over automatic self-scrutiny, and it is the form the workplace study used.[^s2][^s6]
- **Take other perspectives.** Ask how the others involved saw it, and what part you played, before assigning causes.[^s5]
- **End with an insight or a step.** Name one thing you now understand or will do differently; if nothing new comes after several rounds, stop.[^s4]

If reflection keeps turning into distress you cannot set down, that is a sign to stop and, if it persists, to talk to a professional rather than to reflect harder.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is **mixed**. The reflection-rumination distinction is well established in personality research, but most studies linking reflection to wisdom and well-being are cross-sectional and self-reported, so they cannot show that reflecting causes the benefits.[^s3][^s5] Measures of the *amount* of self-reflection often show weak or no links to well-being; insight does the work.[^s4] Grant's original study even found self-reflection associated with more anxiety and stress, while insight went with less anxiety, stress and depression, as Harrington and Loffredo report.[^s4] The workplace experiments are promising but come from a working paper not yet published in a peer-reviewed journal.[^s6][^s7]

## Connections

Self-reflection is **rooted in Socratic philosophy**, which made self-examination central to the good life.[^s1] It **complements insight**: reflection supplies the attention, insight the understanding.[^s2] It **contrasts with rumination**, its anxious, repetitive twin, though the two are easily confused.[^s3] It is **part of** Ardelt's **three-dimensional wisdom model** as its reflective dimension, where it **addresses self-centeredness**; its role is **demonstrated by** Ardelt and Jeste's **study of wisdom in hard times**, which also shows it **applied in adversity**.[^s5] It is **applied in learning from experience** at work.[^s6] It is broader than **self-distancing**, one technique for reflecting without ruminating, and it serves the ancient injunction **know thyself**.[^s1][^s3]
