steps:
  - Write down two or three possible selves you are drawn to, and one you fear becoming, without choosing between them yet.
  - For each, design a small experiment you can run alongside your current life, such as a side project, a short course, a volunteer role or a temporary assignment.
  - Seek out people who already live each possibility, outside your usual circle, and spend real time with them rather than only reading about the path.
  - Run the experiments for a set period, and note after each one what energised you, what felt false, and what feedback others gave you.
  - Borrow selectively from several role models instead of copying one person wholesale, and keep what fits.
  - Revisit your story about who you are every few months, and only then scale up the experiment that keeps pulling you, while keeping a way back.
example:
  situation: An accountant in her late thirties keeps wondering whether she would be happier teaching. She has spent a year reading about career change and taking personality tests, and still feels unsure.

  after: "I'll tutor two evenings a week this term, sit in on a colleague's training sessions, and have coffee with three teachers about their actual week. In four months I'll know far more about whether the teaching self fits."

# Identity experiments

Identity experiments are a way of finding out who you are becoming by trying on possible selves in small, real situations and learning from what happens, rather than waiting for introspection to reveal a single true self before acting.[^s2][^s3] The organisational researcher Herminia Ibarra, who developed the idea, sums up the reversal as "doing comes first, knowing second".[^s4] The practice treats identity during a transition as provisional: something tested, revised and only gradually claimed.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea grew out of Ibarra's qualitative field research. In a 1999 study of 34 junior consultants and investment bankers moving into more senior, client-facing roles, she found that people adapted by watching role models, trying out "provisional selves" with clients, and keeping or discarding each image according to how authentic it felt and how others responded.[^s1] A later three-year study followed 39 mid-career professionals, aged roughly 32 to 51, who were considering a major change of work.[^s3][^s4] She reported that people who relied mainly on self-reflection, trusted advisers and grand plans often stayed stuck for months or years, and proposed a "test and learn" alternative built on three practices: crafting experiments, shifting connections and making sense of the change.[^s2] The approach draws on the psychology of possible selves, the hoped-for and feared versions of oneself that people carry and act towards.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Many of life's largest choices are made on behalf of a future self who does not yet exist. Quoidbach, Gilbert and Wilson found that adults of every age expect to change much less over the next decade than they report having changed over the last, and that this leads them to overpay for future chances to indulge present tastes.[^s5] Ibarra's work points to a related trap: people try to settle who they are by personality tests and solitary reflection, when identity is largely formed through activity and relationships.[^s3] A writer in *Harvard Business Review* recommends recognising past growth and actively redefining one's identity as ways to counter the end-of-history illusion; identity experiments are one concrete form that redefinition can take.[^s6]

## How to apply it

Start with several possibilities rather than one answer. Ibarra describes the period between identities as one in which the self is "multiple, ill defined and provisional", and treats that openness as useful.[^s3] Craft small experiments, such as side projects, courses, weekend ventures or temporary assignments, that stretch you beyond routine while you keep your current role.[^s4] Shift your connections: people who know you best often see only your past self, so look for role models and peers who already live the possibility.[^s4] Borrow selectively from several models rather than imitating one person wholesale; in the 1999 study, wholesale copying tended to produce ill-fitting personas, while selective borrowing let people build a style of their own.[^s1] Then make sense of the results, revising your story about who you are as small changes in activities, relationships and narrative add up.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

The commonest mistake is waiting for certainty: the research suggests that clarity tends to follow action rather than precede it.[^s4] A second is the dramatic leap, resigning and retraining in one move, when change usually proceeds through small, accumulating steps that can be tested on a limited but tangible scale.[^s3][^s4] A third is claiming a new identity too early, before it has been tried enough to know whether it fits.[^s3] A fourth is staying true to an old self so firmly that ineffective approaches are kept long after feedback shows they do not work; in the 1999 study, people who leaned on familiar styles and avoided new behaviours were slower to drop images that were failing.[^s1] Finally, the practice assumes some room to experiment, in time, money and social support, which not everyone has in equal measure.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is mainly qualitative. Ibarra's studies were inductive and designed to generate theory, not to test it: the 1999 sample was small and non-random, drawn from two firms, and the author describes the findings as speculative.[^s1] The career-change work rests on interviews with 39 people, and Ibarra notes that most research on mid-career crises comes from the United States and Europe, where the ideal of self-actualisation may be culturally specific.[^s3][^s4] No controlled trial has tested identity experiments as a remedy for the end-of-history illusion, so that link is a reasoned inference from the illusion's mechanism rather than a demonstrated effect.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Identity experiments are a practice of **self-knowledge** and of **learning from experience**, grounded in the idea that we come to know ourselves partly by acting.[^s3] They were **introduced by** **Herminia Ibarra**[^s1] and are **explained by** the theory of **possible selves**, the repertoire of hoped-for and feared selves people try out.[^s1] The practice **addresses** the **end-of-history illusion** by treating the present self as one draft among several, though that link is tentative.[^s6][^s5] It is **applied in** **career change**[^s2] and in other **major life decisions** where the destination is unclear at the outset.[^s3] It **complements** a **developmental network**, since new relationships outside one's usual circle carry much of the change,[^s4] and the principle of keeping choices **reversible**, because small experiments let a person test a path without leaping into the unknown.[^s3] Related ideas include **transformative experience** and Ullmann-Margalit's essay on **big decisions**, both concerned with choosing for a self one cannot fully foresee; **seeking counsel**, especially from people who have lived the path; **emulating moral exemplars**, a parallel practice of learning from role models; and **reflective journaling**, a natural tool for the making-sense stage.[^s1][^s5]
