steps:
  - Divide your life into chapters - by place, decade, role or relationship - and give each chapter a short title.
  - For each chapter, recall concrete scenes in as much detail as you can, starting with good memories before turning to hard ones.
  - Evaluate each chapter rather than only recalling it - ask what went well, what went badly, what you would do differently and what is still unresolved.
  - For difficult episodes, ask how you got through them and which of those coping skills you could use on a problem you face now.
  - Weigh the whole - name the people, values and threads that run through the chapters, and write a one-paragraph version of your life story as you now understand it.
  - Turn the review toward the future by choosing one or two achievable goals, repairs or conversations that follow from what you found.

# Life review

A life review is a deliberate look back over the whole of one's life in order to evaluate it: recalling its chapters, weighing what went well and badly, and revisiting conflicts that were never resolved, so that the past can be accepted and woven into a more coherent story.[^s1] It differs from ordinary reminiscence, which is simply recalling past events; a life review asks you to judge and reinterpret them.[^s1][^s6] Researchers therefore distinguish three levels: simple reminiscence, life review, and life-review therapy for people with mental-health problems.[^s6]

## Origin

The psychiatrist Robert Butler introduced the idea in the early 1960s; his 1963 paper in *Psychiatry* is the usual reference.[^s1] At the time, older people's talk about the past was often dismissed as a sign of decline or even senility. Butler argued instead that it reflected a normal inner process of reviewing one's life, which he believed occurs in everyone in their final years.[^s1] He noted that it can end in reconciliation, atonement, serenity and acceptance, or, when people judge their lives harshly, in guilt and despair.[^s1] Later practitioners, among them Barbara Haight and Irene Burnside, turned the idea into structured life-review sessions, and the gerontologist James Birren formalised it in Guided Autobiography groups, which he said were not therapy but could be very therapeutic.[^s1][^s6]

## Why it matters

A long life does not guarantee wisdom: experiences have to be reflected on and integrated before they teach anything.[^s6] Life review is one of the main ways people do this. Jeffrey Webster's model of wisdom lists reflective, non-ruminative autobiographical reflection as one of its core elements, because it lends perspective and helps people set goals for the future.[^s6] His Self-Assessed Wisdom Scale includes a reminiscence-and-reflectiveness subscale, which describes evaluative, integrative reflection on one's past as a resource for coping with later difficulties.[^s5] It is also a practical tool when facing critical life events such as retirement or a career change: in older adults, reminiscence aimed at solving problems and recalling past coping was linked to how they handled such events, and life review links what you have learned to the goals you set next.[^s4][^s7]

## How to apply it

What sets structured versions apart from ordinary reminiscing is evaluation: memories are reassessed, not only recalled.[^s1][^s2] A Dutch group course based on life review and narrative therapy, "The stories we live by", ran for eight two-hour sessions. Jojanneke Korte, who evaluated it for her doctoral research, identified four active ingredients: recalling positive memories in vivid detail, weighing positive against negative events to soften bitterness, reviewing how past difficulties were handled to help with current problems, and setting achievable future goals for people who feel stuck in the past.[^s4][^s7] Groups add something that solitary review lacks: self-disclosure, recognition from others and the discovery that others have problems too.[^s4] On your own, a notebook organised by life chapters, or a conversation with a trusted friend, can serve a similar purpose; the steps above follow these ingredients.[^s4][^s7]

## Common mistakes

- **Recall without evaluation.** Pleasant reminiscing has its value, but meta-analyses find that full life review, with evaluation, helps well-being more than simple reminiscence.[^s2]
- **Bitterness revival.** Returning again and again to old grievances is a form of reminiscence linked to distress; good life review balances the hard chapters with the good ones and looks for meaning and mastery in both.[^s4]
- **Treating memory as a record.** Memoirs and life stories are interpretations made in hindsight, not accurate records, so hold the story you construct with some humility.[^s1]
- **Going it alone through deep pain.** Reviewing a life can be painful, and for people who believe they have done something unforgivable it can bring up intense guilt or panic.[^s1] Life review is not a substitute for care: if looking back stirs up persistent distress or low mood, seek support from a qualified professional; the clinical form of life review is delivered by trained practitioners.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is encouraging but **mixed**. A meta-analysis of 15 controlled studies found a moderate effect (about 0.54) of reminiscence on older adults' life satisfaction and emotional well-being, with life review outperforming simple reminiscence and larger effects in community-dwelling adults than in care homes; the authors called for replications with well-defined protocols.[^s2] A larger meta-analysis of 128 controlled trials found moderate improvements in ego integrity and depression and small gains in purpose in life, mastery, preparation for death, well-being and social integration, most of which held at follow-up; effects on depression were largest in people who were already depressed or had chronic illness.[^s3] In a pragmatic trial of "The stories we live by" with about 200 older adults with mild to moderate depressive symptoms, the course reduced symptoms, apparently by reducing bitterness and boredom-driven reminiscence and strengthening mastery and positive thoughts.[^s4][^s7] The main meta-analyses and trials concern older adults, so evidence for younger people is thinner;[^s2][^s4] self-report wisdom measures that include reflectiveness agree poorly with other wisdom measures;[^s5] and although Butler presented the life review as universal, it has been called a Western phenomenon because of its focus on the individual, even though it has since been studied in Asian as well as Western countries.[^s1]

## Connections

Life review was **introduced by Robert Butler** and is chiefly **applied in ageing**, where he first observed it.[^s1] Because experience only becomes wisdom when it is reflected on, it is also **applied in learning from experience**,[^s6] and it **develops self-reflection**, the reflective dimension that self-report wisdom scales try to measure.[^s5] By turning repetitive grievance into evaluation and meaning, it **addresses rumination**.[^s4] It is **supported by** two meta-analyses of reminiscence interventions[^s2][^s3] and **demonstrated by** the "Stories we live by" trial.[^s4] Birren's guided autobiography is a group form of the same practice.[^s6] It sits alongside the daily evening review and reflective journaling at a longer time scale, connects to regret, meaning in life and purpose in life through its search for a coherent story, and, like memento mori and death awareness, gains urgency from the sense that life is finite.[^s1][^s3]
