# Regret

Regret is the sting of comparing what happened with what might have happened, joined to the sense that your own choice made the difference. Psychologists treat it as a counterfactual emotion: it depends on imagining a better alternative and feeling responsible for missing it.[^s1] It is unpleasant, yet when people are asked to weigh their negative emotions, they rate regret as the most useful of them.[^s2] Whether a regret becomes a lesson or a weight depends largely on whether anything can still be done about it and on how you treat yourself while you look at it.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

Modern research on regret is part of social psychology's study of counterfactual thinking. One influential line, from Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec, proposed a time pattern: in the short run people regret things they did, but looking back over years they regret more the things they failed to do.[^s5] Neal Roese and Amy Summerville later pooled eleven data sets of life regrets (about 3,000 people and 4,000 regrets) to ask what people regret most and why.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Regret is a form of self-knowledge delivered as feeling. It points, often precisely, at what you care about and where your choices fell short of it. In Roese and Summerville's pooled data the most common regrets concerned education, career, romance and parenting, which together with regrets about the self and leisure made up most of all regrets reported.[^s1] Their explanation, the opportunity principle, is that regret is strongest where people see the most chance to change course, because regret exists to prompt corrective action.[^s1] People sense this function: in Saffrey, Summerville and Roese's studies participants credited regret with helping them make sense of the past, avoid repeating mistakes, pursue new opportunities, gain insight into themselves and keep relationships in good repair, and when it was rated against eleven other negative emotions on these functions, none outscored it.[^s2] In their words, "people value their regrets substantially more than they do other negative emotions."[^s2]

## How regret helps and how it harms

The same emotion can do harm. A 2024 systematic review of 31 studies found that more intense life regret goes with lower life satisfaction, more depressive symptoms and poorer reported health.[^s4] The review points to two things that shape the outcome. First, fit with opportunity: trying to undo a regret helped when chances to act were still good, while letting go and investing in other goals was healthier when they were not, a pattern found especially among older adults.[^s4] Second, how people think about the regret: intrusive, repetitive thoughts about it were linked to more negative feeling and depression.[^s4]

Self-compassion seems to turn regret toward growth. In three studies, including an experiment, people who took a kind, understanding view of a recalled regret reported more acceptance of it and more personal improvement afterwards, and acceptance partly explained the gain.[^s3] Kindness here did not mean excusing the choice; it made the lesson easier to look at.[^s3]

Applied wisely, then, regret asks three questions. What exactly do I wish I had done, and what does that reveal about my values?[^s2] Is there still an opening to act, such as a friendship that can be repaired, a course that can be taken or a conversation still possible?[^s6] If not, what goal deserves my effort instead?[^s4] Roese's own practical advice is to act while correction is still possible and to treat past choices as turning points that inform the next ones.[^s6]

## Evidence

The evidence status is **mixed**. The pooled findings on what people regret most rest mainly on convenience samples in which women and well-educated people are over-represented, so the exact ranking may not generalise.[^s1] Laboratory studies support the opportunity principle in the immediate aftermath of an outcome, but reframing an old regret as high- or low-opportunity did not change how intense it felt.[^s1] The popular claim that over a lifetime we regret inactions more than actions has been tested in a pre-registered replication: Yeung and Feldman (2022) supported the original pattern in several studies but with weaker effects, and did not support it in another, so the database records the outcome as mixed.[^s5] Most studies linking regret to well-being are cross-sectional and self-reported, so whether regret lowers well-being or unhappy people regret more remains unclear.[^s4] The self-compassion findings come from a single paper and still await independent replication.[^s3]

Persistent, intrusive regret that keeps disturbing sleep, mood or daily life is a reason to talk with a qualified professional rather than to keep working through it alone.[^s4]

## Connections

Regret sits under **counterfactual thinking**, the capacity to imagine how things could have gone otherwise, and is demonstrated by Roese and Summerville's **what-we-regret-most** synthesis, which also introduced the **opportunity principle** that explains where regret clusters.[^s1] The **praise-for-regret** studies show why people value it despite the pain,[^s2] and Gilovich and Medvec's **temporal-pattern** study, now partly replicated, is the source of the action-versus-inaction distinction.[^s5] Regret **complements** **self-compassion**: kindness towards oneself makes it easier to accept a regret and learn from it.[^s3] When regret turns into intrusive replaying it shades into **rumination**, and techniques such as **self-distancing** that help people reflect without reliving are a natural partner.[^s4] Accepting a regret has close ties to **forgiveness**, which the self-compassion experiment measured alongside acceptance.[^s3] Regret differs with age: older adults report more work and family regrets and, in some studies, feel them less intensely, which links regret to **socioemotional selectivity theory** and the wisdom of letting go in later life.[^s4] It also bears on any **major life decision**, where asking which choice you would most regret not making can bring hidden values to the surface.[^s6] Regret is studied mainly in **social psychology**.[^s1]
