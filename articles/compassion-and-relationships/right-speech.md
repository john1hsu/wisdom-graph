  - Ask next whether it is beneficial, meaning it will help the listener or the situation rather than only relieve your feelings or win the point.
  - If it is true and beneficial but unwelcome, do not swallow it; choose a time when the person can hear it, and plan to say it plainly and gently.

  - While you are speaking, notice whether the words are causing needless hurt, and stop, slow down or rephrase if they are.

# Right speech

Right speech (*sammā vācā*) is the third factor of the Noble Eightfold Path, the Buddha's summary of the way out of suffering, and belongs with right action and right livelihood to the path's training in ethical conduct.[^s3][^s4] The early discourses define it by four abstentions: from lying, from divisive speech, from abusive or harsh speech, and from idle chatter.[^s3] It is more than a list of things not to say. The texts also describe what should be said, and when, which makes right speech a practical discipline for joining honesty to kindness.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The teaching comes from the Pali discourses of early Buddhism. In the *Abhaya Sutta* (MN 58) a prince tries to trap the Buddha into admitting that he sometimes says things that upset people. The Buddha replies with a sorting rule: he does not say what is untrue or unbeneficial, even if it would please; and what is true and beneficial he says whether or not it is welcome, but only when he has a sense of the proper time for saying it.[^s1] His reason, given through the image of a parent pulling a pebble from a child's mouth even if it draws blood, is sympathy for living beings.[^s1] Another discourse lists five marks of well-spoken words: timely, true, affectionate, beneficial and spoken with goodwill.[^s3] In the *Ambalatthika-rahulovada Sutta* (MN 61) the Buddha teaches his son Rahula that someone with no shame about a deliberate lie is as empty as an upturned water dipper, and tells him to examine every verbal act before, during and after it, as one checks one's face in a mirror.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Speech is where most people's ethics are tested most often: dozens of small choices a day about whether to shade the truth, pass on a story or let irritation show. Right speech treats those choices as a training ground, and its tests map onto the wise answer to an old dilemma between being honest and being kind.[^s1] Truth is necessary but not enough; usefulness, timing and goodwill also count, and a pleasing falsehood is never the solution.[^s1][^s3] The discourses also tie speech to the rest of the path: one text says right view, effort and mindfulness all circle around right speech, and another counts reconciling people and speaking gently as purifications of verbal conduct.[^s3]

## How to apply it

The practice works as a short filter plus a habit of review. Before speaking, ask whether the words are true, then whether they are beneficial, then whether this is the time and the tone.[^s1][^s3] What fails the first two tests is dropped; what passes them is said, kindly, even when unwelcome.[^s1] While speaking, notice whether the words are causing needless harm, and afterwards review what happened, admitting and repairing harm rather than hiding it.[^s2] In everyday terms that means declining to join gossip about an absent colleague, giving feedback while it can still be used, and saying "I don't know" rather than inventing a confident answer. In conflicts, the tradition advises against point-scoring debate and in favour of words that bring people back together.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "kind" as "pleasant".** The Buddha in MN 58 explicitly says unwelcome truths when they are beneficial; silence that only spares you awkwardness fails the test.[^s1]
- **Treating "true" as "say it now".** True but useless remarks, and true remarks at a moment when they cannot be heard, are both set aside.[^s1]
- **Policing others' speech.** The canonical advice for correcting someone else begins with examining your own speech, conduct and understanding first.[^s3]
- **Assuming one view of exceptions.** Buddhist schools differ. Some Mahāyāna writers, such as Asaṅga, permit a compassionate bodhisattva to lie to save a being from being killed or seriously harmed, while the early discourses stress the danger of deliberate lies.[^s5][^s2] Scholars also dispute whether Buddhist ethics is best read as a virtue ethics or as a form of consequentialism.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

Right speech is a religious and ethical teaching, judged by argument and long practice rather than experiment, so its status here is normative.[^s3][^s5] Modern research bears only on parts of it. In a three-day field experiment, Emma Levine and Taya Cohen asked people to be completely honest, to be kind, or simply to attend to their communication. People who only imagined the honesty condition expected it to be much less enjoyable and more damaging to relationships than participants found it; actual relational harm did not differ between conditions two weeks later, and honesty proved about as socially connecting as kindness.[^s6] Two preregistered lab studies, including one on giving negative feedback to a close partner, pointed the same way.[^s6] The samples were mostly young and drawn from one city, outcomes were self-reported and the longest follow-up was two weeks, so the findings support the narrower claim that people overestimate the cost of honest talk, not the whole Buddhist practice.[^s6]

## Connections

Right speech is **rooted in** Buddhism and is **part of** the Noble Eightfold Path, within the training in ethical conduct.[^s3][^s4] It **develops** honesty by making truthfulness a daily discipline, and **addresses** lying, the first of its four abstentions.[^s1][^s3] It **complements** compassion, since the Buddha gives sympathy for beings as the reason he speaks hard truths at all.[^s1] It is **applied in** interpersonal conflict through its advice against divisive talk and contentious debate.[^s3] Its view of honest communication is **supported by** Levine and Cohen's finding that people overestimate the costs of honesty.[^s6] It sits close to **prosocial lying**, the kind lie that some Mahāyāna texts allow in extreme cases, to **self-reflection**, through the before-during-after review of MN 61, and to giving **workplace feedback**, where unwelcome truths are most often needed.[^s5][^s2]
