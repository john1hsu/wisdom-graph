# Forgiveness

Forgiveness is a response to being wronged in which you stop holding the wrong against the person who did it, while still judging that what they did was wrong.[^s1] Most philosophers describe its core as overcoming resentment, the moralised anger we feel at being mistreated, and psychologists similarly define it as a deliberate decision to let go of resentment and the wish for revenge, whether or not the offender deserves it.[^s1][^s2] As a virtue, sometimes called *forgivingness*, it is the settled disposition to forgive well: at the right times, for the right reasons and in the right way.[^s1]

## Origin

Forgiveness was not prized as a virtue in classical Greek ethics. Aristotle praised *mildness*, being angry at the right things and people, and the Stoics wrote about clemency, but scholars disagree about whether either amounts to forgiveness.[^s1] The modern philosophical debate starts largely with Joseph Butler's *Fifteen Sermons* (1726), Sermons VIII ("Upon Resentment") and IX ("Upon Forgiveness of Injuries"). Butler held that resentment is natural and useful, since it protects us and deters wrongdoing; the danger is its slide into revenge, and forgiveness forswears revenge and moderates resentment rather than abolishing it.[^s1] Kant later called forgiveness a duty that rules out revenge and hatred but not a meek tolerance of having one's rights trampled.[^s1]

Many religious traditions teach forgiveness or its close kin, each in its own terms.[^s1][^s2] In the Buddhist *Dhammapada*, those who keep replaying "he abused me, he robbed me" never still their hatred, and verse 5 states that "hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world" but by non-hatred.[^s7] That teaching concerns releasing hatred rather than forgiveness as Western philosophers define it, so the two should be compared with care.

## Why it matters

Unforgiven wrongs tend to stay alive through rehearsal: the *Dhammapada*'s point about replaying grievances anticipates what psychologists now study as rumination.[^s7][^s2] Researchers link grudges to stress responses and lower trust and cooperation, and forgiveness to better conflict resolution and repaired relationships.[^s2] Forgiveness also matters for wisdom because it requires holding two things at once: a clear judgement that a wrong was done and a refusal to let that judgement harden into a lasting campaign against the person.[^s1]

## What good looks like

A forgiving person still names the wrong. Forgiving is not **justifying** (if the act was justified there is nothing to forgive), **excusing** (forgiveness presupposes the person was culpable), **forgetting**, legal **pardon**, or **reconciliation**, which most philosophers think forgiveness does not require.[^s1][^s2] It also need not mean dropping accountability: you can let go of resentment while the person still faces consequences.[^s2]

The virtue sits between two failings. Too little is **vindictiveness**, nursing grievances and seeking payback; too much is forgiving too readily, in a way that condones wrongs or fails to respect yourself.[^s1] Philosophers disagree about the conditions. Some hold that forgiveness should be earned through apology, repentance or amends; others allow it to be offered to the unrepentant in the hope of change; and most reject the idea that every victim is obliged to forgive every wrong.[^s1] Psychologists likewise debate whether forgiveness requires warm feelings toward the offender or only the release of hostile ones.[^s2]

## How to practise it

Forgiveness is usually described as a process that takes time, not a single act.[^s2][^s6]

- **Name the hurt first.** Acknowledge what happened and how it affected you, and talk it over with people you trust rather than replaying it alone.[^s6][^s2]
- **Decide what forgiving means here.** Separate it from reconciling, excusing and forgetting, and from whether justice is done.[^s6][^s2]
- **Try empathy without excuse.** Structured programmes such as Everett Worthington's REACH ask you to Recall the hurt, Empathize with the offender, offer forgiveness as an Altruistic gift, Commit to it and Hold on to it when doubts return; Robert Enright's process model moves through uncovering, deciding, working and deepening phases.[^s2][^s4]
- **Notice present thoughts.** Fred Luskin's programme stresses that today's distress comes from how you are thinking about the past now, and pairs that insight with calming techniques.[^s6]
- **Keep boundaries.** Forgiving someone does not oblige you to trust them again; with a repeat or dangerous offender, protection comes first.[^s1][^s5]

Wait until you feel ready, and grieve before you try.[^s6] These are reflective practices, not treatment: if a betrayal or injury leaves you persistently distressed, or you are in danger, seek professional or crisis support.

## Evidence and limits

As a virtue, forgiveness is judged mainly by argument and long practice, so its status here is normative.[^s1] Interventions to promote it have been tested. A systematic review of randomised trials of forgiveness therapy found moderate gains in forgiveness and reductions in depression, anger and stress, while calling for more research.[^s3] An earlier meta-analysis reported effects of about half a standard deviation, which grew with hours of intervention, and a five-week REACH trial with Indian students found gains in forgiveness, positive mood and self-esteem.[^s4] Many trials use students and self-report measures.[^s4][^s6]

Context matters. In studies of married couples, forgiving a partner who made amends or was generally agreeable went with better outcomes, but forgiving a hostile or unrepentant partner was linked to falling satisfaction and self-respect, and sometimes to continued aggression.[^s5] Forgiveness is not good in every circumstance, and wisdom lies in pairing it with self-respect.[^s5][^s1]

## Connections

Forgiveness **addresses vindictiveness**, its vice of deficiency,[^s1] and **rumination**, the replaying of grievances.[^s2] It is often **confused with reconciliation** and with **condonation**, from which philosophers distinguish it.[^s1] It is **applied in interpersonal conflict**,[^s2] and Buddhism teaches its kin, the stilling of hatred (**rooted in Buddhism**).[^s7] It sits under **compassion** and alongside **self-forgiveness**, **equanimity** and **perspective-taking**, and **REACH forgiveness** is one structured way to cultivate it.[^s1][^s2][^s4]
