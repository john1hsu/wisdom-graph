# Tzedakah

*Tzedakah* is the Jewish practice of giving to people in need. The Hebrew word comes from a root meaning righteousness or justice, and the rabbis framed giving to the poor as the poor person's right and the giver's duty, not as a favour.[^s2] Later Jewish usage distinguishes it from *gemilut ḥasadim*, acts of loving-kindness, which go beyond money.[^s2] Philosophers of philanthropy note that this makes tzedakah unusual among traditions of giving: it is a strict obligation, scaled to the giver's means and the recipient's need.[^s3]

## Origin

The Hebrew Bible already sets aside a share of the harvest for the poor, such as gleanings, the corners of fields and a special tithe, and ties generosity to the memory of having been strangers in Egypt.[^s2] The rabbis turned tzedakah into a commandment with detailed laws: everyone must give, including people who live on charity themselves; a tenth of one's income was considered average and a twentieth or less stingy; and the sages at Usha capped giving at a fifth so that givers would not become poor themselves.[^s2] Communities ran a charity fund (*kuppah*) managed by wardens and a soup kitchen (*tamḥui*) for anyone short of food for two meals.[^s2]

The best-known statement of the practice is Moses Maimonides' code of Jewish law, the *Mishneh Torah*, in its Laws of Gifts to the Poor, chapter 10.[^s1] Maimonides says the giver should give cheerfully, share the poor person's troubles and speak comfortingly, and that giving with a sour face forfeits the merit of even a large gift (10:4).[^s1] Someone with nothing to give should still conciliate the person who asks, never scold, because their heart is already broken (10:5).[^s1] He then ranks eight levels of giving (10:7–14). From the top: strengthening someone who has become poor through a gift, loan, partnership or work until they no longer need to ask others, which he calls "the highest level beyond which there is none"; giving when neither side knows the other; when the giver knows the recipient but not the reverse; when the recipient knows the giver but not the reverse; giving before being asked; giving after being asked; giving less than is fitting but pleasantly; and, lowest, giving with sadness.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Tzedakah takes two hard questions in giving seriously at once: how much we owe, and how giving affects the person who receives it. Treating giving as justice makes it regular and proportionate instead of dependent on mood.[^s2][^s3] The ladder adds that how you give matters, ranking approaches by the recipient's dignity and the donor's anonymity.[^s3] The rabbis held that aid must never shame the recipient; one sage disguised a gift as a loan to spare a proud man's feelings.[^s2] Whether helping a colleague, relative or neighbour, helping well means attending to the other person's standing and independence, not only the size of the gift.[^s1][^s3]

## How to apply it

The steps above turn the tradition into habits: a set, proportionate amount; giving early, warmly and quietly; and, where it fits, help that makes the person self-supporting.[^s1][^s2] Maimonides also warns the other way: a person should accept hardship rather than become a burden on the community without need, which is one reason the tradition caps giving at a fifth (10:18).[^s1][^s2] People of other faiths and none can draw on the same structure, since many traditions treat giving as part of a good life, including Islamic *zakat* and the Buddhist, Hindu and Jain practice of *dāna*.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Reading the ladder as "anonymity beats everything".** Anonymity ranks high, but the lower rungs still count as tzedakah, and a bigger gift can matter more to the person in need than a perfect form.[^s1][^s4]
- **Using "self-sufficiency" as a reason to withhold urgent help.** The tradition fed anyone hungry through the soup kitchen; the top rung adds help that puts someone back on their feet; it doesn't replace meeting today's need.[^s1][^s2]
- **Deciding for the recipient.** Philosophers warn that paternalistic giving can insult a person's autonomy and entrench unequal power, even when they accept it.[^s3]
- **Treating it as optional.** In the tradition, tzedakah is a duty of justice; leaving it to whim or visibility misses the point.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

Tzedakah is a religious and ethical teaching judged by argument and long practice, not by experiment.[^s3] Two lines of research bear on the ladder. In six experiments, observers' judgments of how charitable a donor was largely followed Maimonides' ranking: double-blind gifts were seen as most charitable. No gift up to 100 times larger restored the standing of a donor who revealed only himself, but imagined beneficiaries preferred larger revealed gifts while observers favoured smaller anonymous ones.[^s4] That suggests the ladder captures how people judge a giver's motives more than what the recipient values most.[^s4] Research on helping relations finds that receiving help can threaten the recipient's self-esteem, and that people who see themselves as capable may reject help that solves a problem for them while accepting help that equips them to solve it.[^s5] In four studies, members of lower-status groups were least willing to accept such full-solution help from a higher-status group when the status gap seemed changeable, while their use of help that only gave hints was unaffected.[^s5] That fits Maimonides' preference for help that makes people self-supporting, though these studies test reactions to help, not the effect of the practice on the giver's character.[^s5]

## Connections

Tzedakah is **rooted in** Judaism, where the rabbis made it a commandment with detailed laws.[^s2] Its best-known form, the ladder of eight levels, was **introduced by** Maimonides in the *Mishneh Torah*, which also asks givers to give cheerfully and kindly, so the practice **develops** generosity as a settled disposition rather than a one-off act.[^s1] It is **supported by** the Maimonides' ladder experiments, in which people's judgments of charitability largely follow the ranking.[^s4] It is also **supported by** research on autonomy-oriented help, which favours help that leaves recipients able to stand on their own.[^s5] It sits close to generosity, compassion and the Golden Rule, and has parallels in Islamic *zakat* and in *dāna*.[^s3]
