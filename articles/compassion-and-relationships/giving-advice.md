# Giving advice

Giving advice is the everyday situation of telling someone else what they might do about a problem: a friend weighing a move, a sibling in a bad job, a colleague unsure whether to accept a promotion. It looks like the easy side of a hard decision, since the adviser bears none of the consequences. Yet the psychology of wisdom treats it as one of the clearest tests of wisdom, and research shows that both what advisers say and how they say it shape whether advice helps.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The link between wisdom and advice is old. Ordinary people, asked to describe a wise person, mention someone who listens well, weighs things and gives good advice, and Paul Baltes and Ursula Staudinger built that intuition into the Berlin wisdom paradigm, which defines wisdom as *a truly superior level of knowledge, judgment, and advice* about the conduct and meaning of life.[^s1] Its procedural-knowledge criterion explicitly includes heuristics for giving advice, and its tasks ask people to think aloud about what someone should consider or do, such as a 15-year-old who wants to marry at once.[^s1] An answer that dismisses the idea outright scores low; a high-scoring one asks what circumstances, culture and feelings might lie behind the wish.[^s1] Traditions also teach restraint in advising. In the *Analects*, Confucius tells a disciple to admonish a friend faithfully and guide him skilfully, then adds: *If you find him impracticable, stop* (12.23).[^s6]

## Why it matters

Advisers have a real advantage. In three experiments with 693 participants, people reasoned more wisely about another person's relationship conflict than about their own: they more often acknowledged the limits of their knowledge, considered other viewpoints, expected change and looked for compromise.[^s2] This gap, known as Solomon's paradox, is one reason a friend's view can help. Advising can also change the adviser. In a set of studies on people struggling with goals such as saving money or finding work, those who gave advice reported more motivation and confidence than those who received expert advice, and middle-school students randomly assigned to write advice to younger pupils later spent somewhat more time practising vocabulary than those who read teachers' advice.[^s3] Most people did not predict this, expecting receiving advice to help more.[^s3]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Check whether advice is wanted, and whether you are the right person.** Advice that is offered and accepted, or volunteered, was rated higher in quality and more likely to be used than advice pressed on someone who had declined it; asking permission mattered most on highly personal problems.[^s4] Researchers on advising also suggest declining or referring the person on when a question lies outside your experience.[^s5]
- **Listen and diagnose before prescribing.** Hear the person out, ask probing questions, and do not assume their problem is the one you once faced.[^s5]
- **Use the outsider's advantage, not the outsider's ignorance.** Your distance helps you reason wisely, but only about a situation you have actually understood.[^s2][^s5]
- **Show your reasoning and offer options.** Talk through the pros and cons of several paths and explain how you reached your view, so the person can judge it rather than simply follow it.[^s5]
- **Hold your view as one perspective.** The Berlin criteria reward recognising that values and priorities differ between people and that life is uncertain, which argues against advice framed as the one right answer.[^s1]
- **Know when to stop.** Once you have advised honestly, the decision belongs to the other person, as the *Analects* passage suggests.[^s6]

## Pitfalls

The commonest errors are answering a question you are not qualified to answer, misdiagnosing the problem, giving self-centred advice of the "here's what I'd do" kind that ignores the other person's constraints, and offering advice no one asked for.[^s5] Advice pressed on someone who has already declined it is rated lowest of all, and is the least likely to be used.[^s4] A quieter trap is enjoying the role of the one who knows: advising is usually easier than living with the choice, and the adviser's confidence is not evidence that the advice fits.[^s2][^s3] Where someone's problem involves a risk to their safety or persistent distress, the wise adviser encourages them to seek a qualified professional rather than standing in for one.

## Evidence and limits

The findings are encouraging but bounded. The Berlin paradigm's authors noted that the link between wisdom-related knowledge in their tasks and how people actually advise or live had not yet been established.[^s1] Their interactive-minds studies did find that discussing a problem with a trusted person improved wisdom-related performance, but only when people then had time to reflect alone.[^s1] The self–other asymmetry in wise reasoning was found in younger and older adults alike, so age by itself does not make someone a better adviser on their own problems.[^s2] The benefits of giving advice were measured for motivation rather than knowledge, in mostly online samples, and the field effect was small.[^s3] The permission findings come from two studies, one with students, and asking first made a clear difference over volunteering only for some kinds of problem.[^s4]

## Connections

Giving advice belongs under **social relationships** and **complements seeking counsel**, its mirror image, which covers how to choose advisers and weigh what they say.[^s5] Its role in wisdom is **studied in the psychology of wisdom**, where the **Berlin wisdom paradigm** uses advice tasks to measure wisdom and the **interactive-minds study** shows that dialogue helps.[^s1] The adviser's edge is **explained by Solomon's paradox** and **demonstrated by the Exploring Solomon's paradox study**, which also shows that **self-distancing** lets people give themselves the advice they would give a friend.[^s2] The **"Dear Abby" study** demonstrates that giving advice can motivate the giver,[^s3] and the **advice-solicitation study** shows why permission matters.[^s4] **Intellectual humility** and **perspective-taking** are the skills an adviser most needs, and the Confucian ideal of **friendship** in the *Analects* pairs honest counsel with the restraint to stop.[^s6]
