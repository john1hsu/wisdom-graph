# Generosity

Generosity is the virtue of giving good things to others freely and abundantly, whether money, time, attention or emotional availability; researchers use it as an umbrella for helping, sharing, cooperation and other prosocial acts.[^s6] As a virtue rather than a single act, it is a settled disposition: the generous person gives readily, but with judgment about whom to help, how much, and when.[^s1]

## Origin

Aristotle calls the virtue *eleutheriotēs*, usually translated "liberality", and defines it as the mean in the giving and taking of wealth, especially giving. The prodigal person gives too much and takes too little; the mean or stingy person gives too little and takes too much. The liberal person gives to the right people, the right amounts, and at the right time, and does so gladly or at least without distress.[^s1] He adds that liberality is judged relative to a person's means: it lies in the giver's character, not the size of the gift, so a small gift from modest resources can be more generous than a large one from great wealth.[^s1]

Buddhism gives the practice of giving (*dāna*) an even more basic place. Bhikkhu Bodhi notes that it is the first topic in the Buddha's graduated teaching, the first of the three bases of merit and the first of the ten perfections (*pāramī*); outward acts of giving cultivate an inward generosity (*cāga*) that weakens greed and hatred and makes the mind more pliable for insight.[^s2] A short discourse (AN 5.148) lists the gifts of a person of integrity: given with conviction, attentively, in season, with an empathetic heart, and without harming oneself or others.[^s3]

## Why it matters

For a wise life, generosity matters in two directions. It is a check on greed: both Aristotle and the Buddhist texts treat the habit of giving as the counterweight to grasping and hoarding.[^s1][^s2] It also widens attention beyond one's own interests, which is the ground of compassion and fair dealing with others.[^s2][^s6] Surveys across 136 countries link donating to charity with greater happiness, and studies in workplaces suggest that seeing the good one's work does for others can protect against exhaustion and support creativity.[^s6]

## What good looks like

The generous person gives because the good of the other is worth doing, not for a return or a reputation.[^s1] Aristotle's account and the Buddhist list converge on the same marks: the gift is fitted to the person and the moment, given attentively and with care, and does not wreck the giver's own life or harm third parties.[^s1][^s3] It is also not the same as giving everything away. Aristotle thought prodigality a lesser fault than meanness, and one that age and poverty often correct, but still a failure of judgment, because unchecked giving runs out and often ends up drawing on the wrong sources.[^s1]

## How to practise it

- Give on purpose and freely. Research summarised by the Greater Good Science Center finds that the emotional benefit of giving is larger when the giver chooses to give, can see the specific impact of the gift, and gives in a way that connects them with the person helped.[^s6]
- Match the gift to the person and your means: ask what would actually help, not what is easiest to hand over.[^s1][^s3]
- Start small and repeat. Aristotle's virtues are formed by practice, and the Buddhist teaching treats each act of giving as training the disposition behind it.[^s1][^s2]
- Give more than money: time, attention, teaching and a fair hearing are often what is most needed.[^s6]
- Thank others for their giving; being thanked makes people more likely to help again.[^s6]

## Evidence and limits

A 2018 meta-analysis of 27 randomised experiments (about 4,000 participants) found that performing acts of kindness raised the actor's well-being by a small-to-medium amount, comparable to other positive-psychology exercises; the authors summed it up as real but relatively modest.[^s4] They also noted that most studies were underpowered, used Western student samples and measured effects only in the short term.[^s4]

The best-known single finding, that spending money on others makes people happier than spending on themselves, has a mixed replication record. A close 2022 replication of the original 46-person experiment found no difference on the original measure, though a simpler happiness item showed a small effect in the predicted direction; the replication authors also describe a larger registered replication by the original team as somewhat mixed but largely supportive.[^s5] The Greater Good review adds that much of the evidence linking volunteering with health and longer life is correlational and may partly reflect healthier people volunteering more.[^s6] The fair reading is that giving tends to do the giver some good, but not reliably or dramatically, and giving chiefly for that payoff misses the point of the virtue.[^s4][^s1]

## Connections

Generosity belongs among the moral virtues and is a clear case of the doctrine of the mean, with prodigality as its excess and meanness as its deficiency; it is **rooted in** Aristotelian virtue ethics and **requires** practical wisdom to find the right amount for the person and the moment.[^s1] It is equally **rooted in** Buddhism, where giving is **part of** the ten perfections and **addresses** greed by loosening the habit of grasping.[^s2] It is **supported by** the acts-of-kindness meta-analysis, which finds a modest benefit to the giver, and **challenged by** the close replication of the prosocial-spending experiment, which questions how large and reliable the popular "giving makes you happy" claim is.[^s4][^s5] It **complements** gratitude, since being thanked makes people more likely to give again, and sits close to compassion, empathy, contentment, simplicity and the four immeasurables.[^s6][^s2]
