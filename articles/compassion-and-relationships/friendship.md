# Friendship

Friendship is a mutual relationship of goodwill that both people recognise, in which each wishes the other well.[^s1] Aristotle opened his discussion by calling it a virtue, or something that implies virtue, and the thing most necessary for living: nobody, he said, would choose a life without friends even if they had every other good.[^s1] As a setting for wisdom, friendship is where advice is most often given and received, and where the pull to please rather than to help is strongest.[^s3]

## Origin

The classical accounts still frame the subject. In *Nicomachean Ethics* VIII.2–3, Aristotle distinguishes three things people love — the good, the pleasant and the useful — and so three kinds of friendship.[^s1] Friendships of pleasure and of use love the other person only for what they bring, and dissolve when that stops; the complete kind exists between good people who wish each other well for each other's sake, and lasts because goodness lasts.[^s1] Such friendship is rare and needs time and familiarity: in his words, "a wish for friendship may arise quickly, but friendship does not."[^s1]

Cicero's dialogue *Laelius on Friendship* defines it as agreement in all things human and divine, joined with goodwill and affection, and calls it, apart from wisdom, the best gift the gods have given.[^s2] He holds that friendship can exist only among good people, using "good" in its ordinary sense rather than the Stoic ideal of the sage, and that virtue both creates and preserves it (§18–20).[^s2] Plutarch's essay *How to Know a Flatterer from a Friend* adds the practical problem: the flatterer imitates the friend so closely that we need tests to tell them apart.[^s3]
## Why it matters

Aristotle lists what friends do: they are a refuge in poverty and misfortune, keep the young from error, help the old and spur people in their prime to noble action, so that with friends people think and act better.[^s1] Friends are also our best source of honest outside views. Plutarch argues that self-love is an inner flatterer that welcomes any outside voice confirming our good opinion of ourselves, which works against the Delphic command to know oneself; a real friend's frank speech is the corrective (§1, §25).[^s3]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Speak frankly, but well.** Plutarch's guidance on *parrhesia*, frank speech, is detailed: keep it free of insolence and mockery; reserve sharp rebuke for important matters; deliver it privately rather than as a public show; mix it with deserved praise; and end gently rather than leaving the friend in pain (§27–37).[^s3]
- **Don't lecture someone who is suffering.** People in adversity need comfort first, not correction (§28).[^s3]
- **Watch for the mirror.** A friend's character is consistent; the flatterer changes with each company like a chameleon or a mirror, agrees with whatever you say, and promises anything, where a friend helps only with what is honest (§7–9, §22–23).[^s3] One test Plutarch suggests is to change your own stated opinion and see whether he follows.[^s3]
- **Invest unhurried time.** In Jeffrey Hall's studies of 355 adults who had recently moved and 112 first-year students, people estimated roughly 50 hours together to become casual friends, about 90 to become friends and more than 200 to become close friends.[^s6] Hanging out, joking, catching up and serious talk built closeness; time spent working together or making small talk did much less.[^s5][^s6]

## Pitfalls

The commonest is flattery dressed as support — praising a plan you doubt to keep the peace.[^s3] Its mirror image is "honesty" used as a weapon: blunt criticism in front of others, at a bad moment, or about trivial faults.[^s3] A third is mistaking a wide network for friendship: Aristotle held that one cannot be a friend in the complete sense to many people, because it takes experience and familiarity that are hard to gain with many (VIII.6).[^s1] Finally, people who are lonely or isolated for long periods should treat that as a reason to seek support from others or from a professional, not as something to reason through alone.

## Evidence and limits

The popular idea that humans can keep only about 150 stable relationships ("Dunbar's number") rests on extrapolating from primate brain data. A 2021 reanalysis with updated data and phylogenetic methods produced estimates whose 95% intervals ran from about 2 to 520, and concluded that specifying any one number is futile.[^s4] Hall's hour figures come from self-reported surveys and are rough estimates; students seemed to become friends faster than adults, perhaps because of campus life or because they overrate new ties.[^s5] Claims about what friendship is and what friends owe each other are philosophical and judged by argument.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Friendship is a narrower case of **social relationships**, and Aristotle's discussion makes it a topic **studied in** **virtue ethics**, with **character friendship** as its complete form.[^s1] It **contrasts with** **flattery**, which Plutarch treats as friendship's counterfeit, and it **complements** **honesty**, since frank speech is the friend's distinctive service (§7–9, §27).[^s3] Hall's research on the **hours it takes to make a friend** **demonstrates** that closeness grows with time and the right kind of shared activity.[^s5][^s6] The **Dunbar's number deconstructed** study **demonstrates** that no fixed cognitive limit on the number of relationships can be derived from primate data.[^s4] Friendship is also the usual setting for **giving advice** and **seeking counsel**, and Plutarch ties it to **know thyself** and the danger of **self-deception**.[^s3]
