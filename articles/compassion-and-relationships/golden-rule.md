# Golden Rule

The Golden Rule is the moral maxim to treat others as you would want to be treated. It comes in two shapes: a positive one, as in the Gospel of Matthew (7:12), which asks you to do for others what you would want done for you, and a negative one, sometimes called the "silver rule", which asks you not to do to others what you would not want done to yourself.[^s1] Versions appear in many traditions, which is part of why the rule is so often offered as a shared core of ethics; it is also why it has attracted careful criticism. Read wisely, it is less a formula than a habit: pause, imagine the exchange reversed, and let that shape what you do.

## Origin

The negative form is old and widespread. Britannica notes it in the Analects of Confucius, in the Jewish teacher Hillel (1st century BCE) and Philo, in the deuterocanonical Book of Tobit (4:15), and in early Christian texts such as the Didache; Greek and Roman writers including Isocrates and Seneca expressed related ideas.[^s1] In the Analects, a disciple asks for one saying that could guide a whole life, and Confucius answers with "reciprocity" (*shu*): do not do to others what you do not desire (15.24).[^s2] Elsewhere Confucius calls reciprocity, with loyalty, the single thread running through his way (4.15), tells a disciple who claims to live by it that he has not yet reached that level (5.12), and describes the humane person as one who helps others to the standing he wants for himself, proceeding by analogy from what is near (6.30).[^s2] The positive Christian form is Matthew 7:12, from the Sermon on the Mount, which Britannica treats as the classic statement of the Christian duty to one's neighbour.[^s1] The often-quoted claim that every religion states the rule "in the same words" is a simplification: wordings, scope and the reasons given for the rule differ between traditions.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

The rule turns a vague wish to be good into a quick, portable test that anyone can run in the middle of a decision: reverse the roles and see whether you could accept what you are about to do.[^s2] It blocks the most common route to unfairness, making an exception for yourself, and it trains the imaginative move from your own case to someone else's that Confucius treated as the method of humaneness (*ren*).[^s2][^s3]

## How to apply it

Use it at the moment of choice rather than as a slogan. First, describe what you are about to do in plain terms. Second, imagine yourself on the receiving end, with the other person's situation and stakes rather than your own.[^s2] Third, separate the specific treatment from the underlying good you would want: being told the truth, being heard, being thanked, being spared needless harm. Finally, find out how this person wants that good delivered. Research on "perspective mistaking" found that imagining yourself in another's shoes did not reliably make people more accurate about others' thoughts and preferences, whereas getting their perspective directly, by asking and listening, did.[^s6] The rule supplies the motive and the fairness check; conversation supplies the facts.

## Common misreadings

- **Projecting your tastes.** Taken literally, the positive form tells you to give others what *you* like. Philosophers have long noted that because the rule runs on the agent's own preferences, people with very different preferences (a hedonist, a misanthrope) would draw different conclusions from it.[^s5] Apply it at the level of respect and care, not personal taste.
- **Treating it as the whole of ethics.** Kant called the negative form "trivial" as a supreme principle: it says nothing about duties to oneself or duties of kindness, many people would happily waive others' help, and a convicted criminal could use it to argue against the judge who sentences him (Groundwork, Ak 4:430, footnote).[^s4] Kleingeld adds that the negative rule yields only duties of omission and results that vary from person to person, unlike Kant's categorical imperative.[^s5]
- **Ignoring roles and power.** Lee argues that the rule presupposes equality between agent and recipient, which sits awkwardly with relationships such as ruler and subject or manager and team; he suggests applying it by seeing the other as an equal human being even within an unequal role.[^s3]
- **Using it as a bargain.** "I'll be nice so they're nice to me" turns a principle of fairness into a trade; the traditions present it as how to treat others, not a deal for return.[^s2][^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The Golden Rule is a normative teaching, judged by argument and long practice rather than by experiment, so there is no trial showing that following it "works".[^s1][^s5] What can be tested is the mental move it often relies on. Across 25 experiments, Eyal, Steffel and Epley found that perspective-taking reduced egocentric bias but did not improve accuracy about what others thought or wanted, while directly asking did.[^s6] That challenges the self-projecting reading of the rule, not its spirit: wanting to treat others as you would wish to be treated is compatible with checking what they wish.

## Connections

- The rule is taught in Confucianism as *shu*, the reciprocity Confucius links to *ren*, humaneness (Analects 6.30, 15.24).[^s2][^s3]
- It is rooted in Christianity (Matthew 7:12) and in Judaism, where Britannica cites Hillel's negative form.[^s1]
- In leading others, Lee's analysis of asymmetrical relationships shows how it must be adapted when one person holds power over another.[^s3]
- It contrasts with Kant's categorical imperative, which judges maxims rather than actions and is meant to give the same answer for everyone.[^s5]
- Kant's *Groundwork* challenges it as a supreme moral principle, and the perspective-mistaking study challenges the idea that imagining yourself in someone's place is enough to know what they want.[^s4][^s6]
- It is related to perspective-taking, empathy and compassion, which supply the understanding and care the rule asks for.[^s6][^s2]
