# Interpersonal conflict

Interpersonal conflict is a clash between people who have to keep living or working alongside each other: partners arguing about money, siblings divided over a parent's care, friends hurt by a broken promise, colleagues at odds over credit or workload. Such conflicts are ordinary, but they are also where wisdom is most often tested, because they combine strong feeling, incomplete information about the other person's motives, and a relationship that outlasts the argument.[^s1] Psychologists who study wise reasoning have used this situation as their main laboratory, asking people to think aloud about conflicts and scoring how they reason.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The research tradition grew from work by Igor Grossmann and colleagues, who gave adults short accounts of conflicts and asked what would happen next and why. In a study of 225 Americans and 186 Japanese aged 25 to 75, the interpersonal stories were letters to an advice columnist about disputes between siblings, friends and spouses, set beside newspaper reports of conflicts between groups.[^s1] Trained coders, blind to participants' ages, rated each answer on six features drawn from philosophical accounts of wisdom: taking others' perspectives, recognising that things can change, seeing several ways the situation could unfold, acknowledging the limits of one's own knowledge, looking for compromise, and expecting the conflict to be resolved.[^s1]

## Why it matters

How people reason about conflict appears to shift with experience and culture. Among Americans, older adults reasoned more wisely about these conflicts than younger ones; among Japanese, age made little difference, and younger and middle-aged Japanese already scored higher than Americans of the same age.[^s1] The authors suggested that people who meet more varied conflicts keep learning from them across adulthood, while those raised to prize harmony and avoid open conflict may learn these habits earlier.[^s1][^s6] Wise reasoning is also a matter of state as much as trait. In a diary study, people's reasoning about daily challenges varied considerably from day to day, and was wiser in social situations than in non-social ones; on days when it was wiser, people also reported more reappraisal and forgiveness, more positive emotion and less emotional reactivity.[^s3] Conflict, in other words, is a situation where wisdom can be practised, not just a test of whether someone has it.

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Hold your account of the other person's motives loosely.** Admitting you may not know why they acted as they did is one of the scored marks of wise reasoning.[^s1]
- **Borrow a neutral observer's eyes.** In a two-year study of 120 married couples, spouses who spent seven minutes three times a year writing about their latest disagreement from the viewpoint of a neutral third party who wanted the best for both did not show the decline in marital quality seen in the control group, and reported less distress about conflict.[^s2]
- **Look a year ahead.** People asked to imagine how they would feel about a recent conflict with a partner or friend a year from now showed more forgiveness, a more positive reinterpretation of the conflict and more positivity about the relationship than those who reflected on how they felt in the present.[^s5]
- **Expect change and several outcomes.** Treating a falling-out as one chapter rather than the final word, and imagining more than one way it could go, are part of what researchers score.[^s1]
- **Seek an arrangement, not a verdict.** Looking for compromise or a way to meet both sides' concerns is the step most directly aimed at resolving the conflict rather than winning it.[^s1]

## Pitfalls

The most common error is the confident, uncharitable story: deciding at once that the other person is selfish or malicious and reasoning from there. A second is mistaking avoidance for wisdom; cultures differ in whether they resolve conflicts directly or through mediation and avoidance, and neither style is wise by default.[^s1][^s6] A third is expecting a quick technique to transform a relationship. The marital reappraisal exercise preserved relationship quality rather than raising it, its benefits were measured over one year, and its authors did not know which ingredient mattered most.[^s2] None of this applies to relationships involving abuse, coercion or threats to safety, where reframing the other person's behaviour is not the goal; there, and whenever a conflict brings persistent distress, help from a counsellor or another qualified professional is the right step.

## Evidence and limits

The findings are promising but limited. The age and culture study compared two samples from two places at one point in time, so it shows differences between age groups rather than change within people.[^s1] The marital study was a randomised trial of a non-clinical sample, relied on self-reports, and no large independent replication had been found when this article was written.[^s2] The future-perspective studies were short-term laboratory work.[^s5] Wise reasoning about conflicts between groups follows a similar pattern: across seven studies, people who reasoned more wisely about an ongoing conflict involving their own group were more positive towards the other group and less polarised, and a brief wise-reasoning exercise shifted those outcomes in experiments.[^s4]

## Connections

Interpersonal conflict sits under **social relationships** and **contrasts with intergroup conflict**, the other kind of dispute used in the same studies, where the cultural pattern differed.[^s1] Its role as a testing ground for wisdom is **demonstrated by** the **age-and-culture study**,[^s1] the **marital conflict-reappraisal study**[^s2] and the **prospective-reasoning study** of close relationships,[^s5] and it is **studied in the psychology of wisdom**.[^s3] Many skills and practices in this graph are applied here: **wise reasoning** names the overall pattern, **perspective-taking**, **intellectual humility** and **self-distancing** supply its parts, **Solomon's paradox** explains why people reason better about others' conflicts than their own, and **forgiveness**, **compassionate reappraisal** and Buddhist **right speech** shape what happens after the argument. The **Berlin wisdom paradigm** also uses difficult life problems of this kind to measure wisdom.
