# Empathy

Empathy is the capacity to sense what another person is feeling and to grasp how things look from where they stand.[^s7] Researchers usually separate two strands: **affective empathy**, feeling something that mirrors or responds to another's emotion, and **cognitive empathy**, identifying and understanding that emotion, sometimes called perspective-taking.[^s7] Neuroscientists Tania Singer and Olga Klimecki add a crucial condition: in empathy you resonate with someone's feeling while still knowing it is theirs. Without that self-other distinction, what you have is emotional contagion, a more primitive response already present in babies.[^s5]

## Origin

The English word is young. Edward Titchener coined "empathy" in 1909 to translate the German *Einfühlung*, "feeling into", which Theodor Lipps had turned from a term of art criticism into a key idea about how we understand other minds; Lipps thought of it as a kind of inner imitation.[^s1] Before that, English writers used "sympathy" for much the same territory. David Hume pictured human minds as mirrors to one another, and both Hume and Adam Smith made fellow feeling the foundation of social life and moral judgement.[^s1] Smith's version was more imaginative and more corrective: we judge by asking how we would feel in another's place, and moral judgement proper comes from the standpoint of an impartial spectator.[^s1]

## Why it matters

People higher in empathy are more likely to help others, even at a cost to themselves, and understanding a partner's feelings goes with closer, more satisfying relationships.[^s7] At work, more empathic managers have healthier, happier staff, and empathic doctors' patients fare better.[^s7] For wisdom, empathy supplies raw information that judgement about people needs: you cannot weigh others' interests if you do not know what they feel or why.[^s1][^s7]

But empathy is not the same as caring, and it is not automatically a good guide. Philosophers distinguish it from **sympathy** or **empathic concern**, a feeling for another's suffering that aims at their well-being and need not match their emotion.[^s1] The link from one to the other is contingent: overwhelming empathic distress can tip into **personal distress**, a self-focused reaction that makes people want to escape rather than help.[^s1] Empathy is also selective. It flows more readily toward the attractive, the nearby, the in-group and the single identifiable victim, which is why the psychologist Paul Bloom has argued that it is a poor guide to moral and policy decisions and that reasoned compassion is better.[^s1][^s6]

## What good looks like

Wise empathy keeps the understanding and steers the feeling. Experiments by Olga Klimecki, Tania Singer and colleagues trained volunteers first in empathic resonance with others' suffering and then in compassion. Empathy training raised negative feelings and activity in brain regions linked to sharing pain; subsequent compassion training reversed that rise, increased positive feelings and engaged a different network associated with reward and affiliation.[^s4] The authors suggest that turning empathic resonance into compassion, warm concern for the other, may be a way to cope with empathic distress and stay resilient.[^s4]

Good empathy is also deliberate rather than merely reflexive. Jamil Zaki's review argues that, although empathy often feels automatic, people constantly regulate it according to their motives: they avoid it when it threatens pain, cost or competition, and seek it when it brings connection or social approval.[^s2] They do so by choosing which situations to enter, where to direct attention, and how to interpret another's feelings, for instance by blaming a sufferer.[^s2] That makes empathy something you can choose to extend, including toward people you would rather not understand.[^s2]

## How to practise it

- **Attend before you judge.** Choose to stay in the conversation and look at the person rather than at your phone or your rebuttal; avoidance often starts with where attention goes.[^s2]
- **Name their feeling and check it.** Say back what you think they feel and why, and let them correct you; accurate identification is what the most effective trainings measure.[^s3][^s7]
- **Keep the self-other line.** Notice your own reaction and remind yourself whose feeling it is, so resonance does not become contagion or distress.[^s5][^s1]
- **Turn feeling into concern.** Shift from feeling their pain to wishing them well and asking what would help.[^s4]
- **Widen the circle.** Ask who else is affected but less visible, and correct for the pull toward the vivid and familiar.[^s1][^s6]

These are everyday skills, not therapy. Lasting empathic distress, the drain Klimecki and colleagues describe, is a reason to seek professional support.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

Empathy can be trained. A meta-analysis of 18 randomised controlled trials with 1,018 participants found a medium effect of empathy training (g = 0.63), about 0.51 after adjusting for likely publication bias.[^s3] Effects were larger for health professionals and university students and when outcomes were measured objectively; the number of training hours did not predict the effect, and follow-ups ran to only six months.[^s3] The neuroscience of empathy versus compassion training comes from small samples and laboratory measures.[^s4]

The case *against* empathy is itself disputed. Daniel Västfjäll, Paul Slovic and colleagues agree that empathy is biased and needs deliberate correction, but argue that compassion has biases too, that some effects Bloom attributes to empathy reflect falling compassion, and that emotion and reasoning are too intertwined to separate.[^s6] Hence the status here is **mixed**: real, trainable and valuable, but partial, and wise only when joined to judgement.[^s1][^s3][^s6]

## Connections

Empathy **contrasts with compassion**, which training studies show is a distinct, warmer and less draining response,[^s4] and when unregulated it **causes empathic distress**.[^s1] It is often **confused with emotional contagion**, which lacks the self-other distinction.[^s5] It **complements perspective-taking**, its cognitive side,[^s7] and was **introduced** into English **by Edward Titchener**.[^s1] Its trainability is **supported by** the Teding van Berkhout and Malouff **meta-analysis of empathy training**,[^s3] while Bloom's **"Empathy and its discontents"** **challenges** its role as a moral guide.[^s6] It is **applied in leading others**,[^s7] **studied in social neuroscience**,[^s5] and sits under **social cognition**. It is related to **emotion regulation**, which keeps resonance from becoming distress, and to **compassionate reappraisal** and **forgiveness**.[^s2][^s4]
