steps:
  - Sit somewhere quiet and settle your breathing for a minute or two before you start.
  - Recall one specific time a particular person hurt or wronged you, and name plainly what they did and what it cost you.
  - Picture the offender as a whole person with a history, fears and other sides to them, not only as the author of this act.
  - Treat the wrong as a sign that this person needs to grow or change, without telling yourself it was acceptable.
  - Try, for about two minutes, to sincerely wish them something good, such as healing or becoming someone who would not do this again.
  - Notice your thoughts, feelings and body as you do this, and repeat the exercise when you catch yourself replaying the hurt.
  - Decide separately, on the facts, what boundary, conversation or accountability the situation still needs.

# Compassionate reappraisal

Compassionate reappraisal is a way of rethinking an offence while you remember it. Instead of replaying the hurt, you deliberately see the person who wronged you as a fellow human being rather than as the offence itself, acknowledge the wrong and its impact without minimising it, read the wrong as a sign that they need to change, and try to genuinely wish them well, even if the relationship cannot be restored.[^s4] It is a form of cognitive reappraisal, changing how you interpret a situation in order to change how you feel about it, aimed specifically at an offender.[^s1]

## Origin

The exercise comes from a series of laboratory studies led by the psychologist Charlotte vanOyen Witvliet and colleagues at Hope College. In the first, published in 2010, 71 people recalled a real offence and in turn ruminated on it, reappraised it with compassion for the offender, and reappraised it by looking for benefits such as lessons learned or strengths they had shown.[^s1] Later experiments compared compassion with suppressing one's feelings and with benefit-finding, and in 2022 the group described five earlier experiments in which compassionate reappraisal outperformed rumination.[^s2][^s3][^s4] The Greater Good Science Center at UC Berkeley turned the laboratory instructions into a five-minute public practice.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Replaying a wrong keeps it alive. In these studies, rumination brought more anger, sadness and arousal, while compassionate reappraisal brought more empathy, forgiveness, positive emotion and sense of control.[^s4] It is one of the few concrete exercises that lets you work toward forgiveness without first pretending the wrong did not happen: the instructions require you to keep the injustice in view.[^s4][^s5] That combination, a clear judgement about what was done joined to goodwill toward the person who did it, is close to what philosophers and psychologists mean by wise forgiveness.

## How to apply it

The public version takes about five minutes: settle your breathing, recall a specific hurt, then spend two minutes seeing the offender as a flawed person who behaved badly and trying to wish them something positive or healing, while noticing your thoughts, feelings and body.[^s5] In the research version, people also wrote about the offender's humanity, the wrong as a sign of their need for growth, one small way to wish them well, and how compassion can be genuine even if the relationship ends.[^s4] The practice is meant to be repeated whenever you catch yourself replaying the offence.[^s5]

Keep the inner exercise separate from the outer decision. Compassion here is compatible with holding someone accountable: the 2022 study found that, the morning after compassionate reappraisal, people were more willing to be accountable to others themselves.[^s4] What you say, report or change about the situation is still decided on its merits.

## Common mistakes

- **Excusing instead of reappraising.** The point is not that the act was acceptable but that the person needs to change; minimising the harm is a different strategy.[^s4]
- **Confusing it with silver-lining thinking.** Benefit-focused reappraisal, asking what you gained or learned, also eased negative emotion, but compassion-focused reappraisal produced the largest gains in forgiveness and empathy.[^s1][^s3]
- **Treating it as suppression.** Pushing feelings down reduced negative emotion in one experiment too, but only compassionate reappraisal raised empathy and forgiveness and went with calmer heart responses.[^s2]
- **Using it to tolerate a harm you could stop.** Research on reappraisal in general suggests it helps most with stressors you cannot control, and may backfire when a situation is controllable and calls for action.[^s6] With ongoing mistreatment, safety and change come first.
- **Forcing it.** The Greater Good page suggests other forgiveness practices for people this one does not suit, and commenters there worried it could fit poorly with severe harms.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The findings are consistent across several experiments: compared with rumination, compassionate reappraisal raised empathy and forgiveness, lowered anger and arousal, and in the 2010 study slowed heart rate and eased facial tension linked to negative emotion.[^s1][^s4] Compared with suppression and with benefit-finding, it was the strategy that most increased forgiveness and empathy.[^s2][^s3] In the 2022 study, people fell asleep somewhat faster and reported less intrusive replaying the next morning.[^s4]

The status here is *mixed* because the evidence is narrow. Most studies come from one research group, use small or student samples that are largely white and Western, measure short-term effects, and rely heavily on self-report; the 2022 study gave every participant rumination first and had no neutral control night.[^s4][^s5] The benefits of reappraisal also depend on context.[^s6] This is a reflective exercise, not therapy: if an injury or betrayal leaves you persistently distressed, or you are not safe, seek professional or crisis support.

## Connections

Compassionate reappraisal **develops forgiveness**, the virtue it was designed to foster,[^s3] and **compassion** toward an offender.[^s1] It **addresses rumination**, replacing the replay of a grievance.[^s4] It is an **instance of cognitive reappraisal**,[^s1] **contrasts with emotional suppression**,[^s2] and is an **alternative to benefit-finding**, the other positive reappraisal these studies tested.[^s3] It is **applied in interpersonal conflict**,[^s5] and was **introduced by Charlotte vanOyen Witvliet** and her colleagues.[^s4] Related structured routes to forgiveness include the REACH model, and the exercise draws on **perspective-taking**.[^s4][^s5]
