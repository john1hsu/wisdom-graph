# Social relationships

Social relationships are the lasting ties that make up most of a life: partners and family, friends, colleagues, neighbours and community. Philosophers treat the closest of them, friendship above all, as a personal relationship in which each person cares about the other for that person's own sake, with some degree of intimacy and shared activity.[^s3] As a context for wisdom, relationships matter because they combine almost everything that makes problems hard: other people's hidden reasons, competing loyalties, strong feelings and situations that keep changing.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Reflection on relationships is as old as the wisdom traditions. Aristotle, in Book VIII of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, distinguished friendships based on pleasure, on usefulness and on admiration for the other's character, and treated only the last as complete; he described the good friend as "another self" and a kind of mirror through which we come to know ourselves.[^s3] Confucian ethics placed particular relationships at the centre of moral life. *Ren*, humaneness, is explained in *Analects* 12.22 as caring for others, ritual propriety gives that care a shape, and filial devotion is among the tradition's most distinctive themes.[^s4] Confucians defended care with distinctions, more for kin than for strangers but extended outward, and the Mohists criticised it in favour of impartial concern for all, a debate about partiality that still runs through modern ethics.[^s4]

Modern psychology has added two strands: large studies of how relationships affect health and happiness, and laboratory studies of how well people reason about their own relationships.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

The quality of a life depends heavily on the quality of its relationships. A meta-analysis of 148 studies with 308,849 participants, followed for 7.5 years on average, found that people with stronger social relationships had about 50 percent greater odds of survival; the authors judged the effect comparable to well-established risks such as smoking and alcohol use.[^s1] The association was strongest for rich, multidimensional measures of social integration and weakest for the simple fact of living with someone, which suggests that the substance of a relationship matters more than its outward form.[^s1]

The Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has followed men recruited in 1938 and later their families, points the same way. Its directors report that satisfaction with relationships at 50 predicted health at 80 better than cholesterol did, and that close ties predicted long, happy lives better than social class, IQ or genes.[^s5] These are correlations from a long-running but largely male, American sample, and the study's director has said it will probably never be replicated.[^s5]

## What wisdom looks like here

Wisdom in relationships draws on the same capacities that researchers measure as wise reasoning: recognising the limits of what you know, considering the other person's perspective, looking for a compromise and allowing that things may change.[^s2] In practice this tends to mean:

- **Asking before concluding.** Treat your account of why someone acted as a hypothesis, and check it with them.[^s2]
- **Caring for the person, not just the benefits.** Aristotle's distinction is a useful test: ties that last through difficulty are those valued for the other person, not only for pleasure or usefulness.[^s3]
- **Honouring particular duties without forgetting others.** Both Confucian and Western thinkers argue that we owe special care to those close to us, and both face the question of how far that partiality should go.[^s4][^s3]
- **Investing over time.** The long-run studies suggest that being able to count on one another in hard times matters more than avoiding every quarrel.[^s5][^s1]

## Pitfalls

The central pitfall is Solomon's paradox. In three experiments with 693 participants, people reasoned more wisely about a partner's infidelity or a friend's betrayal when it happened to someone else than when it happened to them, and older adults were no wiser than younger ones about their own dilemmas.[^s2] Thinking about the problem from a distance, using your own name and the third person, reduced the gap.[^s2] Other pitfalls are treating friends as instruments, which Aristotle saw as the mark of an incomplete friendship, and confusing the number of contacts with the strength of ties.[^s3][^s1]

Some relationships are harmful rather than merely difficult, and most of the health studies did not measure the quality of relationships or count negative ones, which the meta-analysis authors note carry their own risk.[^s1] Persistent loneliness, conflict at home or a relationship that feels unsafe is a reason to seek professional or crisis support, not only to reflect.

## Evidence and limits

The health findings are observational: people cannot be randomly assigned to have friends, so causation runs in uncertain directions, and most studies come from North America and Europe.[^s1] The Solomon's paradox studies used hypothetical scenarios of betrayal and infidelity, and the authors call for tests in real, everyday conflicts.[^s2] Philosophical claims about what friendship is and what we owe to those close to us are judged by argument rather than experiment.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Social relationships are the broader setting for **interpersonal conflict**, **giving advice** and **mentorship**, and an arena of **practical wisdom**.[^s2][^s3] The context is **demonstrated by** Holt-Lunstad and colleagues' **meta-analysis of social relationships and mortality**, which ties the strength of relationships to survival.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** Grossmann and Kross's **exploring Solomon's paradox study**, which shows wise reasoning dropping when the relationship is one's own and links the problem to **self-distancing** and **perspective-taking**; that work is **studied in** the **psychology of wisdom**.[^s2] The **Harvard Study of Adult Development** **demonstrates** the long-run link between close ties, health and happiness.[^s5] Aristotle's account of **friendship** and **character friendship**, and **Confucianism**'s ethic of humaneness and graded care, are the main traditional sources on the subject, alongside teachings such as **ubuntu** and the **Golden Rule**.[^s3][^s4]
