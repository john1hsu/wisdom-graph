steps:
  - Before you respond, name your own first reading of the situation so you can set it aside for a moment.
  - Imagine the other person's day, constraints and goals, and ask what they know that you don't and what they want from this.
  - Write down two or three guesses about how they see it, and mark each one as a guess.
  - Check the guesses by asking open questions ("How does this look from where you sit?") and listen without rebutting.
  - Revise your reading with what you heard, then decide what to do, looking for an option that serves both of you.
example:
  situation: A team lead notices that a usually reliable colleague has missed two deadlines in a row and seems curt in meetings.
  before: "I decide they've stopped caring, picture how I'd feel if I were slacking, and send a sharp message about accountability."
  after: "I sketch what their fortnight probably looked like, note that I'm guessing, then ask privately how the project is going from their side. It turns out a second project landed on them without my knowing; we reprioritise together."
  why: Imagining their side softened my reaction, but only asking told me what was actually going on; taking and getting perspective together led to a fair and workable fix.

# Perspective-taking

Perspective-taking is the deliberate attempt to see a situation from another person's position: to imagine what they know, what they want and how events look and feel from where they stand.[^s3] Psychologists treat it as the cognitive side of social understanding, sometimes called cognitive empathy, as distinct from the emotional resonance of empathy itself.[^s3][^s5] As a practice it is simple to describe and surprisingly easy to get wrong, because imagining someone's view is not the same as knowing it.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea has a long philosophical history. In *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), Adam Smith argued that we grasp others' feelings by imagining how we would feel in their circumstances, a projective account he set against David Hume's picture of feelings spreading by contagion.[^s8] The capacity develops early: researchers find that children grasp that other people have different thoughts by about age three or four, and refine their understanding of others' emotions through school and adolescence.[^s5] Social psychologists later turned it into an experimental instruction, typically asking participants to imagine how another person feels or to picture a day in their life, and compared the results with control groups or with instructions to stay objective.[^s2][^s1] That literature spans helping, negotiation, stereotyping and close relationships.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

Wisdom researchers count the ability to consider other viewpoints and to integrate them as a core feature of wise reasoning, alongside intellectual humility and an awareness that situations change.[^s4] Igor Grossmann and colleagues suggest perspective-taking may have evolved to solve problems of social coordination, which could help explain why people reason more wisely about others' dilemmas than about their own.[^s4] In one study of long-term couples, partners who pictured a conflict through an outsider's eyes were more likely afterwards to recognise their partner's view and look for compromise.[^s7]

At work it pays in a concrete way. Across three negotiation studies, people who tried to understand what their counterpart was thinking reached more deals and found more joint gains than controls, while people told to feel for the counterpart did no better on the deal, though their counterparts were more satisfied.[^s3] The authors suggest empathy may matter more where the relationship itself is at stake, as in disputes and mediation.[^s3]

## How to apply it

The steps above combine imagining with checking. A Greater Good in Education exercise makes the point vividly: partners predict how each other would act in a scenario, then compare notes and see how often they were wrong, before discussing the value of asking people directly.[^s5] The same exercise can be adapted to help two people who have had a real disagreement.[^s5] In everyday use, imagine their side to slow your reaction and widen your options, then ask, because the researchers' strongest advice is to act more like a journalist than a guesser.[^s6][^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Mistaking a guess for knowledge.** In 25 experiments, instructions to take another's perspective did not make people better at reading emotions, spotting lies or predicting a partner's opinions; on average accuracy was slightly worse.[^s1]
- **Projecting yourself.** Imagining how *you* would feel in their place can lead to stereotyping or to your own preferences in disguise.[^s6]
- **Skipping the question.** In the final experiment, spouses who briefly asked their partner about the issues predicted the partner's opinions more accurately, while those who imagined the partner's day did not.[^s1]
- **Overconfidence.** People greatly overestimated how many of their partner's views they had predicted correctly, whatever strategy they used.[^s1]
- **Feeling as a substitute for problem-solving.** Strong empathic feeling for a counterpart can cost you value in a negotiation without helping either side find a better deal.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is mixed. Perspective-taking can reduce egocentric bias and is linked in the literature to more empathy, a sense of connection and cooperation, which is why its authors still see value in it for building social bonds.[^s1][^s6] But it does not reliably make understanding more accurate.[^s1] A meta-analysis of experiments on empathic concern found that instructions to imagine another's feelings did not significantly raise concern compared with no instruction, whereas instructions to stay objective lowered it, which suggests people often empathise by default and the instruction's main effect is to avoid switching that off.[^s2] Many of these studies use brief laboratory tasks, and how they translate to long relationships and high-stakes conflicts is less certain.[^s2][^s1]

## Connections

Perspective-taking **develops empathy**, particularly its cognitive side, and helps build the bonds its researchers see as its main benefit.[^s1][^s5] It **develops wise reasoning**, in which considering and integrating other viewpoints is a core component, and relates to **Solomon's paradox** and **self-distancing**, both about seeing a problem from beyond one's own vantage point.[^s4][^s7] It can **address egocentric bias**, though without guaranteeing accuracy.[^s1] It is **applied in negotiation**, as **demonstrated by** Galinsky and colleagues' studies, and in **interpersonal conflict**.[^s3][^s5] It is **challenged by** the perspective-mistaking experiments and by the meta-analysis of its effect on empathic concern.[^s1][^s2] An early philosophical account of it was **introduced in** Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments*.[^s8] Its natural partners are **seeking counsel** and **intellectual humility**: asking, and admitting that your picture of another mind is a draft.[^s1][^s6]
