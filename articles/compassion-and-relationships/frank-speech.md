steps:
  - Before you say anything, check your motive; speak only if the point is for your friend's good, not to vent, score a point or look wiser than them.
  - Ask whether the matter is important enough to raise; let small quirks go, so that your frankness keeps its weight when it counts.
  - Ask yourself, "If I were them, would I want to know?" and do not assume they would rather not hear it.
  - Choose a moment when they can listen, ideally one-to-one; not as a joke, not over drinks and not when they are already overwhelmed.
  - Open with praise that is true and specific, so they know you are on their side.
  - Name one concern plainly, without mockery or exaggeration, and say why it matters to you and to them.
  - End warmly, leave the decision with them, and do not let the painful point be the last thing said.
  - Invite the same frankness about yourself, and when it comes, hear it without anger.

# Frank speech

Frank speech, *parrhesia* in Greek, is the practice of telling someone you care about an unwelcome truth about how they are living, for their sake rather than yours.[^s1][^s2] In the Epicurean schools it was a recognised discipline of mutual correction among friends, and Plutarch made it the mark that separates a true friend from a flatterer.[^s1][^s2] It matters for wisdom because people are poor judges of their own faults, and a friend who will speak plainly is one of the few outside views most of us ever get.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

The fullest ancient treatment is a treatise by the Epicurean Philodemus of Gadara, preserved in charred papyrus at Herculaneum (PHerc. 1471) and based, its title says, on lectures by Zeno of Sidon, head of the Athenian Garden.[^s1] The Stanford Encyclopedia calls it key to understanding how an Epicurean community was managed: it treats frank criticism as a skill, practised by the sage and by all members, of diagnosing a companion's errors and choosing the right therapy.[^s1] Different people, the treatise holds, need different handling; the sage must judge when to be mild and when severe, and members should learn to criticise themselves and each other.[^s1] Scholars of early Christianity have compared this teaching with the Pastoral Epistles of Paul.[^s1]

Plutarch took up the same theme in *How to Tell a Flatterer from a Friend*. A flatterer can imitate frankness, so the last part of the essay explains how real frankness is given.[^s2][^s3] His distinction is sharp: "frankness is friendly and noble, but fault-finding is selfish and mean."[^s2]

## Why it matters

Plutarch's essay rests on the worry that the people around us may have reasons to please us rather than help us.[^s2] The Epicureans saw frankness as one of the main ways their communities helped members cure their faults, and tied it closely to friendship.[^s1] Modern research suggests the main barrier is often our own forecast rather than other people's fragility. In Emma Levine and Taya Cohen's experiments, people expected honest conversations, including giving critical feedback to a close partner, to be less pleasant and more damaging to the relationship than they turned out to be.[^s4] In a separate set of five experiments with 1,984 participants, people in a position to give constructive feedback consistently underestimated how much the other person wanted it, and the gap was widest when the feedback mattered most.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Plutarch asks the speaker to strip frankness of self-regard and to purge it of arrogance, ridicule and scoffing.[^s2] He stresses the occasion: frank words dropped in jest or over drinks do harm, and frankness always needs care to hit the right moment.[^s2][^s3] He advises opening with praise that is freely given, approaching the person who has erred with good humour and kindness, and not letting something painful be the last topic of conversation.[^s3] He also suggests watching friends when they act well, not only when they go wrong, so that praise and criticism both come from attention rather than a hunt for faults.[^s3] The Epicurean treatise adds two habits: fit the approach to the person, and be as ready to receive frank criticism as to give it.[^s1] The modern studies suggest a simple check before staying silent: ask whether you would want to know if you were them. In the feedback experiments, that perspective-taking prompt made people more likely to speak up.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Counterfeit frankness.** Plutarch's example is a man who used friendship as an excuse for railing at people; harshness for its own sake is not frankness.[^s2]
- **Bad timing.** Criticism dropped as a joke, over drinks or when the person cannot take it in tends to wound without helping.[^s2][^s3]
- **Ending on the sting.** Leaving the friend with nothing but the painful point turns correction into injury.[^s3]
- **Saying nothing.** Assuming the other person would rather not know is common, and usually wrong, especially when the stakes are high.[^s5]
- **Bristling when it is your turn.** The Epicurean sources treat anger as an obstacle: an irritable listener resents frankness, and a harsh critic can look merely irritable.[^s1]

At work, the same choice arises whenever a peer or mentor must decide whether to give unwelcome feedback, which is the situation the modern experiments study.[^s4][^s5] Where a friend's difficulties involve serious distress, frankness is no substitute for encouraging them toward professional or crisis support.

## Evidence and limits

Frank speech is an ethical teaching judged by argument and long practice, so its status here is normative.[^s1][^s2] The empirical work tests narrower claims. Levine and Cohen's three-day field experiment found that people who only imagined being completely honest predicted far worse experiences than participants reported, and actual relational harm two weeks later did not differ between the honesty, kindness and control groups.[^s4] Their samples were mostly young adults, outcomes were self-reported, and follow-ups were short.[^s4] The feedback studies measured how much recipients wanted feedback, not whether it changed their behaviour or friendships over time.[^s5] Neither line of research tests the ancient advice on tone and timing directly.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Frank speech is a narrower practice within **honesty**, which it **complements** by adding goodwill, timing and tact to truthfulness.[^s2] It is **rooted in** **Epicureanism**, where Philodemus' *On Frank Criticism*, drawn from Zeno of Sidon's lectures, set it out for the Garden's communities, and it is **applied in** **friendship** and in teacher–student **mentorship** within those communities.[^s1] Plutarch presents it as the remedy that **addresses** **flattery**, the friend's counterfeit, and his guidance on praise, kindness and timing makes it **applied in** **giving advice**.[^s2][^s3] It is **supported by** Levine and Cohen's **study of mispredicted honest communication**, and by the **study of underestimated desire for feedback** by Abi-Esber and colleagues.[^s4][^s5] It sits close to **right speech**, **seeking counsel** and **workplace feedback**, and the feedback studies used **perspective-taking** as their prompt to speak up.[^s5]
