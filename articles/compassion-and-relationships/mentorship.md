# Mentorship

Mentorship is a lasting relationship in which someone with more experience helps a less experienced person develop — in a craft, a career, a field of study or a way of handling life. Organisational researchers describe two broad kinds of help a mentor gives: career functions such as sponsorship, coaching, protection, challenging assignments and exposure, and psychosocial functions such as acceptance and confirmation, counselling, friendship and role modelling.[^s2] For a graph about wisdom, mentorship matters twice over: it is a situation that asks for wisdom from both people, and it is one of the main routes by which practical judgment, which cannot be learned from rules alone, is handed on.[^s5][^s7]

## Origin

The word comes from Homer. In Book II of the *Odyssey*, Mentor is the friend of Odysseus who was left in charge of his household, with authority over the servants, while Odysseus was away at Troy.[^s6] The goddess Athena then takes Mentor's likeness and voice to encourage the young Telemachus, warn him off the suitors and help him find a ship and crew to go in search of news of his father.[^s6] The guide in the story is thus both a trusted elder and a borrowed face for divine counsel, which already hints at how much a protégé can project onto a mentor. The philosophical case for learning wisdom from the experienced is older still in spirit: Aristotle argues that young people can master mathematics but are not thought practically wise, because practical wisdom concerns particulars and only time gives experience (*Nicomachean Ethics* VI.8).[^s5] He therefore advises attending to the unproven sayings of experienced and older people, because experience has given them an eye for seeing such matters rightly (VI.11).[^s5]

## Why it matters

Mentoring is associated with a range of modest benefits. A meta-analysis of more than a hundred studies of youth, academic and workplace mentoring found that mentored people did somewhat better than non-mentored people on behavioural, attitudinal, relational, motivational and career outcomes, with academic mentoring most strongly tied to performance.[^s1] Its authors stress that every effect was small and that, because most studies were correlational, it is unknown how much mentoring causes these differences.[^s1] Mentoring can also benefit the mentor. Researchers behind Experience Corps, a programme in which older adults tutor and mentor young schoolchildren for at least 15 hours a week, propose that such generative roles may draw on and strengthen older people's wisdom by giving them new experiences, practice in skills such as conflict resolution, and a way to pass knowledge on — a hypothesis they present as untested.[^s7]

## What wisdom looks like here

- **Choose, and check the fit.** Poor fit between mentor and protégé is one of the two main patterns behind negative mentoring, and researchers suggest that even formal programmes let trained mentors and protégés have a say in choosing each other.[^s2][^s3] Agreeing early what the relationship is for gives both people a standard for judging whether it is working.
- **Diagnose before prescribing.** Aristotle's point that practical wisdom is about particulars cuts both ways: a mentor's experience is valuable only when it is fitted to the protégé's actual situation, not replayed as a template.[^s5]
- **Offer both kinds of help.** The richest relationships combine career help, such as stretch assignments and sponsorship, with psychosocial help, such as acceptance and honest counsel; informal protégés in a large survey reported more of both.[^s2]
- **Model, don't only tell.** Role modelling is one of the core psychosocial functions, so how a mentor handles setbacks, credit and disagreement teaches as much as what they say.[^s2]
- **Encourage a wider circle.** A single mentor is neither available to everyone nor permanent; peer relationships offer much of the same support, with more mutuality, at every career stage.[^s4]
- **Expect it to change and end.** Mentoring relationships typically last only a few years and often end with feelings of loss or anxiety; seeing that as a normal stage, rather than a failure, helps both people move on well.[^s4]
- **Know the limits of the role.** If a protégé is struggling with persistent distress, the wise mentor listens and encourages them to seek qualified professional support rather than acting as a counsellor.

## Pitfalls

Mentoring can go wrong. In a study of 242 protégés, negative experiences clustered into two patterns — mentors who were distant or manipulative, and pairs that were simply a poor fit — and these experiences were linked to lower job satisfaction, more stress and stronger intentions to quit.[^s3] Assigned relationships can disappoint: in a survey of more than a thousand professionals, people with informal mentors reported more support and higher pay than those in formal programmes, and formally mentored people did not differ in pay or promotions from people with no mentor at all.[^s2] The quieter vices are the mentor who wants a disciple rather than an independent colleague, and the protégé who seeks only reassurance. Homer's story is a reminder that the person we look up to is partly an image we bring to them.[^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence base is large but mostly correlational. The multidisciplinary meta-analysis found consistently small effects, some of them fragile, and called for more experimental and longitudinal research.[^s1] The comparison of formal and informal mentoring relied on retrospective self-reports, and the authors note that stronger performers may attract informal mentors, so selection could explain part of the gap.[^s2] Claims that mentoring cultivates wisdom itself, in mentor or protégé, remain largely theoretical.[^s7] Aristotle's argument for learning from the experienced is a normative teaching, judged by its reasoning rather than by experiment.[^s5]

## Connections

Mentorship sits under **social relationships** and **learning from experience**, since it is one way to borrow experience before you have it yourself.[^s5] It **complements seeking counsel**: Aristotle's advice to heed experienced people is the principle behind both choosing advisers and choosing a mentor.[^s5] Because role modelling is a core mentor function, it also **complements emulating moral exemplars**.[^s2] Its outcomes are **demonstrated by the "Does mentoring matter?" meta-analysis**,[^s1] its forms by the **formal-and-informal mentoring study**,[^s2] and its failures by the **negative-mentoring-experiences study**.[^s3] The idea of a mentor was **introduced in the *Odyssey***,[^s6] and its value for older mentors may be **explained by generativity**, the drive to pass something on to the next generation.[^s7] Related situations include **giving advice**, **leading others**, **teaching for wisdom**, **wisdom in the professions**, **workplace feedback** and **ageing**, and the virtue at stake throughout is **practical wisdom**.
