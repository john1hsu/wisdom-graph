# Compassion

Compassion is the feeling that arises when you witness someone else's suffering and that moves you to want to help.[^s1][^s6] The word's Latin roots mean "to suffer with", but researchers separate it from its neighbours: empathy is feeling or understanding what another feels, compassion adds the wish to relieve their suffering, and altruism is the helpful action that may or may not follow.[^s6] As a *virtue*, it is a steady readiness to notice suffering, stay with it and respond well. A review of definitions by Clara Strauss and colleagues proposed five elements: recognising suffering, understanding that suffering is part of every human life, feeling for the person, tolerating the uncomfortable feelings this stirs, and being motivated to act to relieve it.[^s2]

## Origin

Several of the world's wisdom traditions put compassion near the centre of the good life. In early Buddhist texts it is one of four "sublime attitudes" (*brahmavihāra*), alongside goodwill, empathetic joy and equanimity; a practitioner pervades every direction with an awareness imbued with compassion.[^s10] Later Mahāyāna writers made it the engine of the path. The eighth-century monk Śāntideva argued that pain is to be removed simply because it is pain: if another's suffering is like my own, it is hard to say what makes me so special that only mine counts (*Bodhicaryāvatāra* VIII.94–96).[^s9] His further claim, that suffering belongs to no one because there is no real self, still divides scholars.[^s9]

In Confucian thought, Mencius offered a famous thought experiment (*Mengzi* 2A.6): anyone who suddenly sees a small child about to fall into a well feels alarm and concern, not to win favour with the parents or praise from neighbours.[^s7] Commiseration of this kind is, he said, the beginning of humaneness (*ren*), one of four innate moral "sprouts" (Eno translates "seeds") that must be cultivated to grow.[^s7][^s8] In *Mengzi* 1A.7 he tells a king who spared a frightened ox that his task is to extend that same care to his people.[^s7][^s8]

Modern emotion science has studied compassion as a distinct emotion. Jennifer Goetz, Dacher Keltner and Emiliana Simon-Thomas argue it may have evolved from caring for vulnerable offspring, from choosing kind partners and from cooperation with non-kin, while noting that these evolutionary arguments are largely indirect.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Compassion turns noticing into helping. In the research Goetz and colleagues reviewed, felt compassion predicted helping, whereas self-focused distress predicted less helping when escape was easy.[^s1] Preliminary work links it to better health, closer relationships and, at work, to less burnout and more positive views of colleagues.[^s6] For wisdom it is not a soft extra. Mencius set the sprout of commiseration beside the sprout of right and wrong, to be cultivated together,[^s8] and Śāntideva allowed that a compassionate person may set ordinary rules aside when doing so truly benefits others (*Bodhicaryāvatāra* V.84), which makes judgement part of compassion itself.[^s9]

## What good looks like

Wise compassion is warm but steady. Goetz and colleagues distinguish it from empathic distress, which aims to reduce *your own* discomfort, from sadness about your own loss, and from pity, which adds a sense that the sufferer is beneath you.[^s1] Strauss's fourth element, tolerating the hard feelings suffering arouses, is what lets you stay instead of fleeing or freezing.[^s2] It is also fair-minded. People feel less compassion when they judge sufferers responsible for their plight, and more for those close, similar or important to them, so a wise person questions those appraisals rather than simply following them.[^s1]

## How to practise it

- **Notice first.** Look for signs of difficulty in the people around you before deciding what you think of them.[^s2]
- **Remember it is shared.** Remind yourself that suffering is part of every life, including yours; this guards against pity.[^s1][^s2]
- **Stay with the discomfort.** Let the feeling be there without acting just to make it stop.[^s2]
- **Ask, then act.** Find out what would actually help and do something concrete, however small.[^s2][^s6]
- **Widen the circle on purpose.** Practise wishing relief to someone close, to yourself, to a stranger and then to someone difficult, as compassion meditations do.[^s6]
- **Make the many concrete.** When suffering is large-scale, think of those affected as one community rather than a count, since numbers alone dull feeling.[^s5]

This is everyday guidance, not therapy. Self-focused distress at others' pain tends to reduce helping rather than fuel it,[^s1] and anyone who feels persistently drained or overwhelmed by others' suffering, carers above all, should seek professional support.

## Evidence and limits

Compassion seems trainable. In Helen Weng and colleagues' study, two weeks of daily 30-minute compassion practice led people to spend more of their own money helping an unfairly treated stranger than an active reappraisal-training group, with matching changes in brain responses to suffering; but the analysed sample was 41 people and altruism was not measured before training.[^s4] A meta-analysis of 21 randomised trials (1,285 participants) by James Kirby and colleagues found moderate gains in compassion (d = 0.55, only four trials), self-compassion, well-being and reduced distress, holding against active controls, though most trials were small.[^s3]

Measurement is a real weakness: Strauss's review found that none of nine existing scales measured compassion comprehensively, and that compassion for oneself and for others correlate only weakly.[^s2] Compassion also has limits of scope. Daniel Västfjäll, Paul Slovic and colleagues found that feelings and donations can begin to fade with the second endangered life, though describing a group as a single unit reversed the fade.[^s5] Hence **mixed**: a real, trainable capacity whose measurement and reach are still uncertain.[^s2][^s3][^s5]

## Connections

Compassion is **rooted in Buddhism**, as one of the sublime attitudes,[^s10] and **in Confucianism**, as the sprout of *ren*.[^s8] It **contrasts with empathic distress** and is often **confused with pity**.[^s1] Its trainability is **demonstrated by** Weng's **compassion-training study**[^s4] and **supported by** the Kirby **meta-analysis**,[^s3] while **compassion fade** **challenges** how far it reaches.[^s5] It is **applied in leading others**, where compassionate workplaces show less burnout.[^s6] It sits under the **moral virtues** and the **four immeasurables**, and is related to **empathy**, **equanimity**, **self-compassion**, **forgiveness**, **compassionate reappraisal**, **loving-kindness meditation**, **ubuntu** and **right speech**.[^s1][^s2][^s10]
